 1 Introduction
 Although the interests of vital communication and it is dependant services necessitated
 an accelerated set of features for privacy and confidentiality in the fabric of modern networks, with encrypted communication protocols leaving state-of-the-art in their dust,
 the very design parameters of the interest, the transmission of information and therefore observability information, which the dependent infrastructure on such technology
 has had the unintended side effect of severely degrading traditional insight and anomaly
 detection mechanisms used for security. Now that traffic is increasingly encapsulated,
 traditional payload-inspection-based IDSs are becoming less effective, leaving many
 blind spots in network defence. With the trend toward adopting Zero Trust Network
 (ZTN) architectures, which do not automatically trust any user or device (inside or
 outside the organisation) and always verify, there is increasing demand for fast anomaly-detection algorithms for encrypted traffic that can circumvent the limitations of
 decryption overhead. While initial traffic classification and anomaly detection have utilised conventional machine learning techniques, practical use has been constrained by
 the inflexibility of such methods in the face of evolving threats and their opacity [1, 2].
 To overcome these challenges, recent work has begun to explore explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methods that balance detection accuracy and interpretability for
 security analysts. Works such as Zebin et al. [12] and Singh et al. [49]. XAI methods
 for anomaly detection based on encrypted service calls are up-and-coming [64]. Islam
 et al. [4] focus solely on trust and provide examples of availability, integrity, and confidentiality; their work reminds us of the broader context of trust that must be built in
 secure systems, including interpretability. However, most methods developed to date
 either focus on protocol, e.g., DNS-over-HTTPS, or on static ML models that do not
 adapt over time. Furthermore, hardening against adversarial attacks or new-to-organisation encryption protocols — essential elements in any meaningful Zero Trust context —
 has received scant attention. The framework is proposed to fill such a gap by providing
 all three dimensions, i.e., high detection accuracy, interpretability, and robustness, for
 encrypted traffic anomaly detection.
 To mitigate this gap, we propose a completely novel explainable anomaly detection framework, called ZTXPlainaAI, based on Zero Trust Networks. At its heart is an
 explainable hybrid deep learning model: Encode: EncXplainNet, that utilises a CNNs–
 GRUs architecture for modelling local features and temporal dependencies, and an
 attention mechanism for interpretable decision making. Finally, SHAP-based feature
 attribution provides post hoc explanations with both intrinsic and global interpretations.
 Together, these design choices enable the model to achieve state-of-the-art performance
 in anomaly detection while also providing explainability that helps security analysts easily apply Zero Trust principles.
 Task: To devise an explainable AI model which can classify if the traffic is encrypted
 or not, detect anomalies in it on the encrypted traffic based on industrial-oriented, nonwhimsical constraints (i.e. without inspecting payload) and on real-world observations
 that IoT-MQTT communication generates encrypted traffic. The framework targets
 the main objectives: (i) high classification accuracy and robustness against a variety of
 encrypted protocols, (ii) human-understandable and interpretable rationale on the prediction of an anomaly dedicated to human analysts, and (iii) adaptability to changing
 network conditions using reinforcement and feedback loop mechanisms.

 There are multiple novelties of this work. The hybrid architecture, constructed by
 incorporating CNNs, GRUs, and attention mechanisms in EncXplainNet, uniquely

 2 Related work
 The growing dependence on encryption and the integration of Zero Trust Network
 (ZTN) architectures have led some researchers to develop not just accurate but also
 interpretable and flexible anomaly detection frameworks. The classic approach of
 inspecting payloads is infeasible in encrypted environments, and this has driven the
 quest for artificial intelligence (AI) and explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methods. In this section, we examine previous work across four interrelated topics: anomaly
 detection in encrypted traffic, the role of deep learning in Zero Trust, XAI for intrusion
 detection, and identify research gaps.

 2.1 Encrypted traffic anomaly detection
 By now, enabling end-to-end encryption has made it challenging to detect anomalies
 in the increasing volume of encrypted traffic. In this context, a hybrid CNN–GRU (i.e.,
 convolutional neural networks with gated recurrent units) method was introduced by
 Bakhshi and Ghita [17] for time-series traffic classification. Similarly, Ibraheem et al. For
 example [18], discussed methods for feature selection to detect anomalies in HTTPS and
 noted that classifiers achieve better performance with appropriate feature engineering.
 Long and Zhang [19] developed a parallel, autoencoder-based deep learning framework
 for encrypted traffic detection, using L1 regularisation to improve anomaly detection
 accuracy. The absence of operational benchmarks and the scarcity of datasets are two
 significant limitations of AI-based detection of encrypted traffic, as shown in a systematic literature review by Ji et al. [20].
 Other research developed deep learning methods specific to encrypted flows. For PF
 Sense log analysis, CNNs and LSTMs were used Fotiadou et al. [21], and feature-mining
 methods for encrypted malicious traffic were proposed by Wang and Thing [22]. Ikram
 et al. [23] and Yoshimura et al. [24] focused on ensemble learning and stacked feature
 extraction to improve robustness for high-dimensional data. Papadogiannaki and Ioannidis [25] provided a comprehensive survey of encrypted traffic analysis and defences,
 examining the trade-off between traffic privacy and security.
 Other work [26, 27, 28, 29, 30] on innovations, such as adversarially regularised autoencoders, machine learning in the cloud, and online adaptive deep learning pipelines for
 dynamic anomaly detection, also directly relates to latent representation learning. Some
 of the new models emphasised flexibility and robustness Gonaygunta et al. [31]. Flexible
 DL anomaly detection [31], scalable frameworks [32], and systematic reviews relating
 detection in the encrypted space with Zero Trust and emerging networks [33, 34, 35,

 2.4 Research gaps
 Despite great achievements, significant issues remain unresolved. Compounding the two
 weaknesses, multiple studies attempt to increase detection’s accuracy without considering its robustness toward emerging encrypted protocols and adversarial perturbations
 [18, 22, 28]. In addition to differences, SHAP and LIME, though popular for model interpretation, are open questions in Zero Trust deployments with high speed [3, 50, 57].
 Furthermore, while much of the literature on Zero Trust has been reviewed [13, 34, 38,
 42, 47], most of these works remain at a conceptual level, lacking concrete deployment
 plans. Finally, little work connects explainable anomaly detection with adaptive feedback
 loops for encrypted traffic, which encourages the creation of ZTXPlainaAI.

 3.2 Background on deep learning for encrypted traffic
 Encrypted traffic on networks poses a significant challenge for existing intrusion detection systems, as it renders payload inspection impossible due to encryption protocols
 such as TLS, SSH, and VPN. As a result, the focus of research has shifted towards
 machine learning and deep learning techniques that operate on flow-level metadata
 rather than raw packet content. As noted above, metadata features – such as byte counts,
 inter-arrival times, and connection durations – remain visible in encrypted streams and
 can be used for behavioural pattern recognition.
 Deep learning approaches, specifically CNNs and RNNs, demonstrated high-performance modeling for structured flow-based data. While convolutional neural networks

 3.4 Zero trust network principles
 The Zero Trust Network (ZTN) is a new security paradigm that replaces perimeterbased security models with a new status quo of “never trust, always verify.” A Zero-Trust
 environment does not trust any entity, inside or outside the organisation, by default.
 Which means that all access requests, every communication between devices, and all
 traffic flows need to be dynamically and continuously authenticated, authorised, and
 validated using adaptable risk assessment. This methodology is critical at the enterprise
 level, where users and devices are constantly crossing network boundaries and accessing
 cloud and on-premises resources.
 The core concept behind ZTN is to use least privileged access. This principle states
 that users and devices must not have a higher level of access than needed to complete
 their task. If traffic is encrypted, it means inspecting all traffic headed to or from the
 organisation. As encryption prevents access to packet payloads, this sort of anomaly
 detection must rely on metadata and behavioural signals to infer potential malpractice.

 4.4 Explainability integration
 The role of explainability in ZTXPlainaAI is pivotal, as evidenced by the case where
 not only is the anomaly detection decision in the EncXplainNet model taken correctly,
 but it is also interpretable and auditable. Justifications for detection output: Since deep
Kavitha et al. Discover Computing (2026) 29:241 Page 16 of 43

 learning models are said to be too opaque to understand, and because decisions made
 within Zero Trust Network environments can be critical, it is essential to know why a
 particular output was detected. This framework links explainability in two ways: internal
 interpretability (attention-based) and post hoc attribution (using SHAP).
 EncXplainNet model has an attention mechanism within itself to compute the importance weights at dynamically to each hidden state ht generated at sequential feature
 processing to identify the attributions of the encoder inputs. The mechanism enables the
 model to pay closer attention to certain time windows or characteristics of the encrypted
 flow being more relevant for abnormal behavior. They then compute a context vector c
 using the attention weights Eq. (3), combining the most relevant information for final
 decision-making. By plotting the values of at we can see which time steps or flow characteristics influenced the output the most, providing an underlying level of interpretability straight out of the box.
 In addition to this intrinsic mechanism, the framework incorporates SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) as a model-agnostic post hoc interpretability method. For a
 single prediction, SHAP calculates a set of values ∅ j (xi ) , for each input feature vector
 xi , characterizing the contribution of feature j to prediction f (xi ). This means that the
 additive property presented in Eq. (4) is satisfied and thus, the final model prediction
 can be explained as the sum of the individual feature contributions and a base value ∅ 0 .
 This makes it clear and mathematically based reasoning for each prediction, allowing the
 analyst to see whether flow duration, packet inter-arrival time or byte count were the
 causative features which flagged a traffic flow as anomalous.
 In this case, the SHAP explanations for the predicted anomaly across all samples
 and the classification output are logged together. The explanations are then visualised
 through interactive plots (i.e., bar charts, waterfall plots, ranked lists of features) for further investigation by the security analyst. In essence, this augments confidence in the
 system whilst also helping refine Zero Trust access policies based on the user behaviour
 attributes the model classifies as malicious or benign.
 Together, this attention layer and SHAP operate as a dual-explanation pipeline, with
 one providing real-time, internal interpretability information about the model and the
 other generating externally verifiable explanations in the form of SHAP features for
 security auditing and analyst-driven investigation. This integration fortifies the ZTXPlainaAI framework, making it robust, lucid, and deployable in situations where accuracy
 and responsible action are paramount.

 4.7 Threat model and assumptions
 ZTXPlainaAI has a threat model based on a Zero Trust Network architecture, which
 does not trust any internal or external network/traffic. The system assumes that attackers have already breached the network perimeter, which is protected by stronger encryption channels against suspicious and malicious activity. Thus, the framework here is
 specifically oriented towards advanced threats, where target traffic flows are encrypted
 and so would circumvent standard signature or payload inspection approaches.
 The primary threat this model targets is a network-level attacker utilising encrypted
 communication protocols, such as HTTPS, SSH, or VPN, for reconnaissance, lateral
 movement, C2, data exfiltration, or DDoS attacks. These threats are typically hidden
 in flows whose packets appear normal but can behave abnormally due to timing, volume, or statistical properties. We do not decrypt the payload either – such a capability is
Kavitha et al. Discover Computing (2026) 29:241 Page 20 of 43

 neither practical nor desirable for privacy and computational reasons – thus, the rest of
 the model operates only on the available flow-level metadata.
 Although adversaries may attempt to shape encrypted flows to resemble benign traffic, we do not assume that flow-level statistics are immutable. In particular, sophisticated
 attackers can alter timing, burstiness, packet sizes, and byte fractions while preserving
 protocol semantics using traffic morphing, adaptive padding/fragmentation, or rate control. Accordingly, our threat model considers an adaptive evasion adversary that can partially manipulate observable flow metadata under operational constraints (e.g., latency
 overhead, bandwidth cost, and throughput degradation). Under this setting, ZTXPlainaAI is intended to detect residual behavioral inconsistencies and temporal irregularities that arise when attackers emulate benign baselines at scale. Evaluating robustness
 against fully optimized traffic-morphing strategies is a non-trivial extension and is identified as a limitation and future work in Sect. 6.
 The defender has access to encrypted traffic flow metadata, system and policy logs,
 historical labeled datasets, and analyst feedback. An assumption is also made that the
 ecosystem allows protection mechanisms, viz. detection models and policies, to be
 updated regularly and incrementally, using real-time threat intelligence. Even though the
 framework runs initially in a supervised manner with data that has been prepared and
 labelled, it has a feedback loop (as indicated in Sect. 4.5) that can be used to adjust and
 improvements over time.
 The system does not assume knowledge of specific attack types in advance; instead,
 it is designed to generalize patterns of abnormality across diverse encrypted traffic scenarios. Additionally, the model assumes that encrypted flows are not tampered with in
 transit (i.e., the encryption is preserved), and that flow records are accurately collected
 without packet loss or sampling errors that could distort feature distributions.
 In closing, the threat-model is overly pessimistic and Zero Trust, but it is a reminder
 that encrypted flows from both inside and outside of your network must be ceaselessly
 monitored, sampled and accounted for. These are enterprise-scale network deployments, leading to realistic assumptions guiding the design of the EncXplainNet detection engine and its supporting framework ZTXPlainaAI.

 5.1 Dataset description and preparation
 Experiments were conducted using the CIC-IDS2019 dataset [81], provided by the
 Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity, which comprises enterprise-level network traffic
 spanning multiple days and includes both benign user activities and a range of attack
 scenarios. In this work, we consider only the subset of encrypted traffic, including flows
 from protocols such as HTTPS and SSH, for anomaly detection under Zero Trust.
Kavitha et al. Discover Computing (2026) 29:241 Page 21 of 43

 Raw packet captures were transformed into bidirectional flow records using CICFlow-
 Meter, which correlates packets based on the standard five-tuple (source IP, destination
 IP, source port, destination port, and protocol). Each flow was characterised as a feature
 vector of session-level attributes, including flow duration, total numbers of forward and
 backward bytes, packet counts (total and in each direction), statistics on packet interarrival times, and the presence or absence of local TCP flags. Payload-related characteristics have been intentionally omitted for the sake of encryption privacy, resulting in
 feature vectors xi ∈ Rd , where ddenotes the number of extracted flow-level attributes.
 This yielded a total of 2,000 flows in the encrypted traffic subset, of which 1,400 (70%)
 were benign and 600 (30%) were anomalous after pre-processing. While this distribution
 is much more moderate than the severe imbalance typically seen in production environments, it still introduces an inherent skew toward benign traffic in anomaly detection tasks. If not properly addressed, this distribution can lead to biased learning for
 the majority class. To reduce this effect during training, class weights were calculated
 inversely proportional to class frequencies, with misclassified anomalous flow receiving
 a relatively higher penalty. This acts the same way, improving the rate of picking minority attack instances during the learning process while preserving learning equilibrium
 among classes.
 To minimise bias arising from differing feature scales and enhance convergence stability, all numerical features were normalised using min–max scaling Eq. (5), mapping
 values to the range [0, 1]. Some attributes, such as protocol identifiers, were converted
 to numerical values because they were categorical and deep learning models accept only
 numerical inputs.
 Lastly, the dataset was split into three folds using stratified sampling, keeping the class
 distribution the same as the original dataset: training (70% flows), validation, and test
 (each with 15% flows). This approach allowed for a fair evaluation without compromising the class proportions. In Zero Trust environments with extreme class imbalance,
 transparent reporting of performance metrics is only possible by explicitly reporting the
 class distribution and class weights during preparation.
 Even though the dataset used in this study does not exhibit the significant imbalance
 typically found in a large-scale operational network, anomaly detection is still naturally
 biased toward normal traffic. Typically, benign flows outnumber malignant ones in practical deployments; therefore, class-sensitive evaluation metrics such as precision, recall,
 F1-score, and PR-AUC are the natural choice. To learn effectively under this mild class
 imbalance, we used class-weighted loss functions during training, so that misclassifying
 an anomalous flow incurs a higher penalty.

 5.4 Performance evaluation metrics
 Various classification metrics were used to evaluate the performance of the proposed
 EncXplainNet for anomaly detection in encrypted traffic flows. To begin with, we calculated Accuracy to measure the overall ratio of correctly classified flows (benign and
 anomalous) Nevertheless, in intrusion detection tasks accuracy alone may give a misguided understanding due to the nature of network level datasets that are characterized
 by class imbalance, with the presence of a class of flows that are beneficial, compared to
 a second class that are abnormal.
 To compensate for these limitations, additional metrics were incorporated, including
 precision, recall, and F1-score. Precision reflects the model’s ability to minimise false
 alarms, since it measures the fraction of all flows predicted as anomalous that are actually anomalous. Recall is the percentage of total anomalous flows correctly detected,
 indicating how sensitive the system is to potential threats. The F1-score is the harmonic
 mean of precision (the ratio of relevant instances among retrieved instances) and recall
 (the ratio of relevant instances among actually relevant instances), thus providing a symmetric measure.
 The class imbalance in encrypted traffic anomaly detection warrants further utilisation
 of Precision–Recall (PR) curves and the PR-AUC metric. In particular, PR-based evaluation is informative for minority-class detection, as it emphasises the precision-recall
 trade-off across different decision thresholds. To enable clearer interpretation of anomaly-detection performance under imbalanced conditions, we plotted PR curves for both
 EncXplainNet and all baseline models in this study.
 Second, the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) was
 employed to assess the model’s discriminatory power at a range of decision thresholds. Using AUC as a threshold-independent metric, we can ensure a fair comparison
 between EncXplainNet and the baseline approaches. In addition, FPR (False Positive
 Rate) and FNR (False Negative Rate) were calculated to evaluate the practical implications of detection errors. The FPR is the ratio of innocent flows wrongly labelled as
 anomalous, which is important in Zero Trust environments because too many false
 alarms can disrupt regular network operations. On the other hand, FNR refers to the
 percentage of abnormal flows incorrectly categorised as benign, and it is an indicator of
 missed threats.

 5.7 Explainability results
 Attention weights and SHAP attributions complement each other but play distinct roles

 5.8 Statistical significance testing
 Table 3 shows that EncXplainNet outperforms all baseline models in terms of accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC; however, it is essential to confirm that these
 improvements are statistically significant. This is particularly relevant to encrypted-traffic anomaly detection, where class imbalance can exaggerate performance differences.
 Accordingly, statistical hypothesis testing was performed to assess the significance of the
 observed gains.
 For this purpose, all models were tested in a set of independent runs with different random seeds but the same data partitioning and hyperparameter settings – see
 Sect. 5.2. For each run, we tabulated F1-score and AUC, which are the most suitable
 metrics for imbalanced security datasets and directly map to the comparable numbers
 displayed in Table 3. EncXplainNet consistently yields higher mean F1-scores (0.96) and
 AUC (0.98), whereas the other three top-performing baseline models, e.g., LSTM and

 t-test of the mean F1-score and AUC values under matched experimental conditions, at
 p < 0.05 (95%). Furthermore, we validated the results by cross-validating the Wilcoxon
 signed-rank test, given that metric distributions may not always be normal. The null
 hypothesis that the performance of EncXplainNet and baseline models is the same is
 rejected in all tasks.
 The statistical analysis verifies that the performance improvements in Table 3 are not

 6 Discussion
 The experimental evaluation results for EncXplainNet are numerous, demonstrating the
 capabilities of the zero-trust-based architecture in detecting encrypted-traffic anomalies. The model consistently ranked first across all primary metrics: 0.96 accuracy, 0.96
 F1-score, and 0.98 AUC, outperforming traditional ML classifiers and baseline DL models. The discrimination between the two analyses confirmed the established embedding
 of CNNs, GRUs, and attention mechanisms as more naturally suited for feature description, time-series learning, and interpretability. Ablation studies also demonstrated that
 all elements were essential for overall performance, with the complete model achieving
 the most balanced results. The robustness analysis further supports EncXplainNetto’s
 ability to generalise to unseen encrypted protocols, noisy settings, and adversarial perturbations, making it a promising candidate for real-world deployment.
 In terms of Zero Trust security, the framework aligns squarely with the Zero Trust
 principle of continuous verification and extremely low levels of implicit trust. EncXplainNet takes a step towards fine-grained anomaly detection while preserving privacy
 by operating on metadata at the flow-level and NOT on decrypted payloads. While
 encrypted traffic analysis avoids direct payload inspection, it does not guarantee complete privacy, as flow-level statistical features may still encode indirect information about
 application behaviour or user activity. Its explainability is dual — the system provides it
 to the operational component as well as to analysts, so the operational component integrates its decisions transparently & action-wise. It provides features for human-in-theloop, such as Chinese Whispers, and action-based explanations via feature extraction,
 providing attention-based model intrinsic interpretability and SHAP feature attribution
 in the same pipeline. This transparency addresses one of the most significant hurdles to
 Zero Trust adoption — the need for understandable decision-making to ensure we do
 not end up unquestioningly trusting black boxes. The vibrant feedback and reinforcement loop enable adaptive learning based on analyst validation, making the system not
 just accurate but also adaptive to additions or changes in the threat landscape.
 That said, the design does come with some compromises. So if it performs wondrously,
 what is the answer? The trade-off: CNNs, GRUs, and attention layers have substantially higher computational costs than simpler machine learning techniques. Additionally, using a GPU to expedite training and inference is valuable for running healthcare
 tools. However, they also tend to be impactful due to the trade-offs in accuracy and
 interpretability they entail, as well as the robustness advantages illustrated in the stress
Kavitha et al. Discover Computing (2026) 29:241 Page 39 of 43

 tests. Furthermore, reliance on labelled data can also be viewed as a disadvantage, as
 it generally requires training the model first, and curation of such data resources in a
 production environment can be an expensive undertaking. However, this is somewhat
 mitigated by the feedback loop, in which, over the years, the human in the loop can validate and improve it. Overall, EncXplainNet achieves a practical compromise between
 detection effectiveness, interpretability, and computational feasibility, making it a strong
 contender for encrypted anomaly detection in Zero Trust networks.

 6.1 Limitations of the study
 Despite EncXplainNet’s good performance, this study has some limitations. First, the
 experiments are primarily based on the CIC-IDS2019 encrypted subset, which cannot capture the variety of real-world scenarios across encrypted protocols and traffic
 behaviours. Second, explainability is implemented via attention and SHAP in our case,
 but user-centric evaluations with security analysts were not conducted to quantify the
 effectiveness of practical interpretability. Third, the computational cost of the trained
 model only introduces the overhead of deeper architectures, which prevents its deployment in resource-constrained contexts, such as edge devices. The shortcomings may be
 overcome in future work, increasing the scalability and generalizability of the proposed
 framework and its practical adoption.
 Robustness evaluation in this study does not include domain-specific traffic morphing or adaptive fragmentation attacks designed to preserve protocol semantics while
 matching benign flow statistics. Extending the robustness analysis to include such adaptive adversaries (and stronger optimisation-based attacks) is part of our planned future
 work. Additionally, robustness evaluation against fully adaptive adversaries and crossdataset protocol generalisation remains outside the scope of the present study. A systematic quantitative assessment of the analyst-guided feedback mechanism, including
 controlled comparisons with non-adaptive and fully automated retraining strategies, is
 an important direction for future work. We do not report hardware-specific deployment
 benchmarks (latency, throughput, and memory footprint). Future work will include systematic benchmarking of inference under realistic flow rates (e.g., thousands of flows per
 second) and across different deployment targets (CPU/GPU/edge).
 Although EncXplainNet operates exclusively on encrypted traffic metadata and does
 not access packet payloads, recent studies have shown that flow-level statistics can still
 be exploited through model inversion or inference attacks to reveal sensitive behavioural
 patterns. The current study does not evaluate resistance to such privacy leakage attacks.
 Addressing model inversion risks through privacy-preserving learning techniques, controlled feature disclosure, or formal privacy guarantees constitutes an essential direction
 for future research in Zero Trust network environments.

 7 Conclusion and future scope
 This work introduced ZTXPlainaAI: an explainable AI-based framework (ZTXPlainaAI)
 for anomaly detection in encrypted traffic in the context of Zero Trust Networks, utilising the EncXplainNet model. By combining CNNs to extract features, GRUs to learn
 temporal sequences, an attention mechanism to render the model interpretable, and
 SHAP to perform feature attribution, the framework achieved the best accuracy (0.96),
 F1-score (0.96), and AUC (0.98). Compared to state-of-the-art approaches, it proved
Kavitha et al. Discover Computing (2026) 29:241 Page 40 of 43

 more effective in terms of the accuracy-survivability trade-off, interpretability, and
 robustness. The importance of individual components was also revealed through ablation studies, while a robust analysis showed that the proposed method was immune to
 unseen protocols, noisy inputs, and adversarial perturbations. Furthermore, the feedback loop mechanism facilitated adaptive refinement, enabling optimised applicability
 in increasingly dynamic Zero Trust scenarios. Even so, there are a few limitations with
 these encouraging findings. This evaluation relied solely on the CIC-IDS2019 dataset,
 which may limit its generalizability to other encrypted protocols, including more diverse
 or emerging ones. Pragmatic usability evaluations of the explainability outputs in terms
 of analytically-centred usability assessments were not performed, which remains a significant achievement to validate real interpretability value in practice. Furthermore, the
 high computational requirements of the hybrid deep architecture might limit its deployment on resource-constrained edge or IoT devices. In future work, we will validate on
 several datasets, including real-world encrypted traffic from various backgrounds. The
 platform will undergo usability studies with cybersecurity analysts to evaluate trust,
 interpretability, and decision support performance. We will optimise lightweight variants of EncXplainNet to facilitate exploration in resource-constrained environments.
 In addition, leveraging state-of-the-art adversarial defence techniques and federated
 learning further enhances adaptability, privacy, and robustness, making ZTXPlainaAIa a
 stand-alone, deployable solution for Zero Trust anomaly detection.
