A B S T R A C T
Nowadays, most network traﬃc is encrypted, which protects user privacy but hides attack traces, further
hindering identifying attacks to inspect traﬃc packages. Machine Learning (ML) methods are widely applied
to attack classiﬁcation on encrypted traﬃc owing to no need for manual analysis. However, existing studies only
concentrate on basic statistical features and cannot obtain the crucial attack behaviors hiding in the encrypted
traﬃc. Worse still, attackers constantly update attack vectors to evade detection, which means outdated features
extracted from historical traﬃc fail to recognize unseen attacks.
As a solution, we propose an attack classiﬁcation approach, attack ﬁngerprint based on graphs of time-window
(TGPrint). We ﬁrst ﬁlter normal traﬃc ﬂows using ML models to eliminate the impact of useless, noisy data
for attack classiﬁcation and maintain suspicious traﬃc. Then, we create attack graphs to depict interaction
behaviors of attack-victim hosts from suspicious traﬃc containing crucial attack behaviors. Besides, we divide a
speciﬁc duration for each attack to precisely elaborate attack graphs, where temporal, statistical, and aggregate
features are extracted to portray attack behaviors. Finally, we utilize Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to mine
and grasp the crucial behavior patterns from attack graphs to generate ﬁngerprints and classify attacks, even
unseen attacks. Extensive experiments are conducted on well-known datasets to verify our approach. It achieves
a precision of 99% in attack classiﬁcation on encrypted traﬃc, an average higher than other ML methods of
50%. Meanwhile, it classiﬁes unseen attacks with an average accuracy of over 80% and has a strong robustness
to false positives.

1. Introduction
Guaranteeing those online activities and the sensitive information
exchanged daily remain private, Transport Layer Security (TLS) protocol was created in 1999 (Dierks, 1999) which provides encryption
for the application layer (Roques et al., 2019). Gartner estimated that
80% of global web traﬃc would be encrypted by 2019 (Cisco, 2019).
However, privacy does not guarantee security. Attackers have started
to leverage TLS to hide their persistent malicious activities. Patternmatching techniques are hard to recognize attacks in encrypted traﬃc,
such as port identiﬁcation and Deep Packet Inspection (DPI). Port-based
approaches encounter failures in identifying attacks due to dynamic
port techniques (Madhukar and Williamson, 2006; Erman et al., 2007).

DPI has been proven ineﬃcient in attack classiﬁcation tasks because
it directly goes against the purpose of encryption protocol, which is to
keep user data conﬁdential. Therefore, how to detect and reveal attack
behaviors based on encrypted traﬃc has become a vital research hot
spot.
Given the above constraints, ML on the encrypted network for attack
classiﬁcation is a natural solution without complicated manual analysis.
The application of ML to recognize attacks in encrypted network trafﬁc classiﬁcation has been well studied in the past decades (Luo et al.,
2020; Zhai et al., 2020; Nychis et al., 2008; Koch et al., 2014; Torroledo
et al., 2018a; Yao et al., 2019). Unfortunately, the promise of ML has
been slow to materialize in the network security domain (Anderson and

Challenge 1: Imprecise features without attack behavior patterns. The sophisticated features decide the model’s detection eﬀectiveness because of garbage in, garbage out. Features in attack classiﬁcation
studies on encrypted traﬃc are mainly classiﬁed into two aspects: 1)
statistical and temporal traﬃc features (e.g., packet size, traﬃc direction, transmission time) (Gu et al., 2008; Arora et al., 2014; Anderson et
al., 2018) and 2)TLS handshake features (e.g., handshake message status, encryption suite). The former focuses on the detailed transmission
information, and the latter grasp TLS session details.
An attack lasts between attack-victim hosts, while attack behaviors
leave traces in the traﬃc. However, these two features, exclusive of the
interaction information, cannot be abstracted to crucial high-level attack behavior patterns. The attack behavior patterns can enhance the
models’ capability of attack classiﬁcation on encrypted traﬃc because
they reﬂect the attacks’ behavior characteristics from interaction activities. The interaction information of hosts’ traﬃc restores the attack
behavior patterns, which are crucial for attack classiﬁcation.

2. Related work
2.1. Attack classiﬁcation
2.1.1. Pattern-matching approaches
Pattern-matching approaches focus on manually set rules to look for
the particular malicious program or the data patterns reﬂected from the
packet traﬃc. Port mapping classiﬁes traﬃc by mapping the port in the
TCP/UDP message header to the speciﬁc attack. However, some applications use dynamic ports (Madhukar and Williamson, 2006), which
may change during data transmission; some applications use ports of
other standard protocols for data transmission in order to avoid system
restrictions (Erman et al., 2007), thus achieving port steganography.
DPI (Park et al., 2013) parses the network packets as a whole during the
traﬃc analysis process and determines whether the traﬃc in the packets
matches the characteristics of a speciﬁc attack. However, DPI only applies to plaintext traﬃc and has a high computational overhead. These
approaches are no longer suited for attack classiﬁcation in encrypted
traﬃc. TLS certiﬁcates sent by web servers in plaintext can also help
detect phishing websites or anomalous connections (Richman, 2022).
Blacklists maintaining certiﬁcates from malicious servers and checking
all incoming certiﬁcates against that list are the easiest way of dealing
with certiﬁcates (Suricata, 2010; Abuse, 2022). However, this method
must be updated regularly by trusted people, and mistakes resulting in
blocked websites can frustrate users. These approaches are no longer
suited for attack classiﬁcation in encrypted traﬃc.

Challenge 2: Unseen attack with new attack vectors. Various attack vectors can implement an attack. For example, bruteforce attack
vectors involve a variety of protocols, such as HTTP and FTP. Diﬀerent
attack vectors execute diﬀerent operations during the attack, and the
malicious traﬃc generated from diverse attack operations is disparate.
ML models learn traﬃc characteristics based on the attack already seen
and make accurate judgments when the same attack reappears. The trafﬁc the new attack vector generates is diﬀerent from the known, making
the model misjudge. Hence, attackers usually change the attack vectors,
which makes the classiﬁcation model confused and unable to detect the
attack activities. Therefore, the ability to identify unseen attacks with
new attack vectors is a critical assessment criterion in addition to the
evaluation, such as accuracy. This paper deﬁnes an unseen attack as a
known attack with a new attack vector.
To address the above challenges, we need to propose an approach.
It can mine the crucial attack behavior patterns in encrypted traﬃc for
accurate attack classiﬁcation. Meanwhile, it identiﬁes unseen attacks
based on these crucial patterns, which reveal fundamental techniques
for the same attack with diﬀerent attack vectors. The emergence of
GNN has demonstrated the capability of learning complex behavior and
extracting high-level features from big data to create the signature or
ﬁngerprint of analyzed data (Pham et al., 2021; Kipf and Welling, 2016;
Veličković et al., 2017). High-level attack behaviors with complex interactions among hosts could be graph-structured for recognizing attacks.
Inspired by this, we propose an attack classiﬁcation approach TGPrint,
which uses attack graphs as a ﬁngerprint for attack classiﬁcation on encrypted traﬃc. TGPrint ﬁrst eliminates noise from normal traﬃc ﬂows
using ML tools and leaves suspicious traﬃc to construct attack graphs.
Then, TGPrint clusters attack-victim hosts’ traﬃc and divides them according to a speciﬁc time window set for each attack to portray attack
behaviors as a graph precisely. Thirdly, TGPrint constructs a set of attack graphs where ports are nodes, attack-victim hosts’ communication
relationships are edges, and temporal, statistical, and aggregate features are edge attributes to generate each attack’s ﬁngerprint. Finally,
TGPrint exploits GCAN’s learning ability of graph representation to generate ﬁngerprints and mines crucial attack behavior patterns to classify
attacks.

2.1.2. Machine learning approaches
ML algorithms have been the critical research direction in recent
years, whose main idea is to determine what attack there is by analyzing the encrypted network traﬃc features (Anderson and McGrew,
2017; FRANTISEK S, 2017; Alqudah and Yaseen, 2020; Lopez-Martin et
al., 2017; Torroledo et al., 2018b; Duessel et al., 2017). Anderson (Anderson and McGrew, 2017) demonstrates six ML algorithms perform
well with malicious traﬃc classiﬁcation when confronted with real network data. Frantisek (FRANTISEK S, 2017) compares four ML methods,
XGBoost, Random Forest, Neural Network, and SVM, for TLS encrypted
malicious traﬃc detection. They conclude that XGBoost and Random
Forest methods are eﬀective, and the accuracy of XGBoost and Random Forest is about 95%. Besides, Deep Learning (DL), a new branch
of ML, is also fully applied to attack classiﬁcation on encrypted traﬃc.
Ivan (Torroledo et al., 2018b) analyze and summarize forty features in
four classes for detecting malware and phishing signatures using LSTM
and obtain 94.87% and 88.64% accuracy, respectively.
However, feature processing is a signiﬁcant challenge in ML approaches, which can directly aﬀect the network’s classiﬁcation performance and require much work. How to extract and represent the limited
features in the encrypted traﬃc are the key to identifying attacks accurately.

3.2. Graph neural networks for graph representation
Graphs, a data structure consisting of nodes and edges, provide an
intuitive representation of abstract relationships and interactions. Mathematically, a graph 𝐺 is deﬁned as a tuple (𝑉 , 𝐸) where 𝑉 is the set of
nodes, and 𝐸 is the set of edges linking to the nodes. Each node, 𝑣 ∈ 𝑉
is associated with n-dimensional vectors 𝑥𝑣 , 𝑛 ∈ ℕ. With the prevalence
of graph-structured data, there is an increasing need for graph representation learning (Chen et al., 2020). Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are
a practical framework for the representation learning of graphs. GNNs
follow a neighborhood aggregation scheme, where the representation
vector of a node is computed by recursively aggregating and transforming representation vectors of its neighboring nodes (Xu et al., 2018).
Many variants of GNNs with diﬀerent neighborhood aggregation and
graph-level pooling schemes have been proposed. Graph isomorphism
network (GIN) (Xu et al., 2018) can achieve maximum discriminative
power by producing the embedding of the entire graph when given embeddings of individual nodes. Graph convolution network (GCN) (Kipf
and Welling, 2016) scales linearly in the number of graph edges via a
localized ﬁrst-order approximation of spectral graph convolutions and
learns hidden layer representations that encode both local graph structure and features of nodes. Graph Attention Networks (GAT) (Veličković
et al., 2017) leverages masked self-attentional layers and speciﬁes different weights to diﬀerent nodes in a neighborhood by stacking layers
to which nodes can attend their neighborhoods’ features. This paper
uses GIN, GCN, and GAT layers to construct the model and represent
and classify the attack.

2.2. Unseen attack classiﬁcation
Coping to recognize unseen attacks is particularly important in network security (Popoola et al., 2021; Duessel et al., 2017; Hindy et al.,
2020; Bar and Hajaj, 2022; Sarhan et al., 2021), where new attack
vectors are constantly emerging and continue to break through cyber
security defenses. The low accuracy rate that occurs when identifying
unseen attacks causes signiﬁcant damage to network security. Sarhan
(Sarhan et al., 2021) maps the network data features using ML models
to distinguish semantic attributes from seen attack classes and detect
the unseen classes by constructing the relationships between known and
zero-day attacks. Popoola (Popoola et al., 2021) proposes the federated
DL (FDL) method for zero-day botnet attack detection to avoid data privacy leakage in IoT-edge devices. Hindy (Hindy et al., 2020) proposes
an auto-encoder implementation beneﬁting greatly from encodingdecoding capabilities in zero-day attack detection. Bar (Bar and Hajaj,
2022) and Duessel (Duessel et al., 2017) inspired by natural language
processing (NLP), incorporate syntactic context into attack detection
and increases unseen attack detection accuracy. These studies work a lot
on feature processing to detect unknown attacks. However, they need to
focus on interaction behaviors between attack-victim hosts in an attack
and ﬁnd fundamental techniques.

4. Approach
This paper aims to ﬁngerprint persistent attacks on encrypted network traﬃc. Fig. 2 shows an overview of our approach TGPrint: we take
network traﬃc of attack-victim hosts as input and generate ﬁngerprints
that identify attacks. After feature extraction, TGPrint ﬁrst removes normal ﬂows to eliminate the noise using ML tools and leaves suspicious
traﬃc. Subsequently, TGPrint clusters suspicious attack-victim hosts’
traﬃc and divides them according to a speciﬁc time window for each
attack. After that, TGPrint portrays attack behaviors as a graph and
constructs a set of graphs for each attack type. Then, TGPrint exploits
GCAN to generate ﬁngerprints and mines crucial attack behavior patterns. Finally, the generated ﬁngerprints can recognize attacks precisely
based on attack behavior patterns from attack graphs.

3. Background
3.1. Attack vectors
Attack vectors are the means and methods used to achieve an attack,
and various attack vectors can accomplish an attack. Diﬀerent attack
vectors execute diﬀerent operations during the attack, and the malicious traﬃc generated from diverse attack operations is disparate. Fig. 1
shows the traﬃc distribution of Bruteforce and DDoS with diﬀerent attack vectors from three datasets. Traﬃc is mapped into 2-dimensions to
show the diﬀerence between various attack vectors of the same attack,
Bruteforce and DDoS, intuitively. Bruteforce has two attack vectors,
HTTP and XML, in HIKARI, and four attack vectors, SSH, FTP, Web,
and XSS, in CICIDS. DDoS has two attack vectors, HOIC and LOIC, in
CICIDS. The traﬃc distribution changes considerably when the attack
vector is changed. Even if the classiﬁcation model has seen the attack
before, it can still not identify it when a new attack vector comes. Unlike 0day attacks, unseen attacks in this paper refer to the emergence of
a new attack vector in the same type of attack. For example, in bruteforce, an XML-based implementation of the attack is an unseen attack
for the original http-based one. Therefore, the ability to unseen attack
recognition is essential in evaluating a model.

4.2.3. Attack graph construction
High-level attack behaviors with complex interactions among hosts
are a natural graph structure where nodes represent hosts and edges
represent interaction activities. The similar step, converting traﬃc ﬂows
to graphs (Yao et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2020; Hammann et al., 2019; Pham
et al., 2021).
In this paper, an attack type has a set of attack graphs whose nodes
are the host’s ports, edges indicate the communication behaviors between ports, and edges’ attributes describe the behaviors. The edges’ attributes indicate the attack communication behaviors through features,
which are statistical, temporal, and aggregate features (as described in
4.1.2). The node vectors of a graph are two hosts’ ports without IP because the IP address is useless for edge attributes in each graph, which
has the same IP pairs after the cluster. Finally, an attack graph is the
attack behavioral record between the attack-victim hosts in one time
window. In 4.2.4, TGPrint uses these attack graphs to mine hidden attack behavior patterns for attack classiﬁcation.

4.2. Attack ﬁngerprinting
4.2.1. Cluster based on host pair
TGPrint clusters the suspicious traﬃc according to the host’s IP pairs
because of the attacker’s persistent behaviors toward the victim host.
After clustering the traﬃc according to IP pairs, it is possible to analyze
the communication interactions between the two hosts and to obtain
attack behaviors.

4.2.2. Division based on time window
Attack duration is unstable and varies in diﬀerent persistent attacks.
TGPrint divides attack traﬃc for each IP pair to adapt to the behaviors’
changing over time. When dividing traﬃc, the longer the time window
is, the more traﬃc is captured, and the more diverse attack behaviors
are observed. However, each attack duration is diﬀerent. Various time
windows according to attack types can divide traﬃc more precisely in
the correct type. So we set the time window ratio 𝜏, a parameter that

5.3.2. Impact of batch size for attack classiﬁcation
Batch size is a critical parameter in the training process of GCAN
since it impacts the experimental results signiﬁcantly. 16 and 32 are
chosen because of their high experiment use. A series of experiments are
conducted on the HIKARI2021 dataset. We compare the classiﬁcation
accuracy with diﬀerent time window ratios 𝜏 when the batch size is 16
and 32.
The red dashed line is the baseline for an accuracy of 0.8. The results
in Fig. 6 show that accuracy is higher when the batch size is 32 in attack
classiﬁcation. This is because an attack graph contains more behavior
information useful for attack classiﬁcation when the time window rate
increases. However, the accuracy decreases as the time window rate
exceeds 0.4 when the batch size is 16. The model fails to obtain the
complete attack behavior information and quickly falls into local optimal if the batch size is too small for training. Therefore, we chose 32 as
the batch size in later experiments.

To eliminate noise traﬃc in attack classiﬁcation, we use the ML

traﬃc denoising. The results on the whole HIKARI 2021 are shown in

As shown in Table 6, SVM, KNN, Random Forest, and DNN achieve
good identiﬁcation capabilities that accurately distinguish between normal and attack traﬃc. Except for SVM, the models can achieve accuracy
recall and F1 values over 90%. Although results indicate that ML models can accurately distinguish normal and attack traﬃc in attack traﬃc
denoising, it is still uncertain how well they identify normal traﬃc.
In group Fig. 5, the x coordinate is the proportion of test samples
to all normal traﬃc. The results demonstrate that the Random Forest
performs best, scoring high evaluation metrics in normal traﬃc, which
indicates that the model accurately identiﬁes normal traﬃc. We believe
this is because normal behavior is ﬁxed and constant, so the characteristics of normal traﬃc are also accessible for the model to learn.
Moreover, no decrease is in model recognition over time. Hence, ML
models can be used for attack traﬃc denoising and accurately identifying normal traﬃc. In TGPrint, we choose Random Forest as the tool.

attacks accurately.

This paper proposes TGPrint to classify attacks in encrypted traﬃc.
However, there are two limitations existing.
Feasibility in real networks. Although TGPrint is veriﬁed on
datasets from a real network environment, how feasible it would work
in a real network still needs to be determined. More practical factors, such as identiﬁcation eﬃciency, resource occupancy, and network
structure, should be considered in a real network. In TGPrint, the time
window rate 𝜏 also aﬀects the performance in real networks. These
eﬀects cannot be judged on experiment results on datasets alone. In future research, TGPrint should implement in real-time in real networks.
Meanwhile, the calculation should be carried out in time complexity
and space complexity to verify the performance of TGPrint.

Despite variations in attack implementations, their persistent network
behaviors on victim hosts are captured in traﬃc records. By constructing attack graphs and learning patterns within deﬁned time windows,
TGPrint excels in identifying attacks characterized by persistent network behaviors.
However, TGPrint’s eﬃcacy in dealing with non-persistent attacks
remains uncertain. Learning attack patterns from a single-ﬂow attack
graph poses challenges for TGPrint. The model struggles to discern attack patterns in such scenarios. Subsequent research endeavors are to
delve deeper into TGPrint’s classiﬁcation capabilities, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of its potential.

Incomplete attack types. The types of attacks are incomplete and
limited in datasets, and more attack types are in real networks. This
paper proves TGPrint’s classiﬁcation performance on collected attacks,
but its performance on other attacks in real networks needs further discussion. TGPrint identiﬁes bruteforce, inﬁltration, mining, and DDoS,
but if a worm occurs, TGPrint may misdiagnose because there is no
10

corresponding attack ﬁngerprint. While attacks will not be classiﬁed
as normal, there may be misclassiﬁcations between diﬀerent attacks.
Therefore, the ﬁngerprint database generated by TGPrint decides the
performance of attack classiﬁcation and aﬀects network security. An incomplete attack ﬁngerprint database will give wrong judgment in attack

Universality of diﬀerent encryption methods. While TLS stands
as the predominant encryption protocol, alternatives like Tor and VPN
persist. Concealed attacks within these methods necessitate attention.

universal applicability to encrypted traﬃc. Thus, further investigation
is required to address attack classiﬁcation in diverse encryption-based
traﬃc scenarios.
7. Conclusion
This paper presents TGPrint, a novel attack classiﬁcation approach
that obtains crucial attack behavior patterns and deals with unseen attack classiﬁcation. We ﬁrst use Random Forest for attack traﬃc denoising to remove the normal traﬃc. Then, we propose a novel parameter
time window ratio 𝜏 to divide a speciﬁc duration for each attack to
elaborate attack graphs precisely. Finally, we utilize GCAN to mine and
grasp the crucial behavior patterns from attack graphs to generate ﬁngerprints and classify attacks. Extensive experiments are conducted on
three datasets to verify TGPrint. The experimental results show that TGPrint achieves a precision of 99% in attack classiﬁcation on encrypted
traﬃc, an average higher than other ML methods of 50%. It also classiﬁes unseen attacks with an average accuracy of over 80% and has a
solid robustness to false positives.
