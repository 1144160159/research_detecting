Adaptive Multi-Scale Window Segmentation and
Attention Interaction Transformer for Intrusion
Detection in Industrial Internet of Things
Zhi Yu , Jiaming Pei , and Mingpeng Zhu

Abstract—As the core of modern industrial automation,
Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) can achieve data collection, control, and remote operations. However, the complex
continuous connectivity exposes IIoT to increasing cyber
intrusions. Existing intrusion detection methods often fail
to adapt to diverse temporal attack patterns, lack effective
feature modeling, and perform poorly under class imbalance. To this end, this article proposes a novel transformerbased IIoT intrusion detection method. First, an adaptive
multi-scale window segmentation (AMSWS) strategy is proposed to dynamically segment traffic sequences via traffic
variation. This can capture short-term and long-term intrusion behaviors. Second, an attention interaction transformer (AITrans) is proposed to enhance feature modeling
by enabling inter-head and inter-layer attention interaction.
Finally, an adaptive focal loss (AFL) is proposed to mitigate
class imbalance by incorporating adaptive class weight and
hardness-aware adjustment. This can improve detection
performance on minority-class intrusions. Extensive experiments validate the effectiveness of the proposed method
for IIoT intrusion detection.

I. INTRODUCTION
HE Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) is revolutionizing
industrial automation and related domains by integrating
intelligent sensing, data communication, and efficient computation [1], [2], [3], [4]. As IIoT relies on persistent network
connectivity, this makes it an attractive target for cyber attackers.
Traditional security mechanisms often fall short in complex intrusion environments [5], [6], [7], [8]. Therefore, constructing a
robust intrusion detection framework is critical for IIoT security.

Early methods rely on convolutional neural networks
(CNNs) [9], [10], recurrent neural networks (RNNs) [11], [12],
long short-term memory networks (LSTMs) [13], [14], and
related variants. However, CNNs cannot capture temporal dependencies, while RNNs and LSTMs face vanishing gradients
that hinder long-range dependence learning.
Recently, transformers have gained attention with strong
modeling abilities in IIoT intrusion detection [15], [16], [17].
However, several limitations remain. First, most methods rely on
fixed-size windows, thereby limiting the capture of short-term
burst anomalies and long-term stealthy anomalies. Second, most
methods lack explicit cross-head and cross-layer information
interaction, thereby weakening discriminative representations.
Third, most methods struggle to effectively learn features from
rare samples, thereby causing degraded performance on lowfrequency intrusions.
To this end, this article proposes a novel transformer-based
IIoT intrusion detection method.
First, an adaptive multi-scale window segmentation (AMSWS) strategy is proposed to capture heterogeneous temporal
patterns for IIoT intrusions. Specifically, when traffic exhibits
short-term drastic changes, AMSWS applies smaller windows to
capture burst threats. Conversely, when traffic exhibits long-term
hidden threats, AMSWS expands windows to analyze longer
sequential features. This overcomes the rigidity of fixed-window
designs by adjusting windows via traffic variation. Although
some adaptive window techniques have been explored in timeseries analysis [18], [19], [20], [21], [22], most methods generally involve heavy algorithmic structures, intricate parameter
tuning, and high computational overhead. In contrast, AMSWS
adopts a lightweight and continuously adjustable mechanism via
traffic variation. Without relying on complex statistical analysis, AMSWS generates non-overlapping multi-scale windows,
which are directly integrated with subsequent feature mappings
to effectively capture both short-term burst anomalies and longterm stealthy anomalies.
Then, an attention interaction transformer (AITrans) is proposed to enhance traffic modeling. While recent hierarchical
transformer variants for intrusion detection have improved feature extraction [23], [24], [25], [26], [27], most architectures
compute multiple attention heads independently within the same
layer, lacking explicit inter-head attention interaction. This hinders the learning of complementary patterns across different
subspaces. In addition, few existing models incorporate effective

The contributions of this article are summarized as follows.
1) An AMSWS strategy is proposed to dynamically adjust
windows. Unlike prior fixed-size windowing methods
that lack adaptability to diverse intrusion patterns or
complex adaptive windowing methods that incur high
computational overhead, AMSWS employs a lightweight
window adjustment strategy via traffic variation. AMSWS utilizes smaller windows to capture short-term
burst anomalies and larger windows to capture long-term
stealthy anomalies. This enables the model to adaptively
align with transient and persistent intrusion patterns.
2) An AITrans is proposed to enhance traffic modeling. Unlike existing transformers that lack explicit cross-head and
cross-layer information exchange, AITrans utilizes IHIA
and ILIA to improve traffic representation. AITrans enables the extraction of complementary information across
attention subspaces and allows higher layers to effectively
utilize the cues learned in lower layers.
3) An AFL is proposed to mitigate class imbalance. Unlike embedding complex generative models to synthesize

directly optimizing the learning with a tailored loss. It
integrates ACW and HAA to tune the class weight and
the focusing parameter. AFL can guide AITrans to focus
on rare samples.
II. RELATED WORKS
Existing IoT intrusion detection methods can be divided into
non-transformer methods and transformer methods. Table I summarizes highlights and limitations of related methods.
A. Intrusion Detection via Non-Transformer Methods
Qathrady et al. [28] proposed a self-attention CNN termed
SACNN to isolate the most relevant traffic features for intrusion detection. It incorporates the self-attention mechanism to
compute attention weights from the input data, followed by
the convolutional layers that utilize these weighted features to
perform accurate detection.
Li et al. [29] proposed an anomaly-based intrusion detection
method termed CRSF. It uses the dimension transformation to
convert input data into 2-D images. Spatial features are extracted
via the CNN, and the generated features are processed via
the RNN to capture temporal dependencies. A support vector
machine (SVM) is employed as the classifier to project the
features and learn nonlinear decision boundaries.
Zeng et al. [30] proposed an unsupervised intrusion detection
method termed EvoAAE. Specifically, a variational autoencoder
(VAE) based on the generative adversarial network (GAN) is designed to adversarially generate multivariate time series. Then,
the particle swarm optimization (PSO) with an efficient binary
encoding is designed to optimize hyper-parameters and neural
architectures in the adversarial VAE.
Lu et al. [31] proposed an intrusion detection method termed
MODEO-CNN based on the multi-objective joint optimization, which integrates the neural architecture search (NAS)
and the hyper-parameter optimization (HPO). It introduces a
hybrid variable-length encoding combining binary and integer

block parameters, and key hyper-parameters.
Vadakkethil et al. [32] proposed an intrusion detection method
that combines the Mayfly optimization algorithm (MOA) with
the bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM). MOA is used for feature
selection by balancing exploration and exploitation to identify
the most relevant features. BiLSTM is used for processing input
to capture long-range temporal dependencies.
Yang et al. [33] proposed an intrusion detection method that
integrates the attention, the bidirectional gated recurrent units
(BiGRU), and the Inception CNN. A mixed sampling strategy
is adopted to mitigate the impact of data imbalance, while
denoising techniques are applied to reduce noise introduced
during resampling.
Overall, intrusion detection methods via non-transformer perform effectively in learning local spatial features and modeling
short-term temporal dependencies. Besides, these methods exhibit clear advantages in terms of lightweight computation and
relatively low model complexity. However, the limited receptive fields and strictly sequential computation hinder effective
modeling of long-range temporal dependencies. Furthermore,
most methods rely on complex sampling strategies or generative
algorithms to mitigate class imbalance, which increases design
complexity.
B. Intrusion Detection via Transformer Methods
Liang et al. [25] proposed a multi-level intrusion detection
model that integrates the transformer with the discrete wavelet
transform (DWT). By embedding DWT and multi-wavelet
learning into the transformer, the model effectively captures
frequency-based features from IoT traffic, improving the detection accuracy for intrusion behaviors.
Fares et al. [34] proposed an intrusion detection model termed
TFKAN, which incorporates the Kolmogorov-Arnold network
(KAN) layers in place of the multi-layer perceptron (MLP)
layers. The KAN utilizes learnable univariate functions instead
of fixed weights, allowing for more compact and expressive
representations. Therefore, TFKAN achieves competitive performance with fewer parameters.
Moghaddam et al. [35] proposed a hybrid architecture that
integrates the transformer for capturing temporal and contextual
dependencies with the GAN for generating synthetic data to
address class imbalance. Besides, an improved non-dominated
sorting biogeography-based optimization algorithm is introduced to optimize hyper-parameters.
Wang et al. [36] proposed an intrusion detection method
termed DIFT to address class imbalance by combining the
diffusion model (DM) with the transformer. The former learns
features of minority-class samples and generates more balanced
data. To improve processing efficiency, a patching method is
applied to shorten the input and enhance local features. Subsequently, a time transformer is designed to extract local and
global features.
Zeng et al. [37] proposed a causal transformer for intrusion
detection. The first module classifies samples into known categories using prior knowledge. The second module employs

causal attention to learn causal features. The final module groups
samples differing from known categories as unknown types and
classifies these samples with a causal transformer.
Wang et al. [38] proposed an intrusion detection framework
termed ImagTIDS, which integrates the Gramian angular difference fields (GADF) with the transformer. The traffic is transformed into images using GADF to retain temporal and global
features. ImagTrans then extracts local and global features. By
leveraging self-attention, the model dynamically adjusts attention weights to emphasize critical features while reducing the
impact of redundant information.
Overall, intrusion detection methods via transformer demonstrate significant strengths in capturing long-range dependencies to improve robustness. Besides, transformer variants can
integrate other information to enrich features. However, these
methods face some limitations. First, these methods mainly
rely on fixed-size windows, which fail to adapt to the diverse
temporal patterns of intrusions. This limits the ability to capture
short-term burst anomalies and long-term stealthy anomalies.
Although some adaptive windowing strategies have been explored in time-series analysis, most methods involve complex
algorithms and analysis. Then, these transformer-based methods
compute attention heads independently within the same layer.
The explicit inter-head and inter-layer information interaction
are ignored. This impedes cross-subspace learning and weakens
hierarchical representation. Finally, these methods cannot effectively address class imbalance. Although some methods utilize
generative models to synthesize minority-class samples, such
methods incur high computational cost and introduce synthetic
noise.
To solve the above three issues, AMSWS, AITrans, and
AFL are proposed. AMSWS embeds a lightweight window
adjustment strategy to adaptively divide traffic for capturing
short-term burst anomalies and long-term stealthy anomalies.
AITran embeds IHIA and ILIA to facilitate inter-head and
inter-layer attention interaction for improving traffic modeling.
AFL embeds ACW and HAA to tune the class weight and the
focusing parameter for mitigating class imbalance.

D. Adaptive Focal Loss
In IIoT intrusion detection, anomalous traffic is only triggered
by attacks or abnormal events. The class imbalance causes the
model to favor learning normal traffic patterns, thereby reducing
detection performance on minority classes. Therefore, focal
loss [39] is introduced to enhance the detection capability for
minority-class samples. However, this loss still suffers from the
issue of fixed parameters, which makes it difficult to adapt to
different data distributions.
Therefore, AFL is proposed to improve focal loss by incorporating ACW and HAA. ACW adjusts the class weight at
the global level via class frequency. It aligns with the sample
balancing principle and mitigates majority-class dominance. Inspired by hardness-aware learning, HAA modulates the focusing
parameter via sample confidence to focus on hard samples.
Unlike using complex generative models to generate samples,
AFL directly improves the loss with lightweight and adaptive
mechanisms. This allows the model to better handle rare anomalies and avoid the computational cost from generative methods.
The standard focal loss is as follows:
LF L = −(1 − pt )γ log(pt )

(21)

where pt is the sample confidence, γ is the focusing parameter.
To enhance focal loss in handling imbalanced data, AFL
introduces ACW and HAA as follows:

via sample confidence to focus on hard samples.

samples struggle to exert sufficient influence on the model,
making it difficult to accurately detect rare anomalous traffic.

the weights for different classes. The core idea is to modify
the class weight via the corresponding class frequency, thereby

2) Hardness-Aware Adjustment: Focal loss mitigates the impact of easy samples by assigning higher loss to hard samples.
However, it employs a fixed focusing parameter. This rigid focus
is difficult to adapt to different data distributions. It has some
positive effects on hard samples, but may not be sufficiently
fine-grained. Therefore, this article introduces HAA, which
dynamically adjusts the focusing parameter based on the sample
confidence as follows:

γt = γmax − (γmax − γmin )(1 − pt )

(24)

where γmin and γmax are min and max focusing parameter, and
pt is the sample confidence. For easy samples (pt is high), γt is
close to γmax , the loss will decrease. Similarly, for hard samples
(pt is low), γt is close to γmin , the loss will increase.
By dynamically adjusting γt , HAA ensures that the model
focuses more on hard samples. This reduces over-reliance on
easy samples and enhances the ability to detect minority-class
anomalies. HAA uses a lightweight, hardness-driven adaptation
mechanism that reflects sample difficulty during training. By
dynamically adjusting the focusing parameter via sample confidence, HAA achieves more flexible and data-driven optimization, thereby better highlighting hard samples. This provides a
theoretically sound and practically effective solution for finegrained optimization in class balance.
By integrating ACW and HAA, AFL not only adjusts the
class weight via class frequency but also modifies the focusing
parameter via sample difficulty. This focuses on minority-class
samples and mitigates class imbalance. When combined with
AITrans, it further enhances intrusion detection capabilities.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Datasets
This article constructs IIoTA and IIoTB datasets. Each sample is captured using Wireshark and system logging modules.
The intrusion categories in IIoTA and IIoTB are aligned with
TON_IoT [6], thereby covering the major intrusion types commonly observed in IIoT systems. Specifically, IIoTA and IIoTB
contain ten categories: Normal, DoS, DDoS, Scanning, MITM,
Injection, XSS, Password, Backdoor, and Ransomware.
In real-world IIoT networks, normal traffic typically dominates, while anomalous traffic accounts for a relatively small
proportion. Moreover, the frequency of occurrence varies across
different intrusions. To reflect this realistic setting, different
numbers of samples are collected for different categories. IIoTA
contains 218 863 samples, the quantities for each category are
100 392, 12 032, 10 679, 9904, 13 561, 8996, 9104, 11 019, 9531,
and 33 645. IIoTB contains 436 794 samples, the quantities
for each category are 231 123, 31 529, 23 638, 19 177, 23 498,
22 916, 19 973, 36 145, 20 561, and 8234. These distributions
are intended to realistically represent traffic imbalances.
Each traffic sample is represented as a fixed-length time series
with 300 time steps. This setting can capture significant intrusion
patterns and retain sufficient contextual information. It also
avoids gradient vanishing and excessive computational overhead
caused by overly long sequences. In addition, many studies on
intrusion detection adopt sequence lengths of around 300 time
steps for input construction [17], [40].

B. Implementation Details
All experiments are conducted on a workstation equipped
with NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU, Intel Core i9-12900 K CPU,
Ubuntu 20.04, Python 3.7, and PyTorch 1.8.1. Accuracy (Acc),
and F1 score are evaluation metrics.
The Adam optimizer with β1 = 0.9 and β2 = 0.999 is employed to train AITrans. These are default settings in the Adam
paper and have been validated in deep learning [43]. The initial
learning rate is 0.001, which allows the model to converge
efficiently while maintaining stability. A decay factor of 0.1 is
set to reduce the learning rate during the training stage, which
avoids oscillation and overfitting. These settings are commonly
adopted in intrusion detection studies [44], [45].
The common batch sizes are 32, 64, 128, and 256 in intrusion
detection. In a reasonable range, larger batches aid training
stability and convergence speed. However, excessively large
batches cause overfitting and exceed GPU memory. Therefore, a
batch size of 128 is adopted to strike a balance between training
speed and generalization. Each batch includes eight categories,
each category with 16 samples. These settings are commonly
adopted in intrusion detection studies [46], [47].
The parameters in (7) are Wmin = 10 and Wmax = 100. Setting Wmin = 10 captures short-term fluctuations to detect rapidly
changing and bursty anomalies. Setting Wmax = 100 covers
longer intrusion patterns to detect slow and stealthy anomalies.
If the window is too small, contextual information may be lost. If
the window is too large, critical features may be smoothed out.
Therefore, setting Wmin = 10 and Wmax = 100 can provide a
balance that enables the model to effectively capture short-term
and long-term intrusions.
The parameters in (24) are γmin = 1 and γmax = 5. The original focal loss paper recommends γ = 2. Building on this, the
range of γ is further extended to allow adaptation to samples
of varying difficulty. If γ is too small, the loss may degenerate

into cross-entropy loss. It fails to suppress the dominance of
easy samples and address class imbalance. If γ is too large,
the loss contributions of samples are excessively amplified,
causing gradient fluctuations and unstable training. Therefore,
setting γmin = 1 and γmax = 5 can effectively guide the model
to suppress easy samples and to focus on hard samples for
mitigating the issue of class imbalance.

The complexity analysis is shown in Table II. To evaluate
the model complexity, this article considers two categories of
metrics based on the association with complexity. The first category of metrics has a relatively strong correlation to complexity,
including Parameters (Params), FLOPs, GPU Usage (GU), CPU
Usage (CU), GPU Power (GP), CPU Power (CP), Inference
Time (IT) per sample, GPU Energy (GE), and CPU Energy (CE).
The lower these metrics, the lower the model complexity. Among
them, Params, FLOPs, and IT are three key metrics. Params
represents the number of trainable parameters, namely the total
amount of weights and biases in the model. FLOPs represents
the number of floating-point operations in a single inference,
reflecting the arithmetic computations during one forward pass.
IT represents the time taken for a sample to be processed from
input into the network to final classification. In general, under
the same software and hardware conditions, a more complex
model tends to exhibit larger Params, higher FLOPs, and longer
IT. However, the relationship among Params, FLOPs, and IT
is not strictly proportional, because these metrics depend not
only on model size but also on architectural design choices
and data processing strategies. The second category of metrics
has a relatively weak correlation to complexity, including Inference Time/Parameters (IT/Params), GPU Energy/Parameters
(GE/Params), and CPU Energy/Parameters (CE/Params). These
metrics provide additional insights into algorithmic efficiency
per parameter, but they may not directly reflect overall complexity. When Params increases significantly, these metrics may
decrease even if IT, GE, and CE are higher. Therefore, a more
complex model with larger Params may yield a lower IT/Params,
GE/Params, and CE/Params.

V. CONCLUSION
This article proposed an IIoT intrusion detection approach.
First, an AMSWS strategy was proposed to dynamically adjust
the segmentation window via traffic variation. This accurately
captured anomalies exhibiting diverse temporal characteristics,
thus effectively detecting various types of intrusion patterns.
Second, an AITrans comprising IHIA and ILIA was proposed
to facilitate effective inter-head and inter-layer attention interaction. This enhanced the capability for representing complex
anomaly patterns. Finally, an AFL comprising ACW and HAA
was proposed to mitigate the class imbalance issue. This enabled
the model to better detect minority-class anomalous samples.
Despite the competitive performance achieved by the proposed method in IIoT intrusion detection, several limitations
remain. First, since AITrans is built upon the transformer, the
complexity is relatively higher compared to non-transformer
approaches. This may limit the scalability when deployed in
extremely large-scale IIoT networks with limited resources.
