MiKA-HAD: Minority-Augmented KAN-Based
Hybrid Self-Supervised Framework for
Hyperspectral Anomaly Detection
Dandan Ma , Zhuozhao Liu, Zhiyu Jiang , Yi Zheng , and Qi Wang

Abstract—In recent years, autoencoder (AE)-based models
have demonstrated significant potential in hyperspectral anomaly
detection (HAD). However, the inherent class imbalance between
anomaly and background samples, coupled with substantial
intraclass heterogeneity within background components, often
leads traditional models to excessively capture majority-class
background features, thereby weakening their discriminative
capabilities for minority-class backgrounds and anomaly targets.
Concurrently, conventional methods fail to sufficiently model
nonlinear features and adapt to complex environments, undermining their detection performance and robustness in intricate
environmental interference. To address these challenges, we
propose MiKA-HAD, a minority-augmented Kolmogorov–Arnold
network (KAN)-based hybrid self-supervised framework for
HAD. It incorporates a density-based clustering-guided sample balancing strategy that dynamically synthesizes minority
background samples with spectral–spatial diversity through
self-supervised learning. This approach achieves feature space
rebalancing while effectively suppressing noise interference on
anomaly boundaries. To overcome high-dimensional data complexity and nonlinear feature extraction challenges, we construct
a hybrid self-supervised network with a KAN-based multipath
collaborative module that orchestrates synergy between local
sensitivity preservation, nonlinear feature modeling, and global
consistency maintenance. This tripartite architecture establishes
a dynamic equilibrium that enhances representation capability
and environmental robustness. Extensive experiments show that
MiKA-HAD achieves superior detection accuracy and stability compared to existing approaches, particularly in complex
environments with varying noise conditions. The framework
establishes a new paradigm for robust HAD by addressing both
class imbalance bias and nonlinear feature modeling limitations.

I. I NTRODUCTION

H

YPERSPECTRAL anomaly detection (HAD) enables
the precise identification of targets by comparing pixels with distinct spatial and spectral characteristics to their
adjacent background, even when prior data is unavailable
[1]. This technology overcomes the reliance on target prior
knowledge, demonstrating unique adaptability in unknown and
complex scenarios. With the development of remote sensing
platforms [2], [3], HAD has emerged as a frontier research
direction in the field of multidimensional information sensing,
deeply integrated into significant engineering practices such
as crop stress monitoring in precision agriculture [4], [5],
disaster life detection in emergency management [6], and
concealed target localization in national defense security
[7].
Statistically based HAD methods operate under probabilistic assumptions, where background pixels conform to
predefined statistical distributions while anomalies manifest
as significant deviations. The Reed–Xiaoli (RX) detector [8]
identifies anomalous regions through Mahalanobis distance
calculation between target pixels and background distributions under Gaussian distribution assumptions. Kwon and
Nasrabadi [9] pioneered the integration of reproducing kernel Hilbert space theory to characterize high-order spectral
relationships in nonlinearly separable scenarios. Zhao et
al. [10] enhanced robustness through a hybrid framework
combining kernelization with robust iterative estimation. Li
et al. [11] extended the isolation forest to kernel space,
leveraging both global statistics and local spatial–contextual
information. Despite their computational efficiency, statistical methods remain constrained by simplified distribution
assumptions and often fail in scenarios with complex
backgrounds.
Data representation-based methods identify anomalies by
computing reconstruction errors from background dictionaries, where normal pixels exhibit lower residuals compared
to anomalies. Li et al. [12] introduced a joint sparse
representation-based detector incorporating atomic decomposition from over-complete dictionaries. Zhao et al. [13] devised
a quantitative sparsity assessment methodology improving dis-

Deep learning-based methods have been established as
the predominant approach for anomaly detection, primarily
employing autoencoder (AE) architectures to model background distributions through reconstruction error analysis [18].
Wang et al. [19] combined fully convolutional AEs with
adaptive weighted loss functions to suppress anomalous reconstruction errors. Aghili et al. [20] developed dual-domain AEs,
separating anomalies from background components through
nonnegative matrix factorization. Various sampling optimization strategies have been explored, including diagonal subsampling for reconstruction consistency [21], random masking
[22], and multiscale masking [23] for enhanced background
feature learning. For multimodal feature fusion, researchers
have introduced dual-window masking [24], feature aggregation mechanisms [25], and spatial–spectral cross-guided
frameworks [26]. In adversarial learning paradigms, Li et al.
[27] proposed an unsupervised transfer detection model with
global–local self-attention modules, while Wang et al. [28]
developed a capsule differential adversarial reconstruction
network. Chen et al. [29] proposed a double-window iterative spectral diffusion and inverse reconstruction method,
which achieved accurate background estimation. He et al. [30]
improved the Transformer network to capture deep reconstruction features from global to local views in the spatial-spectral
domain. Fu et al. [31] proposed a multiscale Mamba network
to enhance the representation of the background and suppress
the reconstruction of anomalies. Liu et al. [32] proposed
adaptive dual-domain learning based on the spatial spectrum to
extract high-frequency abnormal signals in images. Although
these methods have significantly improved detection performance, their reconstruction processes remain susceptible to
interference from low signal-to-noise ratio data, resulting in
lower detection accuracy.

A. Autoencoder
AE, as an unsupervised deep neural network architecture, has gained prominence in HAD due to its distinctive
feature extraction capability and noise robustness. The fundamental structure comprises symmetrical encoder–decoder
components that collaboratively learn low-dimensional representations through data reconstruction [18].
In HAD applications, AE’s effectiveness stems from
hyperspectral imagery’s inherent statistics: background pixels
dominate while anomalies are extremely sparse [34]. During
training, gradient descent prioritizes learning spectral mixing
patterns of background regions, whereas the modeling capacity for rare anomalies remains underdeveloped. This feature
learning bias creates a natural detection mechanism where
anomalous pixels generate prominent reconstruction residuals
compared to background components.

The overall framework of our proposed MiKA-HAD
(minority-augmented KAN-based hybrid self-supervised
framework for HAD) is illustrated in Fig. 1. First, a
background sample selection and amplification strategy is
developed to dynamically identify and augment minority-class

Second, a hybrid self-supervised network is constructed to

sensitivity preservation, nonlinear feature modeling, and
global consistency maintenance for augmenting the model’s
adaptability and flexibility to complex hyperspectral scenes.

To address this challenge, MiKA-HAD incorporates a
density-based clustering-guided background sample selection
and amplification strategy. After PCA-based dimensionality
reduction and normalization, we leverage the hierarchical
density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise
(HDBSCAN) method [40] to identify and separate minorityclass background samples from majority classes and potential
anomalies. HDBSCAN combines the ideas of density clustering and hierarchical clustering, using a density hierarchy
tree to represent the clustering structure of the data. This
approach exploits the inherent spectral–spatial distribution
patterns in hyperspectral data: majority backgrounds typically
form dense, continuous clusters with strong spatial correlation, while minority backgrounds and anomalies manifest
as sparse, isolated structures due to their spectral deviation
from dominant patterns. With hyperparameters min-cluster
size mc and min-samples ms, the HDBSCAN algorithm can
generate multiple sample clusters and isolated noise points.
We designate clusters with fewer than k elements as sparse
clusters (minority-class backgrounds), where the threshold k
is determined dynamically to ensure sparse clusters constitute
r% of the total clustered pixels. This adaptive thresholding
approach ensures consistent minority sample identification
across varied datasets. Importantly, we exclude isolated noise
points (potential anomalies) to prevent anomaly contamination during background modeling. Fig. 3 illustrates the
HDBSCAN clustering results on the Los Angeles-II dataset.
In Fig. 3(b) and (c), black pixels represent background
regions, white pixels denote true anomaly areas, and red
pixels mark isolated pixels identified by HDBSCAN. These
isolated pixels account for only 3.10% of the total pixels but
cover 60.92% of the anomaly regions. To improve anomaly
suppression, we exclude the isolated pixels from network
training. Notably, erroneously excluded background regions
can be compensated through data augmentation from neighboring pixels, whereas true anomalies are difficult to recover
due to spectral differences. This further widens the proportion gap between minority-class backgrounds and anomaly
samples.

After identification, we amplify minority-class samples
through a controlled augmentation strategy. The sparse cluster
samples are expanded m-fold, where m is a hyperparameter
controlling the augmentation multiple. To enhance feature

introduce Gaussian noise following N (0, 1 × 10−7 ) to each

expanding the feature space representation of minority-class

This balanced augmentation approach enables the model to
develop more discriminative representations for both majority
and minority background classes, significantly enhancing its
ability to distinguish between anomalies and complex background variations.
B. Hybrid Self-Supervised Network
We propose a hybrid self-supervised network combining
AEs and GANs to enhance the ability of HAD networks to
characterize minority background samples through a multistage feature optimization mechanism. The network employs
a cascaded structure with two AEs and a GAN. After initial
feature compression and reconstruction by the first-stage AE,
the network performs adversarial optimization via the GAN,
followed by final reconstruction through the second-stage AE,
forming a self-supervised closed loop. This hybrid architecture offers dual advantages: it prevents pattern collapse in
GAN training through the cascaded reconstruction constraints
of the AEs, while leveraging the adversarial mechanism to
focus model learning on subtle spectral differences between
minority-class backgrounds and anomalies, addressing a key
limitation of pure AE approaches in sparse sample learning. Additionally, we introduce a joint dictionary structure
that ensures semantic consistency during feature refinement
through self-consistent constraints. The network comprises
three complementary components: a feature encoding module,
an adversarial generation module, and a feature refinement
module.
In the encoding phase, the first-stage autoencoder Ae, consisting of an encoder En and a decoder De, compresses and
reconstructs the input pixel x to generate the potential feature
z and the reconstructed sample x0 . In the generation phase, the
generator Ge, which has the same structure as the En, extracts
the refinement feature z0 from x0 . Meanwhile, the discriminator
Di distinguishes between the original feature z and the refined
feature z0 through adversarial training. During the refinement
stage, the second-stage autoencoder Ae0 , consisting of a generator Ge and a decoder De0 , reconstructs z0 into x00 , establishing
a cascading optimization path with the first-stage AE. To
enhance the network’s capacity for modeling complex highdimensional data and extracting nonlinear features, we replace
traditional linear layers in the AEs with our KAN-based
multipath collaborative module, detailed in Section III-C.
Notably, a joint dictionary structure is introduced to map
potential spatial features, ensuring L2 distance minimization between dictionary-reconstructed features zre and z0 .

C. KAN-Based Multipath Collaborative Module
Within the hybrid self-supervised network, we design a
KAN-based multipath collaborative module to address the
inherent complexity of hyperspectral data characterized by
high dimensionality and nonlinear spectral–spatial correlations. This module replaces conventional linear layers in
the AEs, substantially enhancing network flexibility and
adaptability. Our design integrates three complementary mechanisms: spline interpolation for localized feature precision,
RBF mapping for nonlinear feature completion [42], and linear
transformations for global structural coherence. This multipath
architecture establishes dynamic collaboration among specialized pathways, enabling joint optimization of local sensitivity
and global consistency.
The module simultaneously activates three independent
computational paths to process input data after initialization.
The basic linear transformation path constructs global feature
baselines through parameterized weight matrices and SiLU
nonlinear activation. The B-spline basis function path performs
local adaptive partitioning of input space using differentiable spline interpolation. By imposing continuity constraints
on piecewise polynomial functions at preset grid nodes, it
constructs a flexible feature mapping for locally complex highdimensional data

IV. E XPERIMENTAL R ESULTS AND A NALYSIS
This section presents a comprehensive evaluation of our
proposed MiKA-HAD framework through four systematic
investigations. All experimental data are from our own
experiments. First, comparative experiments are conducted
across several publicly available datasets with diverse backgrounds and anomaly characteristics. Subsequently, parametric
sensitivity analysis examines the robustness of key hyperparameters. Then, rigorous ablation studies are implemented.
Finally, anti-interference experiments verify the framework’s
robustness against varying noise levels. The experiment is
implemented using Python 3.9 and MATLAB R2018b on
an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. The experiment used
the Adam optimizer with 20 epochs, the AE learning rates
of 0.001, the GAN learning rate of 0.0001, the dictionary
collaboration degree of 0.5, and noise added to the augmented
pixels as random values obeying N(0, 1e − 7). The dimension
settings for each layer of the GAN and the two AE groups were
the same, namely 40 and 80. The number of grid divisions
for the B-spline is 5, and the degree of the spline is 3.
The number of grid divisions for the RBF is 5, the degree
is 2, and the scaling factor is 2. The PCA dimension was
preset to 120, but due to the insufficient number of bands in
the U-HAD dataset, the PCA dimension was set to 50 for

U-HAD. All experiments were conducted with ten repeated
trials to ensure reliability, with reported results representing
average outcomes. The experimental analysis incorporated
four principal indicators for evaluating anomaly detection
performance: receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves,
their corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values, and
the signal-to-noise probability ratio (SNPR). ROC provides
a threshold-independent performance analysis by plotting the
relationship between the probability of detection (PD) and the
false alarm rate (FAR). Higher PD with a lower FAR indicates
superior performance. AUC quantifies the ROC’s overall discriminative power. Larger AUC values signify better detection
capability, particularly useful when ROC curves intersect. The
SNPR measures detector confidence in identifying genuine
targets amidst interference by comparing the probability of
correct detection to the probability of false alarms. A higher
SNPR indicates superior separation between signal and noise
distributions, implying greater reliability in discerning targets
from clutter. This metric emphasizes inherent probabilistic
separability over absolute energy thresholds, complementing
tools like ROC analysis, which rely on threshold-dependent
outcomes. Furthermore, to assess computational efficiency, the
running times of all methods were recorded across all datasets,
with centisecond precision.

D. Parameter Analysis
In this study, we conducted a detailed sensitivity analysis on
the key hyperparameters involved in our MiKA-HAD across

all datasets. First, we investigated the impact of the min sample
and min cluster size parameters in the HDBSCAN density
clustering algorithm on network performance. As shown in
Fig. 8, when the min sample and min cluster size are set
to 2 and 5, respectively, the clustering algorithm optimally
separates the majority and minority background samples, thus
providing a clear distinction boundary for subsequent anomaly
detection. This parameter combination effectively balances
the heterogeneous distribution characteristics of background
samples: the smaller minimum cluster size preserves more
spectrally discrete minority background samples, while the
moderate minimum cluster size prevents over-segmentation
caused by excessively low-density thresholds. Furthermore, we
analyzed the critical parameters in the process of minority
sample augmentation—augmentation multiple and augmentation ratio. As depicted in Fig. 8, the network’s detection
performance is optimal when the augmentation multiple is
8 and the augmentation ratio is 10%. This finding reveals
a critical insight: increasing the absolute number of minority samples to a specific threshold creates sufficient feature
space density to influence model training, effectively shifting
decision boundaries to better differentiate between minority
backgrounds and anomalies. However, excessive augmentation
beyond this optimal point provides diminishing returns and
may introduce noise that degrades performance.
In addition, changes in the augmentation multiple have a
more significant impact on the results than the other three
hyperparameters, which is consistent with our expectations:
changes in the other three hyperparameters lead to insufficient
feature learning for certain samples, such as those not correctly
clustered by HDBSCAN or not augmented by the sample
augmentation method, while changes in the augmentation
multiple directly affect the proportion of different types of
background samples. This impact no longer pertains to only
certain categories of background samples but affects all background samples. Therefore, maintaining a balanced quantity
of different types of background samples is more important.
This conclusion further validates our initial hypothesis that
rebalancing the model’s feature space can effectively improve
detection performance.

V. C ONCLUSION
This article proposes MiKA-HAD, a minority-augmented
hybrid self-supervised framework based on KAN for HAD.
By identifying minority-class background samples through
density-based clustering and implementing augmentation
strategies, our method explicitly guides feature learning to
address the challenge of distinguishing minority-class backgrounds from anomalous targets in heterogeneous scenes.
To tackle the challenges posed by high-dimensional data
complexity and nonlinear feature extraction difficulties, we
integrate AE, GAN, and a KAN-based multipath collaborative
module to establish a self-supervised model, thereby enhancing the network’s adaptability and robustness when processing
complex high-dimensional data. Experimental results demonstrate that despite limitations in computational efficiency and
scalability, our method achieves marked effectiveness across
multiple datasets, demonstrating enhanced performance and
robustness under various noise conditions. In future work, we
will focus on optimizing resource consumption and enhancing scalability. We anticipate that this research will provide
valuable insights for future explorations in HAD and related
fields.
