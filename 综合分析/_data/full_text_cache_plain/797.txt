SatFedGuard: Semi-Supervised Federated
Contrastive Learning With RL-Assisted
Bidirectional Distillation for Anomaly Traffic
Detection in Satellite Networks
Yu Gu , Le Zhang , Yunyi Zhang, and Ye Du , Senior Member, IEEE
Abstract—Federated learning-based intrusion detection methods for satellite networks enable model training without sharing
local data, thereby ensuring network security while significantly
reducing communication overhead. However, due to the difficulty
of obtaining large-scale high-quality labeled data in satellite
environments, a key challenge lies in how to train intrusion
detection models using abundant unlabeled traffic data. We
propose SatFedGuard, a semi-supervised federated contrastive
learning approach for anomaly traffic detection in satellite
networks. SatFedGuard effectively integrates unlabeled in-orbit
data with labeled data from ground stations for model training.
First, it models the unlabeled satellite traffic data using a
contrastive learning framework. To address the challenge of nonIID data distribution, an attention-based dual-path aggregation
strategy is designed to generate personalized models for each
satellite by leveraging model similarities. Then, a bidirectional
multi-granularity distillation method between larger and smaller
models is implemented, where reinforcement learning is employed
to optimize the weights of different loss terms dynamically.
Experiments on two satellite network traffic datasets under nonIID settings demonstrate that the proposed method significantly
improves anomaly detection performance while reducing dependence on in-orbit labeled data, achieving F1-Scores of 93.38%
(↑ 11.63%) and 99.80% (↑ 8.72%), respectively.
Index Terms—Federated learning, satellite network, intrusion
detection, semi-supervised learning, edge-cloud collaboration.

With the steady improvement of onboard computing capabilities and the strong demand for space-native intelligence,
satellite-based federated learning has become an effective
solution to this problem [23]. Federated learning is particularly
necessary for satellite anomaly detection because it enables
satellites to collaboratively build a globally optimal model
without exchanging local data, thereby inherently addressing
bandwidth bottlenecks and privacy concerns. On this basis,
each satellite node only needs to periodically upload model
parameters or gradient information, thus avoiding the high
communication overhead of raw data transmission over downlink channels and alleviating system bottlenecks caused by
limited bandwidth and high latency [24]. Moreover, its decentralized nature provides essential fault tolerance; even if certain
nodes experience temporary dropouts due to intermittent link
interruptions or bursty conditions, the global training process
can remain stable [25], which is a decisive advantage over
centralized paradigms in volatile space environments.
However, existing satellite-based federated learning
approaches remain limited in effectively addressing two
major challenges: insufficient data annotation and data
heterogeneity. First, due to the difficulty of obtaining
large-scale labeled data in orbit and the limited per-client
data availability on individual LEO satellites [26], traditional
supervised learning is difficult to implement effectively. While
unsupervised learning relies solely on intrinsic features, it
lacks clear optimization direction and performs unstably
in complex environments [27], [28], [29]. Second, satellite
networks are characterized by Non-IID data distribution. Due
to varying orbital positions and geographic coverage, traffic
patterns across different satellites exhibit significant diversity.
A single global model aggregated via traditional Federated
Averaging (FedAvg) often fails to adapt to these diverse local
distributions, leading to poor robustness. Therefore, how
to fully leverage the federated learning paradigm in space
environments where labeled data is extremely scarce and data
distribution is heterogeneous remains a key challenge.
To address the above problems, this paper proposes SatFedGuard, an anomaly traffic detection method for satellite
network scenarios. It is based on semi-supervised federated
contrastive learning, attention-based personalized aggregation,
and reinforcement learning-assisted transfer learning mechanisms. It is capable of fully exploiting the rich information
of unlabeled data collected in orbit, while also using a small
amount of labeled samples gathered and annotated by ground
stations to guide and optimize the model. Specifically, to tackle
the Non-IID challenge, we design a dual-path aggregation
strategy to generate personalized models for each satellite.
Furthermore, to enhance knowledge transfer efficiency, we
employ a reinforcement learning-assisted bidirectional distillation framework. The proposed method is suitable for model
training in onboard anomaly traffic detection scenarios, where
satellites lack local labeled data, while ground stations are able
to obtain and annotate a small amount of historical data. The
goal of this method is to make efficient use of limited groundlabeled resources to achieve efficient and robust detection of
anomalous traffic on satellites. The main contributions of this
paper are as follows:

A. Intrusion Detection in Satellite Networks
Intrusion detection in satellite networks has increasingly
adopted intelligent methods such as machine learning and deep
learning in recent years, in order to address the network’s complex characteristics, including high dynamism, high latency,
and limited resources. Pacheco et al. [30] proposed a hierarchical classification framework that applies machine learning and
deep learning techniques to classify heterogeneous Internet
traffic—such as encrypted, unencrypted, and tunneled data—in
satellite communications, thereby improving QoS. Azar et al.
[31] introduced SAT-IDSs, which leverage a random forestbased Sequential Forward Selection (RF-SFS) method to
identify key features, and combine them with models such
as Random Forest, Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Long

Short-Term Memory (LSTM), and Gated Recurrent Unit
(GRU) to enhance the accuracy and efficiency of malicious traffic detection in both satellite and ground networks.
Driouch et al. [32] proposed CANSat-IDS, an adaptive distributed intrusion detection system that integrates ANN and
K-SVM. Onboard, it performs real-time detection of DoS
and fuzzing attacks based on timestamps and CAN-IDs; on
the ground, it analyzes data fields to detect replay attacks.
The distributed architecture of “real-time detection–offline
training–firmware update” balances detection performance
with the resource limitations of satellites. Xu et al. [33]
addressed the challenge of acquiring labeled traffic data in
SAGIN by enhancing the HAST-NAD network model and
introducing a skip connection mechanism to transition from
supervised to semi-supervised learning. They also built a
horizontal federated learning architecture centered on ground
gateways with high-orbit satellites as participants, ensuring
data security while conserving bandwidth. He et al. [34]
utilized federated learning to collaboratively train a differentially private conditional diffusion model, which generates data
representing the global traffic distribution. Each node then
combines this synthetic data with local data to train intrusion detection models, enabling online detection of network
intrusions.
Although the above approaches have made notable progress
in improving the accuracy of intrusion detection in satellite
networks, they still face two major limitations. First, centralized learning methods [30], [31], [32] overlook the substantial
communication overhead caused by the downlink transmission
of raw data—an issue particularly significant in large-scale
LEO satellite environments. Second, although some studies
[33], [34] have adopted distributed learning architectures and
advanced related technologies, the high cost of in-orbit annotation for satellite data poses challenges to their widespread
application.
B. Intrusion Detection Based on Semi-Supervised Learning
In the field of intrusion detection, semi-supervised learning
is gradually becoming a mainstream solution to address the
scarcity of labeled data. Such methods aim to efficiently
utilize massive amounts of unlabeled data by leveraging
semi-supervised learning techniques. Jiang et al. [35] proposed an incremental dynamic weighted semi-supervised
learning approach that integrates a block-based strategy
with fuzzy rule-based classification, enabling effective model
updates using limited labeled samples and abundant unlabeled
data. Dang et al. [36] reformulated unsupervised anomaly
detection as a proxy supervised task by projecting normal samples into a Gaussian latent space and generating
pseudo-anomalies, achieving efficient detection without prior
anomaly knowledge. Zhao et al. [37] further enhanced semisupervised detection by combining collaborative interpolation
with pseudo-labeling, where specialized traffic data augmentation and interpolation mechanisms were employed to
refine decision boundaries under extremely scarce labeling
conditions. However, these methods are generally designed
for centralized environments, while federated semi-supervised
learning is better suited to distributed settings where data are

decentralized. Zhao et al. [38] proposed SSFL, an intrusion
detection method based on semi-supervised federated learning
and knowledge distillation. In this approach, clients first train
classifiers using local labeled data, then train discriminators
to filter prediction results. Hard labels are uploaded after
screening, and the server determines global hard labels through
a voting mechanism. Clients perform distillation training using
global hard labels, thereby reducing communication overhead
while improving detection performance. Wang et al. [39]
proposed a federated semi-supervised learning-based network
traffic classification method. This approach labels traffic on
edge devices using DPI and DNS, and utilizes a convolutional autoencoder-based semi-supervised classification model
composed of an encoder, decoder, and classifier. Through alternating training, the model extracts high-dimensional features
from unlabeled samples and performs classification on labeled
samples, reducing reliance on labeled data. The global model
is updated via federated learning to enhance traffic classification performance while preserving data privacy. Aouedi et al.
[40] proposed a federated semi-supervised learning approach
for attack detection in industrial IoT. An autoencoder is
used on local devices to learn low-dimensional features
from unlabeled data, and after aggregating the global model
through federated learning, a fully connected layer is added
in the cloud to perform supervised learning using public
labeled data. Li and Li et al. [41] introduced a security
threat detection framework named FedUPS based on semisupervised federated learning. It uses CNNs for feature
extraction and applies uncertainty-aware pseudo-label selection to generate reliable pseudo-labels for unlabeled data. A
dynamic weighted aggregation method is designed to optimize model updates, addressing the issues of data privacy
and unlabeled data utilization in distributed power control
systems.
The above methods demonstrate the great potential of
federated semi-supervised learning in the field of intrusion
detection, but they still face unique challenges in the context of
satellite networks. Although these methods reduce dependence
on labeled data, some [35], [36], [37] rely on centralized
environments and are difficult to adapt to satellite scenarios,
while others [38], [39], [41] still require a certain amount of
in-orbit labeled data, which is difficult to obtain in satellite
scenarios. Moreover, current solutions [38], [39], [40] generally adopt a homogeneous model structure between satellites
and ground stations—i.e., both sides use lightweight models
to match the limited computational capacity of satellites. This
restricts the full utilization of ground-labeled data, thereby
affecting detection accuracy. To provide a clearer comparison,

III. OVERVIEW OF THE S YSTEM M ODEL
The satellite network illustrated in Figure 1 considers a
generic Space-Air-Ground Integrated Network (SAGIN) architecture. This hierarchical design integrates LEO satellites,
geostationary Earth orbit (GEO) satellites, and ground nodes
to leverage their complementary strengths—combining GEO’s

stable coverage with LEO’s high-speed access. Satellites operate periodically along predetermined orbits, providing dynamic
and seamless coverage of ground nodes [42]. Satellites connect
to ground nodes via satellite-to-ground links, while intersatellite communication is enabled through inter-satellite links
[43]. Due to their unique orbital characteristics, GEO satellites
have ample energy supply and longer design lifespans, allowing them to integrate more advanced and complex computing
and communication capabilities [44]. In this work, GEO
satellites are responsible for data aggregation, in-orbit model
training, and communication with ground stations for uploading and downloading models. LEO satellites, characterized
by low latency and high bandwidth, have limited onboard
resources. In this framework, they are mainly responsible
for collecting and preprocessing traffic data, which is then
transmitted to the GEO satellites. The ground nodes, consisting
of ground gateway stations, routers, and user terminals, are
equipped with ample computing resources and storage capabilities. They transmit data via terrestrial networks, receive
models uploaded by GEO satellites, and send the updated
global model back to GEO. Ground nodes are also responsible
for collecting and labeling a small portion of satellite data and
deploying high-performance larger models.

The threat model in this paper considers a range of adversarial attacks targeting the space segment. Satellite systems
are capable of communicating not only with ground stations
but also with various other devices, such as user terminals
and routers, enabling direct data exchange. We assume that
the adversary possesses advanced penetration capabilities,
allowing them to control and exploit a large number of groundbased network routers and user terminal devices. Leveraging
these compromised devices, adversaries can directly target the
space segment—such as in-orbit satellites—through various
attack vectors, including malicious command injection, signal
spoofing, and denial-of-service (DoS) attacks. Additionally, by
compromising critical ground infrastructure (e.g., ground control stations), they can perform more covert and sophisticated
indirect attacks. Such attacks can result in precise disruption of
satellite nodes, theft of sensitive data, or even functional failure
and permanent paralysis, posing a severe threat to the stability
and security of the entire space system. Therefore, to effectively counter the growing cyber threats in satellite networks,
there is an urgent need to develop a robust intrusion detection
method specifically designed for satellite environments. By
deploying this method across various nodes—especially those
in the space segment—it becomes possible to achieve accurate
detection of anomalous traffic in satellite networks.
IV. M ETHODS
Traditional anomaly traffic detection methods in satellite
networks typically rely on collecting onboard traffic data and
transmitting it via the downlink to ground gateway stations.
After model training based on historical data on the ground,
the trained model is then deployed back to the satellites.
However, with the development of large-scale low Earth orbit
(LEO) satellite networks and the surge in network traffic,
the reliance on downlink resources in traditional schemes
has increasingly intensified. To reduce the consumption of
precious downlink bandwidth, the concept of space-native
intelligence has gradually emerged, promoting the direct use
of local data onboard satellites for training and updating AI
models.
To address challenges such as limited computing resources
and difficulties in data labeling during in-orbit training, this
paper proposes a satellite–ground co-evolutionary approach
for anomaly traffic detection. This approach integrates federated semi-supervised contrastive learning with reinforcement
learning-assisted bidirectional knowledge distillation strategy,
aiming to build an efficient, intelligent anomaly detection
model with in-orbit adaptive capabilities, thereby enhancing
in-orbit detection performance of satellite networks.

The operational logic of SatFedGuard is realized through
four synergistic functional modules. At the satellite tier, the
system employs a self-supervised feature extraction module
based on the Barlow Twins algorithm, enabling satellites
to learn robust latent representations from local unlabeled
traffic without manual annotations. Upon transmission to the
ground, an attention-based dual-path aggregation strategy is
utilized to concurrently generate a global model and dedicated
personalized models by calculating inter-model similarities.
Subsequently, a multi-granularity bidirectional knowledge
distillation framework is established to facilitate efficient
knowledge flow. By aligning knowledge at distinct feature
levels, forward distillation integrates general knowledge into
the server model, while backward distillation transfers refined
domain knowledge back to the personalized models. Finally, to
enhance the knowledge transfer efficiency during the distillation process, a reinforcement learning agent based on the PPO
algorithm is introduced. This agent autonomously perceives
the training state and dynamically adjusts the importance
weights of the aforementioned multi-granularity distillation
losses, thereby ensuring the stability and efficiency of the
system evolution.

as backdoor attacks, botnets, web attacks, and four types of
DDoS attacks. In this work, the SAT20 dataset is selected as

G. Robustness and Security Analysis
1) Resilience to Intermittent Connectivity: To evaluate the
capability of the proposed framework in handling topological
instability and link failures within LEO satellite networks,
we designed a simulation experiment specifically targeting intermittent connectivity. We model the availability of
satellite-ground or inter-satellite links as a stochastic process.

Specifically, we introduce an upload failure probability p, also
referred to as the dropout rate, to represent the probability
that a satellite node fails to successfully upload its local
model updates during the current communication round due to
factors such as physical blockage, network congestion, or link
interruptions. In the experiment, we established three distinct
scenarios: an ideal communication environment (p = 0%), a
moderate failure scenario (p = 30%), and a severe failure
scenario (p = 50%). The experiment involved continuous
observation over 50 communication rounds on both the STIN
and STI datasets to record variations in the model’s F1-score.
As illustrated in Fig. 10, the proposed framework exhibits
exceptional robustness under intermittent connectivity. Under
ideal communication conditions, the model converges rapidly.
In contrast, in intermittent connectivity scenarios, the model
demonstrates a noticeable lag in convergence due to the gradient sparsity inherent in single-round aggregation. Simultaneously, the stochastic nature of node participation introduces
additional variance, intensifying curve fluctuations. Nevertheless, even under extremely harsh communication conditions
with an upload failure probability as high as 50% —where half
of the satellite nodes fail to upload updates in each round—the
model achieves stable convergence without suffering from
performance collapse. Specifically, on the STIN dataset, the
model demonstrates superior robustness, maintaining a final
F1-score of 90.7%, which represents a decline of only 2.35%
compared to the ideal state. Similarly, on the STI dataset,
the performance gap is merely 2.68%. These experimental
results fully demonstrate the system’s ability to effectively
cope with the highly dynamic topological changes common
in LEO mega-constellations.
This superior robustness is primarily attributed to the proposed dual-path aggregation architecture. First, in the global
aggregation path, the employed FedAvg algorithm possesses
inherent statistical fault tolerance; even when a subset of nodes
is missing, it maintains an unbiased estimate of the global
model direction by aggregating gradients from the remaining
nodes, thereby ensuring fundamental convergence stability.
Second, and more critically, the architecture introduces a
high-capacity Ground Station Model functioning as a teacher.
Owing to its larger parameter scale and stronger feature
extraction capabilities, the ground station model can extract
high-level invariant features from complex raw data. Through
the knowledge distillation mechanism, this essential knowledge is transferred to the lightweight satellite models. This

On the STI dataset, the advantage is equally significant: at
 = 0.30, SatFedGuard retains a high F1-Score of 77.5%,
distinctly outperforming the 73.2% of [40] and the 28.2%

displays the most gradual declining trend, indicating the lowest
sensitivity to attacks.

of SatFedGuard in adversarial environments. This defensive
capability is a testament to the combined effects of contrastive
learning and knowledge distillation. First, the Barlow Twins
contrastive learning algorithm employed by satellite nodes
minimizes the cross-correlation of feature components, compelling the model to learn the intrinsic structural essence of

VI. C ONCLUSION
This paper primarily addresses the issue of anomalous traffic
detection in satellite networks when on-board labeled data is
scarce. We propose SatFedGuard, a semi-supervised federated
contrastive learning method for satellite network anomalous traffic detection that combines reinforcement learningassisted personalized multi-grained bidirectional distillation.
This approach enhances model performance by performing
contrastive learning at each satellite node, while the ground
station executes dual-path aggregation and supervised learning alongside reinforcement learning-assisted multi-grained
bidirectional distillation between larger and smaller models. Experimental results demonstrate that, even with limited
on-board labeled data, our method maximizes the utilization
of available annotations, reducing reliance on data labels while
improving on-board anomalous traffic detection performance.
The F1-scores on the two datasets were boosted to 93.38%
and 99.80%, respectively. In the future, we plan to focus on
two aspects: first, deeply researching the dynamic topology
of satellites and exploring the impact of dynamic aggregation
mechanisms based on topological changes on model performance; second, we will focus on integrated space–air–ground
networks and investigate collaborative learning mechanisms
among cross-domain heterogeneous nodes to address the
challenges posed by high heterogeneity in computational capabilities, communication conditions, and data distributions.
