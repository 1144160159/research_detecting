Abstract—Network intrusion detection systems (NIDSs) face
critical challenges from continuously evolving cyber-attacks.
Traditional machine learning methods, while requiring extensive
labeled training data, still often fail against unknown and outof-distribution (OOD) attacks. Furthermore, new sophisticated
adversaries are exploiting the detection blind spots inherent
in traditional feature representation approaches that do not
provide adequate comprehensive traffic analysis. In this paper, we
propose MDCG-IDS, an NIDS framework that introduces multidimensional cross-granularity (MDCG) feature representation for
open-set detection, in which network traffic is analyzed thoroughly across three complementary dimensions (traffic statistics,
temporal, spatial), each at multiple granularity levels. These
dimensions and granularities jointly capture the structures of
sophisticated attacks that may be invisible from single analytical
perspectives. We design a tensor structure that provides a unified
encoding for the MDCG features while supporting the use of
optimal transport theory to measure the distance between benign
traffic and known or unknown attacks. MDCG-IDS uses a
semi-supervised learning model that is trained exclusively on
benign traffic and validated on a small set of labeled data,
significantly reducing the effort of data labeling. Experiments
on various datasets achieve AUC-ROC scores of more than
0.948, exceeding the best competing state-of-the-art methods by
up to 7%. Regarding the amount of labeled validating data,
MDCG-IDS obtains an AUC-ROC score of over 0.94 with only
3% of entire validating samples, outperforming the baseline
models.
Index Terms—Network intrusion detection, open-set intrusion
detection, semi-supervised learning, optimal transport, multigranularity analysis.

which possible attacks remain unbounded and are constantly
expanding. Conventional supervised NIDS approaches suffer
from critical limitations. First, they require extensive labeled
datasets containing representative samples from all attack

example, an Advanced Persistent Threat (APT) may appear
benign at the packet level while exhibiting suspicious crossflow patterns at the session level and anomalous protocol
sequences in the temporal dimension. On the other hand,
multi-granularity feature analysis has shown its advantages
over single-granularity approaches in [15] (multi-instance
learning that combines bag-level and instance-level granularities), [16] (host-level intrusion detection that combines
system call-level and behavior-level granularities), and [17]

benign and known/unknown attacks through three complementary perspectives: (i) the traffic dimension, which captures
numerical patterns across packet, flow, and session-levels;
(ii) the temporal dimension, which analyzes symbolic feature
sequences across multiple time-scales; and (iii) the spatial
dimension, which examines symbolic attributes across session
and host-level granularities. This comprehensive decomposition enables the analysis of attacks on different statistical,
temporal, and spatial scales. To encode these multi-perspective
features, we introduce a unified MDCG tensor structure
that enables vectorized computations across granularity levels
simultaneously. This representation preserves the relationships
across dimensions and simultaneously supports efficient batch
processing and parallel computation.
We use optimal transport [18] to quantify the distributional
deviations between attack samples and benign traffic. Given
that there exist several studies employing optimal transport for
detecting outlier/anomalous data in other research fields (e.g.,
image processing, audio processing), MDCG-IDS is, to the
best of our knowledge, the first application of optimal transport
in network intrusion detection. This framework allows us to
compare probability distributions that capture both the magnitude and geometric structure of the multi-dimensional shifts
between traffic samples. For example, when benign traffic
and an attack have similar average packet sizes, traditional
statistical measures (mean, variance) might consider them
equivalent, whereas optimal transport can reveal the difference
in how the probability mass is distributed across packet sizes.
The design of MDCG tensor structure also allows optimal
transport to quantify such difference for each individual granularity level, then combine features across dimensions and
granularities. Distance measurement is performed using the
Wasserstein metric through Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithms [19]
to reduce complexity and enable real-time intrusion detection.
This framework accommodates both numerical and symbolic
features through specialized formulations: sliced Wasserstein
distances for numerical features and Gromov-Wasserstein distances for symbolic features.
The main contributions of this paper are summarized as
follows:
• We model a novel multi-dimensional cross-granularity
framework that decomposes network traffic into three
complementary analytical perspectives and multiple granularity levels, enabling comprehensive analysis and
comparison between benign and known/unknown/OOD
attacks. These dimensions are unified through tensor representations that support vectorized computations across
heterogeneous feature types.
• To the authors’ knowledge, this study is the first
application of optimal transport theory to network intrusion detection, providing a mathematical foundation
for cross-granularity anomaly scoring through Wasserstein distances. We define specialized formulations of
sliced Wasserstein distances for numerical features and
Gromov-Wasserstein distances for symbolic features.

II. R ELATED W ORK
This section presents related work across three key areas:
closed-set, open-set, and multi-granular intrusion detection.
A. Closed-Set Intrusion Detection
Early intrusion detection systems relied on rule-based
signatures and statistical anomaly detection, while modern
approaches leverage deep learning architectures based on
popular frameworks such as Convolutional Neural Networks
(CNN) [20], Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) [21], [22],
Graph Neural Networks (GNN) [23], [24], [25], autoencoders
[26], and Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN) [27]. The
study [22] proposed to combine attention mechanisms, Long
Short-Term Memory (LSTM), and Gated Recurrent Unit
(GRU) for early flow classification in NIDSs (LS-GRU),
leveraging the sequential nature of packets and allowing
prompt attack detection without requiring flow termination.
The authors in [24] proposed TCG-IDS, a self-supervised
learning model designed based on a temporal contrastive
GNN. This model integrates temporal, asymmetric, and
masked contrasting strategies to learn the network by masking
some data during training. Wei et al. [26] proposed AHDM,
an autoencoder-based hybrid detection model with a dualclassifier scheme. In AHDM, autoencoder-enhanced features
target small-sample malicious traffic, while a complementary
classifier on raw features addresses normal and large-sample
traffic. This methodology translates into superior performance
on standard benchmarks, notably improving small-sample and
DDoS detection over advanced baselines (e.g., Deep Neural
Networks). These closed-set approaches do not provide the
capability to reject new attack categories that are not seen during training. Instead, they force classification into predefined
categories, even for novel attacks.
B. Open-Set Intrusion Detection
Traditional approaches for open-set NIDS include Oneclass Support Vector Machine (OSVM) [28] and Isolation
Forest [29]. Recent deep learning advances have introduced
new methodologies based on architectures such as Isolation
Trees [30], CNN [31], auto-encoders/variational auto-encoders
[32], [33], and extreme value theory [32], [33]. The study in

[30] introduced FOSS, a fine-grained unknown class detection
model against variable attack spectra, incorporating adaptive
contamination modeling. FOSS leverages an isolation-tree
backbone with hyperplane splitting, isolation-based discrimination, and incremental updates to obtain accurate fine-grained
detection of previously unseen DDoS attacks. In [31], the
authors propose a scalable IDS for detecting unknown attacks
by combining a CNN with a nearest class mean (NCM)
classifier, jointly optimized by Fisher and maximum mean discrepancy losses. They further introduce semantic-embedding
clustering to reveal latent unknown classes and an incremental nearest cluster centroid method to assimilate them by
updating the NCM classifier. The study [32] formulates finegrained known/unknown intrusion detection as a two-stage
problem using class-conditioned autoencoders. They model
reconstruction-error tails via extreme value theory and benign
clustering to boost unknown-attack detection. Other openset approaches include MinMax auto-encoder with pseudo
extreme value [33], deep reinforcement learning with selfevolving moving targets [34], Mahalanobis distancing [35],
Dandelion Network [36], and image-based representations of
packet-level data [37].
C. Multi-Dimensional and Cross-Granularity Feature
Analysis
Recent research has leveraged multi-dimensional models
for intrusion detection by applying spatio-temporal feature
analysis. Wang et al. [11] developed an attention-weighted
spatial-temporal graph model to extract features in parallel
using a message-passing mechanism and a Bi-LSTM, then
fuse them for the final classification of intrusions. The authors
showed that a comprehensive spatial-temporal correlation
approach helps to compensate when single-dimensional extraction yields weak representations. The authors in [12] proposed
an attention-based Spatial Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (ST-GCN) to model normal link states in networks.
Extreme Value Theory (EVT) is used to identify Link Flooding
Attacks (LFA) as extreme performance degradation events.
The authors demonstrated that modeling network states as a
spatio-temporal graph is more efficient than modeling with
solely temporal (LSTM) or spatial (GCN) dimension. The
study [13] introduced an anomaly detection framework for
5G that captures anomalous patterns across multiple spatiotemporal perspectives using a Multi-Scale Fuzzy Contrastive
Learning approach. It performs anomaly detection explicitly
in the latent space by measuring the distance to normal data
prototypes. Yang et al. [14] used spatio-temporal contextaware graph embedding to learn representations of normal
behavior, from which an anomaly score is generated for
outlier detection. Similar to [11], the authors in [13] and [14]
showed that spatio-temporal approach allows to extract more
comprehensive traffic patterns; especially when combining
with multi-scale feature analysis [13].
Several studies have explored multi-granularity approaches
to capture network behavior with different feature scales.
Geng et al. [17] proposed S3WD-MG, a multi-granularity
intrusion detection algorithm based on sequential three-way
decisions. This algorithm incorporates autoencoder-based feature learning across different granularity levels. The authors
in [16] developed BR-HIDF, an anti-sparsity host intrusion

D. Summary and Comparisons
Current approaches suffer from several limitations:
(1) both multi-dimensional and cross-granularity approaches
are applied only to closed-set detection; hence, open-set
methods lack comprehensive multi-dimensional crossgranular representations that integrate traffic, temporal,
and spatial perspectives simultaneously, and (2) traditional
distance metrics typically rely on single-dimensional deviation
without capturing multi-dimensional geometric relationships
between traffic samples. In this paper, we measure that
relationship by designing a multi-dimensional distance metric
based on optimal transport theory.

We introduce a new tensor structure to encode the MDCG
features, preserving the relationships between different dimensions and granularity levels, while adapting to the computation
of optimal transport-based distances. By using vectorized
operations, the representation supports batch processing of
distance computations across multiple granularities simultaneously. These tensors can be processed with GPU acceleration
to improve scalability with respect to the number of dimensions and granularity.
1) Unified Tensor Structure: In this paper, each flat data
sample xi , i = 1, 2, . . . (i.e., either training, validating, or
testing sample) typically corresponds to a network session. We
note that training samples include only benign traffic records,
while validating samples are small quantity of labeled records,
and testing samples are labeled records used for inference.
For each sample, we define a unified tensor representation
zi ∈ RD×Gmax ×Fmax where D is the number of dimensions,
Gmax = max{|G F |, |G T |, |G S |} represents the maximum number
of granularity levels across all dimensions, and Fmax is the
maximum number of features (Fig. 3). Each tensor element
contains the features of a specific granularity level g within a
dimension D. This tensor is extracted through the dimensionspecific encoding function as zi = φ(xi ). Fig. 3 corresponds to
the examples in Table III, in which D = 3 and Gmax = 3
with unused tensor positions are zero-padded to maintain
consistency, and Fmax = 2 since no granularity in the examples
of Table III has more than 2 features.
2) Dimension-Specific Encoding Functions: The encoding
functions are designed to handle the heterogeneous features
across different dimensions while ensuring compatibility with
optimal transport computations. For the traffic dimension
with exclusively numeric features, the function φ(xi ) applies
normalization using the mean and standard deviation computed from the training data, followed by zero-padding to
achieve uniform dimensionality Fmax . Before normalization,
the granularity-specific numeric features are extracted from
the flat data sample xi using a projection operator.
Temporal and spatial dimensions contain symbolic features,
with temporal features being sequential while spatial features
are unordered. For the temporal dimension, we combine
positional embeddings with learned categorical embeddings to
maintain sequential relationships. The encoding function φ(·)
aggregates all embeddings at the granularity level g, preserving
sequential order through weighted averaging based on temporal proximity. For the spatial dimension, we employ standard
categorical embeddings without positional constraints, and the
encoding function uses uniform averaging.

To handle the discrete features, we incorporate regularization into the optimal transport formulation. The regularization
and distance computation are adapted with respect to dimension: the traffic dimension (i.e., numeric features) uses
the Sinkhorn-Wasserstein (SW) distance, while the temporal and spatial dimensions (i.e., symbolic features) employ
the Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) distance. This is because the
formulation of SW distance requires direct cost (distance)
between points, as shown in below formulas; hence it is
suitable for numeric traffic features where pairwise distances
are directly quantifiable. This distance measures how much
numeric mass must be shifted to match two feature distributions. In contrast, the formulation of GW distance relies on
the internal relational structures of two distributions rather than
requiring direct point-to-point comparison. As a result, to measure the distance between two symbolic values (e.g., HTTP
and FTP protocols), we use GW distance to compare how these
categories relate to each other through their co-occurrence
patterns. Both distances are computed using iterative algorithms: Sinkhorn–Knopp for SW, and alternating minimization
for GW. The matrix-scaling algorithm Sinkhorn–Knopp can
provide closed-form iterative updates for SW, while GW’s
quadratic structure requires alternating minimization for iterative optimization.

A. MDCG-Decomposed Optimal Transport Distance
In this paper, we define an incoming sample as either
a validating or testing sample. The distance metric quantifies the dissimilarity between an incoming sample ẑ and
the benign training distribution. We propose to use optimal
transport (OT) [18] to leverage MDCG decomposition and
Wasserstein distances. Optimal transport is a mathematical
framework for measuring the minimum cost of transforming
one probability distribution into another, where the cost reflects
how much probability mass must be moved and how far.
In the context of intrusion detection, OT quantifies how much
the distribution of an incoming traffic sample differs from
the benign reference distribution. The Wasserstein distance,
derived from OT theory, provides a principled metric for this
distributional comparison. Unlike statistical measures such as
Euclidean distance, which use the mean and variance, OT and
Wasserstein distance capture both the magnitude and geometric structure of distribution differences, aligning with MDCG
traffic analysis. For instance, two traffic samples with similar
mean packet sizes but different distributions are distinguished
by how their probability masses are spatially arranged. In the
following, we present the OT distance computation that is
decomposed into different dimensions.

D. Computational Complexity
The framework’s computational cost comprises two phases:
(1) MDCG tensor construction operates in O(D×Gmax × Fmax )
per sample, which is linear in the input size; (2) OT distance
computation using Sinkhorn-Knopp approximation scales as
O(n2 × k × D × Gmax ), where n is the training set size, k
is the number of Sinkhorn iterations, and D × Gmax accounts
for parallel computation across all dimension-granularity pairs.
The Sinkhorn-Knopp approximation has reduced computational complexity of exact optimal transport from O(n3 log n)
[19] to O(n2 × k). In practice n represents a manageable
reference set (e.g., 103 − 104 benign samples), and k typically
ranges from 10 to 20.

1) Datasets: The proposed model is evaluated on four
datasets: CIC-IDS-2017 (CIC17) [5], UNSW-NB15 (NB15)
[39], TON-IoT (TON) [40], and 5GAD [41]. These datasets
are chosen to cover both generic conventional enterprise

MDCG-IDS by capturing the temporal patterns of network
traffic. This dataset contains specific attacks in 5G, such as
GetUserData, GetAllNFs, and FakeAMFInsert (Table IV). The
CIC17 dataset provides divert attack coverage, such as DDoS,
several types of DoS, and Web attacks; while NB15 comprises
9 modern network attacks including DoS, Reconnaissance, and
Worms. TON targets IoT environments with heterogeneous
telemetry data from smart devices and sensors, containing
attacks such as dos, injection, and backdoor.

a 30-second active timeout. CIC17 labels are re-assigned

more than 60 seconds of inactivity. The experiments use

4) Evaluation Metrics: We evaluate the MDCG-IDS
framework using both threshold-independent and thresholddependent metrics. The primary metric is AUROC, which
measures the model’s ability to discriminate between normal
and anomalous traffic across all possible decision thresholds,
making it robust to the class imbalance issue. At the optimal
operating points that are determined by Youden’s index, we
report the threshold-dependent metrics, including the False
Positive Rate (FPR) and F1-score. The FPR is particularly
critical because it quantifies benign traffic misclassified as
attacks, which directly impacts operational feasibility. The
experimental results reported in the following sections are
computed over the entire testing set in Table IV (i.e., combining ID, OOD-1, and OOD-2 samples), with the exception
of the attack-specific performance results which provide a percategory breakdown. All experimental results are reported as
mean ± standard deviation across 5 independent runs with
different random seeds.
5) Baselines: MDCG-IDS is compared against various
baselines, including both closed-set K-class and open-set
methods. For K-class baselines, models are trained on a
standard K-class dataset and, at inference, assign every testing
sample to one of the K classes, even if the sample comes from
an unknown category. For open-set baselines, the models are
designed to detect inputs that do not belong to the K known
classes (i.e., rejecting them as unknown/OOD). We note that
the K-class baselines are trained on the combination of the
training and validating sets (Table IV).
a) Closed-set k-class baselines: We evaluate various
closed-set baselines, including (i) traditional supervised learning methods: Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Random
Forest, and (ii) state-of-the-art models: TCG-IDS [24],
LS-GRU [22], and AHDM [26]. TCG-IDS is a temporal
contrastive graph learning approach that constructs dynamic
graphs from network flows and distinguishes attack patterns
through temporal dependencies and graph structural information. We evaluate TCG-IDS with temporal window sizes of 30
s (TCG-IDS) and 60 s (TCG-IDS-60s) to assess the impact
of temporal granularity on graph-based attack detection. The
baseline model LS-GRU combines attention mechanisms with
GRU for early network intrusion detection, enabling focused
learning on critical features in traffic sequences. To evaluate the effects of attention mechanism in LS-GRU, we
implement it with 64 and 128 hidden dimensions, named LSGRU and LS-GRU-128, respectively. The AHDM approach
is an autoencoder-based hybrid model specifically designed
for small-sample intrusion detection. This model combines
reconstruction-based anomaly detection with supervised classification components.

b) Open-set baselines: We evaluate the following traditional open-set baselines: Isolation Forest, which utilizes
ensemble-based anomaly scoring through random data partitioning, and One-Class SVM (OSVM), which learns a decision
boundary around benign samples for novelty detection. The
state-of-the-art baselines include FOSS [30], Scalable-NIDS
(S-NIDS) [31], and CVAE-EVT [32]. The model FOSS
employs adaptive isolation mechanisms and multi-scale feature
analysis to handle unknown attack patterns. We implement
FOSS with a feature dimensionality of 64, while its variant, FOSS-128, is enhanced with a dimensionality of 128.
We also evaluate FOSS-mg, a variant that equips MDCG
feature processing to validate whether MDCG decomposition enhances isolation-based OOD detection. The baseline
S-NIDS utilizes clustering and soft-boundary classification
for hybrid novelty detection. S-NIDS is implemented with
a feature dimensionality of 128, while S-NIDS-256 is
enhanced with a dimensionality of 256. Similarly to FOSS-mg,
we also implement the S-NIDS-mg variant for validating
the MDCG decomposition. The baseline CVAE-EVT combines the Conditional Variational Auto-Encoder (VAE) with
Extreme Value Theory for tail-based uncertainty modeling. We
implement CVAE-EVT with 64 and 128 latent dimensions,
named CVAE-EVT-64 and CVAE-EVT-128, respectively.
CVAE-EVT-ot is the variant that is integrated with the optimal
transport module to assess whether the Wasserstein distance
can enhance VAE-based anomaly detection.

The proposed framework achieves improved performance
with additional computational cost due to its comprehensive
MDCG feature analysis. On the one hand, Fig. 10 shows
that the framework achieves lower throughput than simpler
baselines, but remaining within a comparable range to state-ofthe-art methods (e.g., CVAE-EVT, S-NIDS), while delivering
consistently higher detection accuracy. On the other hand,
reflecting the throughput characteristics in other works such
as [24] (standard enterprise network) and [50] (IoT network),
our deployment targets edge servers or security gateways
rather than high-speed backbone links, where comprehensive analysis is prioritized over raw line-rate processing.
For resource-constrained environments, the framework targets
aggregation points such as IoT gateway, rather than individual
resource-limited endpoints. Future engineering optimizations
will include self-optimizing sampling techniques, adaptive
tensor-compute designs, and sparse tensor implementations.
B. Optimality
To mitigate the risk of local optima, we improve the
convergence consistency by two common practices: multiple
random initializations and early stopping [24], as described in
Section V-A. Although global optimality cannot be guaranteed,
(i.e., a known characteristic of gradient-based methods) the
convergence in Fig. 6 as well as the low-variance curves in
Fig. 8 and Fig. 9 demonstrate consistent performance across
5 independent runs with different initializations. Further, the
evaluations throughout the paper, particularly the cross-dataset
robustness analysis, show stable performance across four
diverse datasets with varying traffic characteristics.
C. Deployment Architecture
Similar to the deployment mechanism in [51] which
separates offline model update and online inference, the

THI AND GURUSAMY: MULTI-DIMENSIONAL CROSS-GRANULARITY OPEN-SET NETWORK INTRUSION DETECTION

MDCG-IDS’s semi-supervised paradigm is designed for a
hybrid deployment where the benign reference set and its
corresponding parameters are updated offline, while real-time
inference processes incoming traffic independently. In this
way, the computationally intensive optimization is decoupled
from the online detection which mainly relies on distance computation. Regarding the preparation of benign traffic, although
this type of data already greatly reduces labeling effort, collecting purely benign records remains a common challenge for
weakly-supervised, semi-supervised, cluster-based, and oneclass detection frameworks [52]. Given this challenge, our
framework demonstrates that it maintains robust performance
(less than 6.7% AUROC degradation) even with 15% training
set contamination (Section V-B8).
D. Feature Extraction
While most MDCG features are derived from packet headers
and flow statistics without inspecting application-layer content,
some features such as application protocol may be affected by
encryption. In such cases, features in the MDCG tensor can
be substituted with observable proxies (e.g., destination port
as an approximation of application protocol under TLS) or
zero-padded, as described in our 5GAD dataset processing.
Such substitution or zero-padding strategies apply to other
encryption mechanisms such as IPsec, which are challenges
shared by NIDS frameworks.
E. Future Works
Several extensions would enhance the framework’s practical
deployability. First, given that the current temporal granularities are fixed based on flow timeout conventions, an adaptive
granularity selection mechanism could automatically adjust
granularity levels based on network characteristics. This adaptation can optimize the detection performance while reducing
the computational overhead for different environments (e.g.,
bursty control-plane traffic vs. steady sensor streams). Second,
the integration of federated learning would create opportunities
for collaborative detection, wherein multiple organizations
enhance their intrusion detection capabilities without sharing
sensitive data. In this way, we address both privacy concerns
and the challenge of limited diverse training data in individual
organizations.
VII. C ONCLUSION
In this paper, we developed a multi-dimensional crossgranularity framework for open-set network intrusion detection. The framework can decompose network samples
into three complementary dimensions—traffic, temporal, and
spatial—each at multiple granularity levels. The integration
of optimal transport theory provides a novel foundation for
quantifying the distributional deviations between benign and
known/unknown network attacks. Our evaluations demonstrate substantial improvements in detection accuracy while
addressing practical deployment challenges. Specifically, the
framework consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods
across diverse experimental scenarios while requiring minimal
labeled validating data. The reduced dependency on labeled
traffic, combined with the training exclusively on benign samples, addresses the critical issue of data scarcity in practical

deployment. The practical applicability of the proposed framework is also validated by its reasonable processing throughput
and resource consumption.
