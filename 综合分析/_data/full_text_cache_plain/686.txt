FlowXpert: Context-Aware Flow Embedding for
Enhanced Traffic Detection in IoT Network
Chao Zha , Haolin Pan , Bing Bai , Jiangxing Wu, and Ruyun Zhang

Abstract—In the Internet of Things (IoT) environment, continuous interaction among a large number of devices generates complex
and dynamic network traffic, which poses significant challenges
to rule-based detection approaches. Machine learning (ML)-based
traffic detection technology, capable of identifying anomalous patterns and potential threats within this traffic, serves as a critical
component in ensuring network security. This study first identifies
a significant issue with widely adopted feature extraction tools
(e.g., CICFlowMeter): the extensive use of time- and length-related
features leads to high sparsity, which adversely affects model
convergence. Furthermore, existing traffic detection methods generally lack an embedding mechanism capable of efficiently and
comprehensively capturing the semantic characteristics of network
traffic. To address these challenges, we propose a novel feature
extraction tool that eliminates traditional time and length features
in favor of context-aware semantic features related to the source
host, thus improving the generalizability of the model. In addition,
we design an embedding training framework that integrates the
unsupervised DBSCAN clustering algorithm with a contrastive
learning strategy to effectively capture fine-grained semantic representations of traffic. Extensive empirical evaluations are conducted on the real-world dataset (Mawi) and two simulated datasets
(CICIDS-2017 and UNSW-NB15) to validate the proposed method
in terms of detection accuracy, robustness, and generalization.
Comparative experiments against several state-of-the-art (SOTA)
models demonstrate the superior performance of our approach.
Furthermore, we confirm its applicability and deployability in
real-time scenarios.
Index Terms—Traffic detection, sparsity, context-aware,
DBScan, contrastive learning, real-time.

network traffic detection without adequately incorporating the

network environments. As a result, truly effective and efficient
detection capabilities have yet to be fully realized. Despite
significant progress in the field, network traffic detection still
faces numerous critical challenges that warrant further in-depth
investigation and innovation.
a) Challenge 1. Limitations of Flow-Based Features in Model

packets that share the same five-tuple: source IP, destination
IP, source port, destination port, and transport-layer protocol
within a specific time window [21]. Flow features are structured
statistical attributes extracted from these packet sets and serve

Feature sparsification can hinder model convergence and restrict gradient propagation [30], [31], [32]. Deep learning relies
on backpropagation to update parameters, but sparse inputs
often cause many neurons to remain inactive (i.e., output zero),
yielding zero gradients and blocking parameter updates. These
silent neurons reduce the expressive capacity of the network,
limiting its ability to model data distributions effectively. As

scope of gradient updates, ultimately affecting overall training
performance.
b) Challenge 2. Lack of an Efficient and Comprehensive
Semantic Embedding: Most existing traffic detection methods
mainly follow two paradigms: unsupervised approaches [16],
[17], [18], such as clustering algorithms that model the underlying structure of the data, and supervised approaches [6], [21],
[22], [23] that rely on cross-entropy loss as the optimization
objective and require manually labeled data for training. These
methods can achieve satisfactory performance under ideal conditions with sufficient labeled data. However, these methods
exhibit significant limitations in capturing the complex semantic
structure of network traffic. Specifically, benign traffic does
not constitute a single semantic category but rather comprises
multiple subclasses characterized by diverse behavioral patterns.
Relying solely on coarse-grained labeling with a generic benign tag neglects the inherent semantic heterogeneity, leading
to learned representations that lack fine-grained discriminative
power. The same issue applies to anomalous traffic: different
attack types exhibit distinct behavioral signatures, and using a
unified anomalous label fails to capture their intrinsic differences. Furthermore, even when fine-grained labels are available
to better express semantic characteristics, the embedding representation must still preserve intraclass diversity to enhance
the model’s generalization capacity. Maintaining such structural
diversity not only prevents overfitting to specific feature patterns
but also improves the robustness and adaptability of the model.
Our work: We propose a novel traffic detection method named
FlowXpert to solve the above issues. Firstly, we design a novel
feature extraction scheme that constructs contextual feature
vectors for model training by associating the destination node
of a network flow with its source host node. This approach
eliminates many features of the sparse problems commonly
found in traditional methods, thus avoiding their negative impact
on model convergence. In addition, contextual characteristics
demonstrate superior generalization performance, enhancing
the practicality of the model in real-world applications. Secondly, we incorporate the unsupervised DBSCAN clustering
algorithm [33] and employ contrastive learning [34], [35] to
train the embedding space, which collectively improves the
overall performance of the model in traffic detection while also
enhancing its generalization ability and adaptability. This process can be performed efficiently on downsampled data. Finally,
we conduct experiments on the MAWI real-world dataset [36],
[37] and two commonly adopted synthetic datasets, namely
UNSW-NB15 [29] and CICIDS-2017 [27], to better approximate real-world deployment scenarios and validate the effectiveness of FlowXpert. We perform a comprehensive evaluation of
FlowXpert from multiple dimensions, including cross-tests, generalization tests, encrypted traffic identification, ablation studies,
comparative experiments, and real-time performance tests, all of
which confirm the effectiveness and progress of the method. In
addition, we rigorously control the experimental setup to avoid
potential label leakage issues often found in previous studies.

II. RELATED WORK
In this section, we provide a discussion and summary of recent
significant studies on ML-based algorithms and graph-based
methods for traffic detection tasks. These comparisons are further illustrated in Table I.
a) ML based Traffic Detection: Traditional ML algorithms
have been widely applied to network traffic detection tasks.
For example, Mirsky et al. [20] introduced an unsupervised
online network intrusion detection method, whose core component, KitNET, employs an ensemble architecture of small
autoencoders to perform reconstruction error analysis on

extracted network traffic features, thereby allowing the detection
of anomalous behaviors, as noted by Wang et al. [38] proposed a
cloud intrusion detection method based on a stacked contractive
autoencoder combined with a Support Vector Machine. Yang
et al. [21] proposed a two-stage learning approach that integrates conditional variational autoencoders with extreme value
theory to construct a hierarchical intrusion detection system.
Moreover, by incorporating the clustering of benign traffic,
the approach significantly reduces the false-positive rate. Fu
et al. [15] presented a method that uses features in the frequency
domain to capture the temporal characteristics of attack traffic
without losing contextual information and integrates clustering
algorithms to achieve intrusion detection. Furthermore, Zha
et al. [22] proposed an intrusion detection method that combines
flow-based features and payload-based features through bilinear
fusion to enhance the accuracy and robustness of network intrusion detection. Du et al. [19] proposed using long short-term
memory neural networks to treat system logs as natural language
sequences, learning the patterns of log sequences under normal
execution to detect anomalies effectively.
Traditional ML-based approaches are mostly trained on flowlevel features, and while they often achieve impressive results
on synthetic benchmark datasets, this focus on benchmark performance may overlook real-world practicalities [25]. In realistic network environments, the sparsity of flow features can
significantly hinder model convergence, ultimately rendering
such methods ineffective. Moreover, conventional ML methods typically do not incorporate contextual information, which
can lead to poor generalization and limit their applicability. In
contrast, our proposed FlowXpert leverages novel context-aware
features as model input, deliberately discarding most traditional
flow features. In addition, it integrates an unsupervised embedding training strategy to further enhance detection performance,
offering a more robust and practical solution for real-world
deployment.
b) Graph based Traffic Detection: In recent years, graphbased algorithms have increasingly been applied to traffic

detection. For example, Eshete et al. [39] constructed HTTP
interaction graphs to detect malicious static resources, and Invernizzi et al. [40] used graphs derived from plaintext traffic to
identify malicious infrastructure associated with malware. Fu
et al. [16] introduced the construction of an in-memory flow
interaction graph combined with unsupervised graph learning
to detect encrypted malicious traffic. King et al. [41] proposed
EdgeTorrent, which enables the embedding of streaming in real

Wu et al. [43] proposed a self-supervised temporal graph neural

proposed a byte-level encrypted traffic classification framework,
where traffic graphs are constructed using point-wise mutual
information (PMI). The model adopts a dual embedding structure and a cross-gated fusion mechanism to efficiently integrate
header and payload representations. It constructs sequential
temporal attributed graphs and applies temporal, asymmetric,
and masked contrastive strategies to learn robust node representations, thereby enabling the detection of unknown attacks.
Traffic detection algorithms based on graph features can
improve robustness, generalization, and overall performance.
However, constructing such graphs is often complex and may
hinder the practical deployment of these approaches. For example, when IP addresses are used as graph nodes, the memory
graph can grow rapidly with an increasing number of IPs, leading
to a sharp increase in the number of subconnected components [16]. Given the large number of IPs in real-world scenarios,
this challenge is almost unavoidable and significantly limits
the scalability of graph-based methods. In contrast, FlowXpert
adopts a more practical approach by considering only the contextual information of the source host and using unsupervised
learning to train the model. This design not only improves the
generalizability of the model but also enhances its practicality
for real-world deployment.
c) Pretraining based Traffic Detection: With the emergence of
large-scale pre-trained models in natural language processing,
pre-trained models for encrypted traffic analysis have also been
widely developed [44], [45], [46], [47], [48], [49], [50]. For
instance, Lin et al. [44] segmented traffic into directional and
temporally continuous BURSTs, encoding each as a language
model-like token sequence. They designed two self-supervised
tasks: Masked BURST Model (MBM) and Same-origin BURST
Prediction (SBP) to learn generalizable representations from
unlabeled traffic data. Wang et al. [45] proposed replacing the
Transformer with a Unidirectional Mamba to achieve lineartime modeling. They also designed a stride-based representation scheme that incorporates both header and payload information, forming the NetMamba framework, which integrates
self-supervised pre-training with fine-tuning. Zhao et al. [46]
proposed a masked autoencoder (MAE)-based traffic transformer that constructs a hierarchical flow representation matrix and employs packet-level and flow-level attention mechanisms to extract traffic features efficiently, leveraging MAE pretraining to reduce reliance on labeled data. Furthermore, Zhou
et al. [47] developed a BERT-based encoder architecture that
integrates bigram representations processed via byte pair encoding (BPE). By designing two pre-training tasks, MBM and Same

directional, and sequential patterns in network traffic. During

Pretraining methods offer the advantage of unsupervised

B. Flowvision
As discussed in Section IV-A, the widely adopted flow-based
features commonly used in existing approaches have shown
significant adverse effects on model convergence in practical
scenarios. Based on this observation, we discard the most highly
sparse flow features and propose a novel feature extraction tool
named FlowVision.1 Unlike existing tools such as CICFlowMeter [51], [52], FlowVision not only retains a subset of fundamental statistical flow features, but also places greater emphasis on
the contextual semantic information of the flows. The general
data structure of FlowVision is illustrated in Fig. 3, which uses
five tuples as keys to segment and aggregate the pcap packets
into distinct flows. Additionally, each flow node is enriched with
contextual semantic attributes related to its source IP, forming a
lightweight semantic structure that is more concise and efficient
compared to directly constructing a full graph structure.
Specifically, FlowVision first captures packets from the network environment and reads them in the form of pcap files. Then
it undergoes the following processes to gradually compute the
1 FlowVision is open-sourced at https://github.com/ids-sec-hub/FlowVision

final network traffic features, which are used as input for the
traffic detection task.
r Packet Parsing: FlowVision sequentially parses each
packet layer by layer, extracting field information from
the data link layer, network layer, and transport layer.
This information is subsequently used by the following
computation modules.
r FlowBlock Statistics: After extracting the parsing results
of each packet, FlowVision uses the five-tuple as a key
to locate an existing flow node. If the corresponding flow
node is found, the flow information is updated based on
the parsed packet data. If no such node exists, a new flow
node is initialized. During the flow information aggregation
process, FlowVision assesses whether a flow has terminated
by examining conditions such as flow timeout, the number
of FIN packets, and the presence of RST packets. Once a
flow is determined to have ended, the flow information and
its associated context are exported, and a new flow node is
created accordingly.
r IPBlock Statistics: After completing the flow information
aggregation, FlowVision further locates the associated IP
node based on the source IP. If the corresponding IP node
does not exist, a new node is created. Once the associated
IP node is identified, contextual information is aggregated
on the basis of packet data. It is important to note that
contextual information is typically accumulated within
a specified time window, the duration of which can be
customized according to the specific network environment
and application scenarios. Data that fall outside the time
window are discarded. Moreover, contextual information is
computed on all data within the window in a label-agnostic
manner, as a label-dependent manner could lead to the risk
of label leakage.
r Output Module: When a flow node times out or receives end
packets, such as FIN or RST, the current flow is completed.
Meanwhile, the statistical features are constructed and
output based on the aggregated information from the flow
node and its associated IP node.
FlowVision is primarily composed of the aforementioned
modules and is designed to support a multi-threaded architecture
that accelerates the entire feature extraction process, thereby
reducing processing latency and improving throughput. Ultimately, FlowVision extracts a total of 13 key features for model
training and intrusion detection, encompassing both flow-level
and contextual semantic information, as detailed in Table II. The
flow-level features include protocol type, flow duration, interarrival time (IAT), the number of SYN/RST/FIN packets, and
packet rate. Contextual features capture higher-level semantics,
such as the total number of source ports initiated by a given
source IP, the number of destination IPs and ports accessed, and
the rate at which the source IP establishes connections over time.

As described in Challenge 2, due to the nature of traffic
detection tasks, a single label often fails to accurately and
comprehensively represent all subtypes within a given traffic
class. In other words, traffic samples belonging to the same class
may exhibit internal structural differences, necessitating finergrained labeling to distinguish between subtypes. This objective
can be effectively achieved using unsupervised algorithms, such
as clustering methods [33], [53], [54].
Common clustering algorithms include KMeans and DBSCAN. KMeans [54] is a distance-based method that requires
the number of clusters to be specified and assigns each sample deterministically to a cluster. In contrast, DBSCAN [33]
does not require a predefined number of clusters, can identify
clusters of arbitrary shapes, and allows certain samples to remain unclustered, and these are treated as noise or outliers.
During the embedding learning process, it is inadvisable to

The MAWI dataset provides raw traffic data in PCAP format, encompassing a wide range of attack types, including

CICIDS-2017 dataset captures realistic network traffic in a
simulated environment, covering benign behavior alongside a
wide range of contemporary attacks, including DoS, DDoS,
brute-force attacks, infiltration, and web-based attacks. It also
includes detailed flow-based features, making it suitable for
evaluating intrusion detection methods.
Specifically, data from all datasets were used for model training with a 3-fold cross-test (2:1 split), along with the number of
training and test sets illustrated in Table III (numbers in brackets
within the Test column denote the count of encrypted flows). To
evaluate the generalization performance of the model, that is, its
effectiveness when applied to data collected at later time periods,
we selected data from the Mawi dataset on March 8, 15, and 22 of
2021, as well as June 10, 18, and 25 of 2023, each approximately
one week apart. In addition, we used data from the UNSW-NB15
dataset on February 17, 2015, for testing. Correspondingly, the
training data for the three experiments were taken from March
1, 2021, June 6, 2023, and January 22, 2015, respectively.
To ensure the applicability of the proposed method in realworld scenarios, both the data set and the evaluation methodology were designed to meet the following conditions.
r The MAWI data set used in our experiments was utilized
without removing any data. Random sampling was applied
during the embedding training phase, classification training
and generalization evaluation were performed on the full
dataset.

9323

r Features such as IPs and ports, which can potentially
reveal label information, were excluded from the training.
In addition, potential associated cheating behaviors were
explicitly avoided. For example, most samples in the data
set are concentrated on a limited set of IPs and ports.
r Due to the inherent class imbalance between benign and
malicious traffic, a metric such as accuracy was not utilized.
Instead, detection performance metrics were evaluated separately for benign and malicious traffic to provide a more
comprehensive and fair assessment.
c) Baselines: We selected six methods for comparative experiments to demonstrate the correctness and improvement effects
of our method. The details are as follows:
r Kitsune (K.S): Mirsky et al. [20] proposed an unsupervised
network intrusion detection method centered on KitNET,
which uses a lightweight ensemble of autoencoders to
detect anomalies through the analysis of reconstruction
errors in network traffic features.
r CVAE-EVT (C.E): Yang et al. [21] proposed a two-stage
learning method that combines conditional variational autoencoders and extreme value theory to build a hierarchical
intrusion detection system.
r HyperVision (H.V): Fu et al. [16] proposed using an inmemory flow interaction graph with unsupervised graph
learning to detect encrypted malicious traffic.
r TFE-GNN (T.G): Zhang et al. [42] proposed a byte-level
encrypted traffic classification model that builds PMIbased traffic graphs and integrates header and payload
features through dual-inclusion and cross-gated fusion.
r ETBert (E.B): Lin et al. [44] represented traffic as directional temporal BURSTs encoded into language-like
tokens, and pre-trained the model using MBM and SBP
tasks to learn generalizable representations.
r TrafficFormer (T.F): Zhou et al. [47] developed a BERTbased encoder that integrates BPE-processed bigrams. Using pre-training tasks, MBM and SODF, to capture traffic
patterns, and a fine-tuning augmentation method, RIFA,
the model achieves enhanced generalization.
All comparative methods were evaluated using the same
source data and official implementations from their GitHub
repositories. Only minor modifications were made, primarily for
compatibility purposes, such as adapting data loading interfaces.
For HyperVision and TFE-GNN, processing datasets with many
unique IP addresses led to excessive memory usage, exhausting a
256 GB DRAM server. Consequently, we adopted data sampling
to complete the experiments.
d) Metrics: We use five metrics to evaluate the performance of
FlowXpert, including three commonly used metrics in machine
learning algorithms: Precision, Recall, and F1-Score [60]. Furthermore, we include two metrics that are crucial for practical
deployment: Latency and Throughput.

critical for real-world deployment, as the feature distribution
of network traffic changes over time. Overfitted models may
suffer significant performance drops in evolving environments,
limiting their effectiveness. Given the high sensitivity and stringent reliability requirements of network security, ensuring the
accurate detection of benign traffic is essential. Even a 1% performance drop can lead to substantial misclassification and disruption of normal services. To assess generalization, we trained
models on two baseline dates, March 1, 2021 and June 3, 2023,

critical issues. Firstly, it mitigates the convergence challenges
of flow-based features by selecting only a small subset of flow
features and incorporating contextual features related to the

and improved detection capabilities. Second, by integrating
contrastive learning with the DBSCAN unsupervised clustering algorithm, FlowXpert constructs robust traffic embeddings,
further enhancing detection performance.

Although the development of DL has brought significant
opportunities for traffic detection tasks, such as improving generalization to unknown attacks and improving adaptability to
data drift, DL-based traffic detection still faces several pressing
challenges that require further investigation.
Firstly, deep learning models are inherently black-box in
nature, making it difficult for security administrators and system developers to interpret and justify their decisions, thereby
limiting their trustworthiness in security-critical environments.
Although recent advances in explainability techniques, such
as SHAP [61], have enabled structural-level interpretability
to some extent, these methods are generally insufficient to
provide high-level insights, such as revealing the underlying
attack mechanisms. Recently, the integration of large language
models (LLMs) into traffic detection pipelines [62] has been
explored as a promising approach to enhance interpretability
and provide more context-aware explanations.
Secondly, the sheer volume of network traffic data renders
both the labeling process and model training extremely
labor-intensive and time-consuming, often making the entire
training pipeline nearly infeasible in practice. Although
pretraining techniques [44], [47] that employ unsupervised
learning can effectively alleviate the burden of manual labeling,
the presence of massive redundant data within large-scale traffic
datasets still results in excessive computational overhead. This
highlights the need for efficient data sampling strategies that
can significantly reduce the volume of data while preserving
essential information.

Finally, as presented in Section VI-E, the detection performance on encrypted traffic is inferior to that achieved on the
full dataset, which includes plaintext traffic. This observation
indicates that encrypted traffic detection still faces several critical challenges. In particular, how to more effectively model the
behavioral characteristics of encrypted traffic without relying on
plaintext information, remain open problems that require further
investigation. In addition, as shown in Section VI-F, model
performance continues to degrade over time. This degradation is
primarily caused by the emergence of out-of-distribution (OOD)
data, or more generally, data drift, which leads to insufficient
model generalization. This issue represents a fundamental challenge in traffic detection and necessitates the development of
dedicated model update strategies to address it.
Therefore, advancing research on decision interpretability,
data sampling, encrypted traffic and data drift will be key components of our future work, as these directions are expected
to further enhance the practicality and effectiveness of deep
learning in traffic detection tasks.
VIII. CONCLUSION
We propose a novel traffic detection method for IoT environments, named FlowXpert. First, we identify the theoretical
limitations in the widely adopted flow-based features used in existing detection methods, which can severely hinder model convergence. To address this, we introduce a new context-aware feature extraction method termed FlowVision. Second, to improve
generalization capability, we design an unsupervised embedding
approach that integrates DBSCAN and contrastive learning for
effective representation learning. Finally, we conduct extensive
experiments on the real-world dataset (Mawi) and two simulated datasets (CICIDS-2017 and UNSW-NB15) to evaluate
the detection performance, generalizability, and improvements
over SOTA methods. Considering the real-time requirements of
IoT scenarios, our model is lightweight and achieves ultralow
detection latency, with per-sample inference time as low as 30
microseconds on a low-resource virtual machine.
