HAVEN: A Hybrid Anomaly Detection System for
Intra-Vehicular CAN-Bus Communication Using
Rule-Based and Neural Networks
Shaurya Purohit

Abstract—Modern vehicles rely on ECUs connected over an
Intra-Vehicular CAN-bus network to facilitate data exchange.
However, in recent times, the quantity of potential attack surfaces
for malicious cyberattacks, such as DOS, Fuzzy, etc., has notably
surged owing to their amplified connectivity. Unfortunately, the
CAN bus solitarily cannot ensure its protection due to its deficiency in security components, leading to grave safety and security
issues like disabling the brakes, etc., and hence requires a reliable
mechanism for detecting anomalies in the system. To address these
challenges, this paper proposes HAVEN, a Hybrid Anomaly Detection System for Intra-Vehicular CAN-bus Communication using
combinations of Rule-based with Machine Learning techniques
and Neural Networks (Binary and Multiclass Classification forms).
The experimental results show that our proposed hybrid models
achieve high detection accuracy on different datasets incurring significantly low execution time and high F1-score. This is attributable
to the parallel execution and multi-threading nature of the Machine
Learning and Neural Networks employed in conjunction with the
Rule-based techniques. Upon comparing the results of both models,
our evaluation demonstrates that the second model incorporating
neural networks yields superior results, establishing its potential
as a highly efficient and promising solution and opening future
avenues for further research work.
Index Terms—CAN-bus, anomaly detection, machine learning, neural networks, multiclass classification.

and connected vehicle technologies. Modern automated and
connected vehicles have intricate designs that merge different
automated driving functions, and diverse communication interfaces [1].
The inter-connectivity and autonomy of vehicles are increasing as Electronic Control Units (ECUs) communicate with each
other to manage various functions, including engine control,
telematics control, and airbag deployment. Different communication standards have been established within vehicles. Nearly
ubiquitous, Controller Area Network (CAN) act as intermediaries for data exchange between ECUs by furnishing a communal network with a standard protocol. It is a serial-bus communication protocol that allows sensors and actors to communicate

master that governs transmission. CAN nodes or devices initiate
transmissions on the CAN frame only when the bus is unoccupied, and the node is prepared to transmit. The communication
protocol used by the CAN-bus is designed to be a broadcast
domain, which implies that any message sent is received by
all the nodes linked to it. The CAN frame lacks the recipient’s
address but includes a unique message priority bit known as the
Arbitration ID. Nodes receive the frame by matching it with an
arbitrary bit of the sent frame, and each node decides whether
or not to accept the received frame.
In situations where several nodes attempt to send messages simultaneously, they employ a non-destructible system
called Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance
(CSMA/CA) and Arbitration on Message Priority (AMP) to
contend for access to the bus. The arbitration process in this
system prioritizes messages with lower ID, allowing them to be
transmitted first, while nodes with lower priority must wait until
higher priority nodes complete their execution.

vehicles have emerged as a novel arena for hackers to exploit,
thereby making automotive cybersecurity a matter of profound
importance within the present-day automotive industry. In fact,
every single node within these vehicles presents a potential
point of target for serious cyber-attacks. Given these risks,
developing and implementing a rigorous and comprehensive
methodology for identifying and addressing critical security
threats has become indispensable. Eclectic techniques, such as

in previous studies to ensure the legitimacy and reliability of
CAN messages [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. These methods are efficacious in preventing unauthorized access to the CAN network but
are limited in their ability to defend against attacks emanating
from compromised ECUs. Since ECUs are a vital part of the
vehicular network and are typically equipped with the necessary
credentials for secure communication, they can become a source
of security threats, hence allowing these malicious actors to
possess the capability to infiltrate the vehicular networks and
transmit unauthorized CAN messages that disrupt the regular
functioning of the vehicle, thereby leading to consequential
safety repercussions.

Given the lack of encryption and authentication procedures,

would be an efficacious method for detecting anomalous data
and malevolent attacks through network traffic data transmitted
between the vehicle and other devices. If an attacker executes an
attack by injecting harmful traffic into the CAN-bus, the ADS
sifts through the traffic and notifies the user of any unusual
activity by contrasting the normal and aberrant behaviors.
While ADSs have been widely studied here, many prior
approaches fall short in key areas. Most focus on a limited range of attacks—often ignoring threats like spoofing or
impersonation—and rely on simulated data, reducing real-world
applicability. Additionally, several models emphasize accuracy
but neglect execution time, which is critical for responsiveness in
CAN-bus environments. Many also lack lightweight preprocessing, making them inefficient for resource-constrained systems.

and potent detection and mitigation solutions for threats and
attacks. Several of these systems also exploit the recurring and

the message. Another intrusion detection algorithm estimates

Hamming distance data of reference to detect attacks [11]. The
majority of the suggested ADS lack efficacy due to their high
incidence of false positive and false negative alerts.

novel two-step Hybrid ADS’s for Intra-Vehicular CAN-bus
Communication (refers to the implementation of the CAN bus

which is a subset of the broader in-vehicle networks) that
maintain an equilibrium between detection rates and efficiency.
The initial model employs rule-based detection coupled with
tree-based ML algorithms, such as Decision Tree (DT), Random
Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine
(LightGBM). The second model employs rule-based discovery
in combination with deep neural networks, including Binary and
Multiclass classifiers. By integrating rule-based detection with
machine learning and neural networks, our framework ensures
high detection accuracy and low false negatives while effectively

addressing the challenge of complex non-periodic attacks (e.g.,
Impersonation, Spoofing) that lack consistent timing or structure and often bypass traditional frequency- or sequence-based
methods. The rule-based layer serves as a lightweight, rapid filter
that flags overt timing and sequence anomalies in CAN traffic.
The subsequent ML or neural network layer—depending on the
model—focuses on learning more subtle and irregular patterns
that evade deterministic rules. This two-step design allows us
to efficiently detect both frequent and complex attack behaviors
with minimal execution time.

A. CAN Bus
CAN is a bus communication protocol designed to broadcast
messages, with the ability to transmit frames that can contain
up to 64 bits of data. In Fig. 1, the standard format of a CAN

frame is presented. The CAN frame includes various fields such
as the Start of Frame (SOF), Control Field, Arbitration Field
(CAN ID), Data Field (payload), CRC Field, Acknowledge Field
(ACK), and End of Frame (EOF).
The Start Frame, which has a chief bit of ‘0’, marks the
beginning of a CAN message. The CAN ID field is capable of
supporting different identifier formats, such as a base frame of 11
bits, an extended frame of 29 bits, and an 18-bit extension. The
Remote Transmission Request field determines whether a message is being transmitted or received. The Control Field specifies
the transmitted data length, which ranges from 0 to 8 bytes. The
Data Field contains the actual content and critical information
sent to the network’s intended node. The Cyclic Redundancy
Check is a partial check used to detect any alterations made to
the original data. The Acknowledge Field is used to validate that
the node has received the data correctly. The End of the Frame
marks the message’s conclusion.
CAN is message-oriented and does not require sender and
receiver addresses. Instead, each CAN frame has a predetermined ID and message structure outlined in the DBC file. The
DBC file is a confidential file in an exclusive format, such as a
database, that is part of a specific vehicle configuration. During
compilation, the ECU is set to accept only CAN messages with
a specific CAN ID and disregard all other messages.
CAN ID refers to the designated ECU’s destination address,
and its payload has a maximum length of 8 bytes with no encryption or authentication mechanisms. As a result of this, CAN
is susceptible to message injection attacks due to its broadcast
communication strategy and the lack of authentication mechanisms. Adversaries can leverage this vulnerability to launch
DoS and Spoofing attacks on the CAN-bus, thereby occupying
resources and extracting sensitive information such as RPM
and Gear information. Furthermore, Replay and Fuzzy attacks
represent another class of frequently encountered categories of
attacks that target the CAN-bus. These attacks occur when the
attacker inserts a message into the bus to drive it into, signaling
an abnormal state or malfunction, thereby providing a false
impression to the vehicle’s control systems.
Many studies have posited the notion that an adversary can
acquire absolute authority by gaining entry to the CAN network
through a plethora of attack interfaces, including but not limited
to TPMS, Bluetooth, telematics, and OBD2. The deficiency of
the fundamental security protocols renders the CAN-bus system
susceptible to a variety of severe assaults. An adversary could

easily execute an array of harmful actions, including but not
limited to injecting harmful messages to observe network traffic
or launching hostile attacks, while nodes within the network are
obliged to process the message automatically without identifying its source.
B. Attack Model
As stated earlier, attackers are extremely incentivized to exploit the vulnerabilities present in the CAN Bus. In this paper,
we worked with some of the primary categories of in-vehicle
attacks that can be executed on the CAN-bus system: Denial
of Service (DOS), Fuzzy, Impersonation, Spoofing, and Replay
attacks. Fig. 2 portrays the prevalent categories of attacks on the
CAN-bus that have been addressed in our work.
Denial of Service Attack: DOS attacks on the CAN-bus system
involve sending many requests to the targeted nodes, which
consumes their resources and may lead to vehicle unavailability
or mishaps [12]. As Autonomous Vehicles (AVs) use wireless
technology, DOS attacks can be easily executed by adversaries.
In this type of attack, the attacker inundates the bus with messages having high frequency and high priority (such as messages
with a CAN ID of 0x000 or some different ID that is smaller
than all valid message IDs) to congest the bus and occupy its
entire bandwidth. This causes the true messages to be unable to
transmit successfully, eventually compromising the vehicle. If
the attackers gain access to the vehicle, they can easily insert
flooding assaults into the CAN-bus network, thereby interrupting or disabling the services of the ECUs in the vehicle.
Fuzzy Attack: The adversary launches an attack by injecting
CAN messages with unpredictable CAN IDs, which results
in a delay in the delivery of certain legitimate messages with
higher priority (i.e., larger CAN IDs). This form of attack is
more surreptitious than a DoS attack, owing to the random
nature of the CAN IDs assigned to the injected messages, and
a single malicious message injected of such nature into the
CAN-bus network system can potentially result in the failure of
the entire network, leading to unintended consequences such as
the steering wheel shaking, automatic gear shifts, turning on/off
indicator lights, and other similar effects.
Impersonation Attack: In this type of attack, the attacker
deliberately injects modified CAN messages with specific CAN
IDs to cause confusion within the ECU. If the attacker’s CAN
IDs are indistinguishable from legitimate ones, it is challenging

Replay Attack: In a replay attack, the attacker intercepts,
records valid messages sent over the bus, and then replays them
to the same or other nodes on the bus later. This type of attack
can be particularly dangerous in systems where messages are
used for control safety-critical functions, such as in automotive
control systems. The replayed message may cause unintended or
dangerous behavior, leading to serious consequences such as accidents or equipment damage. For example, suppose a message
with a specific CAN ID is sent to turn on a car’s headlights. In
that case, an attacker could intercept and record that message and
later replay it multiple times, causing the headlights to repeatedly
turn on and off, which could be dangerous for the driver and other
vehicles on the road.
Spoofing Attack: A spoofing attack on a CAN-bus refers to an
attack in which an attacker sends a message to the bus with a fake
source address to deceive the recipient node into thinking that
the message came from a trusted source. The attacker modifies
the message’s ID and data payload fields to make it appear
legitimate. This type of attack can have serious consequences,
such as compromising the integrity of the data, disrupting the
system’s normal functioning, or causing safety-critical systems
to fail. For example, an attacker could spoof a message from the
ECU to the braking system, instructing it to apply the brakes or
release them suddenly, causing a potential accident. Similarly,

an attacker could spoof a message to the ECU, instructing it to
shut down the engine or make other critical changes. Notably,
spoofing attacks can exhibit a non-periodic nature, meaning
they can occur at irregular intervals, making it challenging for
conventional security measures to identify and mitigate them
effectively. This type of attack can have serious safety implications, especially in autonomous vehicles where the system
relies heavily on the information being communicated via the
CAN-bus.
C. Problem Statement
On a vehicle’s CAN-bus network, the complexities and diversity of network traffic, encompassing both benign and malicious
activities, pose challenges in accurately identifying malevolent attacks such as DOS, Fuzzy, Impersonation, Replay, and
Spoofing attacks. Achieving high precision and minimizing
false negatives in detection are critical objectives. This paper
addresses this problem by proposing two potent ADS models,
designed using combinations of Rule-based, Machine Learning,
and Neural Networks techniques, that exhibit efficient performance in enhancing the accuracy of identifying these attacks
while ensuring low latency.

real-life scenarios to improve their functionality and enhance the

a simulated CAN environment with four nodes representing

training the LSTM network to forecast the next data word sent

accordingly. The research conducted by Li et al. [17] presents
an innovative technique for identifying anomalous CAN-bus
messages through the fusion of a deep neural network and a
triplet loss network. Specifically, the DNN is first employed
to train CAN-bus sequences and extract feature vectors which
are then utilized for the triplet loss network, which calculates
similarities between three randomly selected data sequences
from the CAN-bus database. Ali Alheeti et al. [18] introduced
an IDS using Artificial Neural Network (ANN) to oversee the
car’s status based on information gathered from the internal
buses. The primary objective of this system is to ensure the
security and safety of the internal network. To achieve this, the
parameters of the ANN structure are established by training it
with CAN packet information to derive the essential statistical
properties of normal and attacking packets. This system aims to
extract the relevant attribute to classify the attack and protect
the car from any potential security threat. The research presented by Kosmanos et al. [19] presents a cross-layer Intrusion
Detection System (IDS) utilizing probabilistic approaches and
Machine Learning (ML) techniques, specifically KN and RF.
Olufowobi et al. [20] suggested an anomaly-based IDS for CAN
attack detection using the adaptive cumulative sum (CUSUM)
technique. This technique can effectively identify intrusions
based on statistical changes, but it may take a significant amount
of time to execute. Song et al. [21] proposed a lightweight IDS
for in-vehicle networks based on time interval analysis (TIA)
of CAN communications. Another proposed approach [22] suggested using a combination of in-vehicle sensors to verify message formality, position, data range, data plausibility, and other

IV. RULE AND MACHINE LEARNING BASED HYBRID ADS
MODEL
This research endeavors to devise a pioneering ADS model
for vehicular networks, which can detect and pinpoint any
malicious conduct on the CAN system in real-time and draw
attention to any aberrations from standard or malignant behaviors. Our proposed methodology comprises a two-stage ADS
model aimed at attaining the desired objectives. A robust rulebased system is put into operation in the first stage, while
for the second stage, a tree-based ML algorithm system is
employed.
The high-level design of the ADS model is presented in Fig. 3.
The proposed two-stage ADS model utilizes a Rule-based system at the initial stage to filter out most attacks and only provides
efficient detection before allowing remaining messages to move
forward. Rules are designed to have a low rate of false negatives.
Messages that do not pass through the rules in the system are
deemed as an attack, and an alert is extended. Subsequently, the
remaining messages are directed towards the ML-based model
(Model1) for further processing. The multi-threading nature and
parallelism of the ML techniques render the overall model to
have a significantly reduced computational time. The messages
that satisfy both stages are then transmitted to the vehicular
network via the CAN-bus.

C. Rule Based Intrusion Detection
The foremost stage of the proposed ADS model is a rule-based
component, which offers efficient detection prior to moving on
to the second stage. The characteristics of CAN traffic can be
exploited to establish rule-based systems, and a plethora of rules
can be implemented. The key objective is to determine a set of
criteria that can filter out most of the attack messages in the
first stage, thus preventing them from moving on to the timeconsuming second stage. In order to achieve quick detection, it
is crucial to select rules that possess a low false-negative rate
and minimal processing latency.
Our model comprises of 4 rules which are based on the
subsequent attributes:
1) Valid ID: A valid message transmitted via CAN protocol
necessitates the presence of a singular and exclusive identifier. Any communication lacking such an identifier will
be promptly recognized, identified as potentially malevolent, and subsequently flagged. Consequently, the message will be deemed invalid and omitted from the further
stage.
2) Message Frequency: The rule underpinning this regulation
stems from the observation that a vast majority of CAN
packets are transmitted in a consistent and predictable pattern, thus leading to a recognizable frequency distribution.
3) Message Sequence: The underlying foundation for the
sequence rule is the observation that a set of specific IDs
inevitably accompanies a unique ID. For instance, this rule
is particularly effective in detecting DOS attacks, as such
attacks are often characterized by the presence of 0x0000
value in their messages, which can be promptly eyeballed
through inspection.

4) Time Interval: The time interval rule is pertinent to CAN

identifying injected messages, as it scrutinizes the time

D. ML Based Anomaly Detection
Decision tree, Random Forest, XGBoost, CatBoost and LightGBM are the ML techniques used in the ADS model.
r Decision Tree: The Decision Tree (DT) is a classification
method that recursively splits data based on the attribute
with the highest information gain. It builds a tree-like
model of decisions and stops when reaching a predefined
criterion, such as maximum depth or minimum samples
per leaf node.
r Random Forest: Random Forest (RF) is an ensemble learning method that selects the final classification result based
on majority voting from multiple decision trees. Each tree is
trained on a random subset of data and features to increase
diversity and prevent overfitting. The predictions from all
trees in the forest are combined to make the final decision.
XGBoost: XGBoost [29] is a boosting ML algorithm that
combines decision trees using gradient descent, improving speed and accuracy. It iteratively trains trees on the
residuals of the previous ones, correcting errors with each
iteration to create a more accurate overall model.
r CatBoost and LightGBM: CatBoost [30] and LightGBM [31] are advanced gradient boosting algorithms.
CatBoost excels in processing categorical data with
minimal preprocessing, ideal for complex datasets. LightGBM reduces memory usage with histogram-based methods and leaf-wise growth, enhancing efficiency. Both algorithms like XGBoost, iteratively refine decision trees,
offering precise corrections and notable accuracy on large
datasets.
The rationales underlying the selection of these algorithms
are:
r The primary motive for opting for these tree-based algorithms is their reduced computational time attributed to
multi-threading utilization.
r Furthermore, the capability of these algorithms to deal with
non-linear and high-dimensional data, which is characteristic of the network data being analyzed, also contributed
to their selection.
r As most of these machine learning models are based on tree
structures that incorporate ensemble learning, they tend to
exhibit superior performance compared to single models
such as KNN and SVM.
r Also, the ability to perform parallel execution is a notable
advantage of these algorithms, resulting in a significant
reduction in the time required for model training and
refinement in detection accuracy.
Under the assumption that the number of instances is denoted
by n, the number of features is represented by K, and the number
of decision trees in ensemble models is represented by t, the
time complexity of DT, RF, XGBoost, CatBoost and LightGBM
algorithms can be expressed as follows:

r DT has a time complexity of O(n2 K).
r RF has a time complexity of O(n2 √Kt).
r XGBoost, CatBoost have a time complexity of O(nKt).
r LightGBM has a time complexity of O(n log n · Kt).
It is worth mentioning that the optimization of some hyperparameters is necessary to enhance the performance of the algorithms. Furthermore, the number of decision trees T represents
a crucial parameter that greatly influences the performance of
tree algorithms, as the majority vote of multiple decision trees
influences their outcomes.
Consequently, the grid search approach is employed to determine the optimal parameter values. The models are initially
trained using a small number of decision trees and a low-depth
level to ensure accuracy. Subsequently, these two values are
incrementally increased while checking for accuracy until overfitting is detected, which is characterized by a deterioration in
the accuracy of the model.
E. Performance Evaluation
1) Validation Metrics: In order to assess the generalizability of the proposed model and prevent overfitting, both crossvalidation and hold-out approaches are utilized. The train-testvalidation split and model evaluation process employed for the
dataset are as follows:
1) The dataset is split into training and test sets using a 70%30% train-test split. Specifically, 70% of the data samples
(chronologically ordered by timestamp) are assigned to the
training set, while the remaining 30% (which follow the
training portion) are reserved as the test set for final holdout validation. This preserves temporal order and avoids
potential data leakage by ensuring that no future data is
used during model training.
2) To assess the performance of the proposed model across
multiple areas of the dataset, 10-fold cross-validation is
conducted on the training set. Specifically, in each iteration/fold of the 10-fold cross-validation, 90% of the
original training set is used for model training, while the
remaining 10% of the original training set is utilized as
the validation set for model testing.
This process is repeated ten times, with each subset serving
as the validation set once, and the overall performance is
evaluated as the average of the performance across all ten
iterations/folds.
3) The model is tested on the untouched test set to evaluate
the performance of the trained model obtained from Step
2. This step ensures that the model can generalize well to
new, unseen data and provides an accurate measure of its
performance.

A. Binary Classification Neural Networks
A binary classification neural network is a computational
model that includes an input layer, several hidden layers, and an
output layer. The layers are composed of multiple neurons, and
a single-node Sigmoid output layer for distinguishing between
normal and attack classes.
They are used for binary classification tasks, where the goal
is to classify an input into one of two possible classes, and
hence there is only one output node that gives the probability of
belonging to one of the two classes. In our case, they are trained
to classify inputs into one of two categories - normal or attack.
In our model, the subsequent step after obtaining data from the
initial stage 1 involves developing a binary classification neural
network using the TensorFlow API.
The neural network is created through a sequential method
by adding two dense layers with 40 and 20 neurons, respectively, along with input feature size. Only one neuron is in the
output layer, as the output is either 1 (normal) or 0 (attack) for
classification purposes.
Following the standard hyperparameters and optimization
strategies of neural networks for binary classification [35], the
activation function, ‘Relu’ is used at the input layer to ensure
non-negative output from all neurons. At the output layer, the
‘Sigmoid’ activation function is employed to specify binary classification by determining the size of the output label. The model
is trained using ‘Adam’ optimizer with a learning rate of 0.01
and is compiled with a binary cross-entropy loss function model,
which measures the difference between the predicted probability
of the positive class and the true probability of the positive class,
i.e., it penalizes the network for making incorrect predictions and
rewards it for making correct predictions. Table VI shows the
hyper-parameter list of Binary Classification Neural Network.
Finally, the model is trained using xtrain (featuring values
used for training and prediction) and ytrain (output label values),
and the performance metrics are reckoned.

VI. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we presented two novel two-step ADS’s models for in-vehicle CAN buses. In first model, we employed a
rule-based component to check injected CAN signals in the first
step and tree-based machine learning methods in the second
step. The SMOTE oversampling technique was used to lessen
the impact of class imbalance and computational expense. The
results on the CAN-intrusion dataset reveal that the proposed
system provides an detection accuracy of above 90% with very
low execution time (in all three cases), thus showing a better
trade-off between the accuracy and the execution time. The
second model employs rule-based discovery in combination
with deep neural networks, including binary and multiclass
classifiers which was something missing from previous works.
The model was tested on three large datasets and achieved
high detection accuracy rates of over 95% for all three datasets
while also maintaining low execution times, including various
types of attacks such as DOS, Fuzzy, Impersonation, Spoofing,
and Replay attacks. The optimization of performance in the
model is attained through the meticulous adjustment of specific
parameters while testing and examining the model’s functioning
through fine-tuning parameters such as the number of hidden
layers, the encoding methods employed, and the model’s dropout
and learning rate. After thorough evaluation, it is evident that our
second hybrid ADS model integrating neural networks outperforms the tree-based machine learning variant in both accuracy
and execution efficiency. This advantage stems from the neural
network’s ability to analyze complex, high-dimensional data and
swiftly adapt to nuanced patterns. While tree-based models such
as LightGBM, XGBoost, and CatBoost offer strong performance
across accuracy, recall, and F1-scores, they do not consistently
provide faster inference times. DL models, on the other hand,
show superior performance in detecting non-periodic attacks

like spoofing and impersonation, while maintaining low execution latency. This highlights the complementary strengths of both
approaches, validating our hybrid architecture that strategically
integrates them for a balanced and robust anomaly detection
system
As a part of our future endeavors and a natural progression
of this research, we aim to augment the performance of our
hybrid ADS model by continuously seeking and scrutinizing
new rules and machine learning algorithms that could potentially provide superior results. Furthermore, we can enhance
the proposed model’s outcomes on datasets by optimizing the
hyper-parameters of the algorithms, which can be achieved
by utilizing advanced optimization techniques such as particle
swarm and Bayesian optimization. This would aid in fine-tuning
the model parameters and eventually lead to better results. Furthermore, we intend to incorporate emerging advancements in
machine learning, including one-class classifiers, autoencoders,
and self-supervised anomaly detection methods, to improve
resilience against previously unseen attack types and reduce
dependency on labeled data. Finally, evaluating the model in
real-time conditions using live CAN traffic is another important
direction that will help validate HAVEN’s detection latency and
practical deployment feasibility.
