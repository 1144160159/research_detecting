Class Incremental Website Fingerprinting Attack
Based on Dynamic Expansion Architecture
Yali Yuan , Yangyang Du , and Guang Cheng , Member, IEEE

Abstract—Encrypted traffic on anonymizing networks is still at
risk of being exposed to the Website Fingerprinting (WF) attack.
This attack can seriously threaten the online privacy of users of
anonymity networks such as Tor. While deep-learning-based WF
attacks achieve high accuracy in controlled experimental settings,
they cannot continuously learn after deployment. In real-world
environments, new websites are constantly emerging, requiring
attackers to expand their monitoring scope continuously. This
necessitates attack models capable of continuous learning and
expanding classification capabilities. In this paper, we explore
how attackers can leverage incremental class learning techniques
to continuously learn new classes while retaining the ability to
distinguish old ones. This approach mitigates the catastrophic
forgetting problem in dynamic, open-world scenarios. We introduce a new WF attack, Class Incremental Fingerprinting (CIF),
which employs a scalable architecture enabling Class Incremental
Learning (CIL) with limited resources. We evaluate this attack in
various scenarios, such as learning 100, 200, and 500 monitored
website classes across 5 and 10 incremental tasks, achieving an
average accuracy of 97.8% and above. Additionally, we assess
the CIF attack’s effectiveness in open-world multi-classification
scenarios and test it in few-shot settings using the proposed
data augmentation method, Mixtam, achieving an average task
accuracy of 87.6% and above with only 30 samples per class.
Index Terms—Tor, privacy, website fingerprinting, deep learning, class incremental learning.

protection of users has become increasingly important.

five million active users utilize Tor to protect their privacy
every day. The Tor network employs multi-layer encryption
and relay switching to protect user privacy. User data passes
through multiple relay nodes in the Tor network, each of
which can only decrypt a single layer of information, making
user identity and activities difficult to trace. However, the
Website Fingerprinting (WF) attack [3], [4], [5], [6], [7] is a

continuous learning and adaptation in real-world open-world
settings, especially when adapting to new websites under
limited computational and storage resources. Conventional
deep learning-based WF attacks [12], [13], [15] use precollected data from a large set of target websites to train
a complex model capable of recognizing all sample classes
simultaneously. However, such a model can only generalize to
known classes.

environments, even under limited computational and storage

expanding site set scenarios. These methods are generally
evaluated in closed-world and open-world settings, neither
of which can simulate the dynamic, open-world environments of real-world scenarios or evaluate the incremental site
fingerprinting attack methods we propose. In response, we
introduce two novel scenario settings: dynamic closed-world
and dynamic open-world.

significant increase in computational or memory resource

II. T HREAT M ODEL
Tor is a widely used anonymous communication network.
Users access the Onion Network through the free Onion
Browser to browse websites while protecting their personal
information. However, attackers can still compromise users’
privacy by collecting traffic from monitored websites and
utilizing Website Fingerprinting (WF) attack techniques to
identify the websites users visit. As depicted in Fig. 1, akin to
earlier WF attacks [8], [13], [32], within this threat model, the
attacker is regarded as a localized passive observer. Positioned
between the Tor client and the Tor ingress node (the access
point to the Tor network), this attacker is capable of monitoring
and logging network traffic without making any modifications
or injections. The attacker’s goal is to identify the specific
websites visited by Tor users. Although traffic is encrypted
through multiple layers of the Tor network, a WF attacker can
still infer user behavior by analyzing features like timestamps
and packet sizes using machine learning.
Closed-world and open-world scenarios are frequently
employed to evaluate WF attack methods [12], [32], [33]. The
closed-world scenario restricts the user’s access to a subset of
websites defined by the attacker, constituting the monitored
sites. This scenario significantly differs from real-world conditions, but it provides a simplified and effective environment
for analyzing and evaluating WF attack capabilities. The openworld scenario is more realistic, assuming a user can access
both the monitored sites and those outside the attacker’s
scope, which form a substantial set of non-monitored sites. In
the open-world multi-classification scenario, the attacker must
determine if a traffic trace is part of the monitored set. If so, the
attacker must pinpoint the specific site within the monitored
set that the trace matches.
Neither scenario allows dynamic scaling of monitored
websites to simulate a real open environment. Therefore,
we propose two scenarios, dynamic closed-world, and openworld, to evaluate evolving WF attack scenarios.
III. BACKGROUND AND R ELATED W ORK
In the Tor network, messages are transmitted in fixed-size
units (512 bytes) and are padded with zeros if insufficient
data is available [15], [16]. An attacker can analyze Tor’s
traffic patterns and extract features such as interval time
(IPD), direction, and packet size. These extracted features

can then serve as inputs for training classifiers to identify
the websites visited by users. Broadly, WF attack methods
discussed in the literature are categorized into two groups
based on feature extraction techniques: traditional WF attacks
that involve manual feature extraction and WF attacks that
utilize deep learning for automated feature extraction.
Traditional website fingerprinting attack methods.
The earliest related research was conducted by Herrmann
et al. (2009) [34], achieving an accuracy of merely 2.96%.
Wang et al. [11] proposed a WF attack based on a manually extracted feature set, applying a k-NN classifier with
feature weighting to emphasize the most important features.
Pachinko et al. [33] introduced the CUMUL method, which
accumulates Tor unit-sampling features and uses a Support
Vector Machine (SVM) classifier to achieve a higher accuracy
( 91.38%) in a closed-world setting. Moreover, Hayes and
Danezis [32] created the k-fingerprinting ( k-FP) technique.
This technique integrates a random decision forest and a
kkk-nearest neighbor k-NN classifier to make use of a substantial collection of statistical features, showing high accuracy.
While these traditional approaches based on machine learning
techniques, such as SVM, (k-NN), and Random Forest, have
achieved notable success, their reliance on hand-designed
features and the lack of effective interaction between feature
representation and classifier models have constrained their
overall performance.
Deep website fingerprinting attack methods. Researchers
have explored applying deep learning to WF attacks, inspired
by its success in computer vision, natural language processing, and other fields. Deep learning methods enable direct
end-to-end feature learning from raw data, removing the
need for manual feature engineering. Rimmer et al. [12]
employed only directional sequences from traffic traces as
inputs and utilized three types of deep networks (SDAE,
CNN, and LSTM) to achieve 96% classification accuracy
in a closed-world scenario. Sirinam et al. [13] put forward
the Deep Fingerprinting (DF) attack approach relying on a
more intricate CNN structure DF also used only directional
sequences as inputs, achieving 98% accuracy in closed-world
scenarios and effectively bypassing WTF-PAD defenses [35].
Bhat et al. [36] developed the Var-CNN model using Dilated
Causal Convolutions on ResNets, demonstrating superior
performance with few-shot training samples compared to DF.
Deng et al. [37] proposed Holmes, a model using temporal and spatial distribution analysis to enhance WF attacks
on partial traffic, achieving notable improvements in earlystage traffic recognition under dynamic network conditions.
Attarian et al. [38] proposed a method that focuses on the first
part of the tracked packets, achieving high preprocessing and
classification efficiency while highlighting the critical role of
directional information in website detection.
Few-shot website fingerprinting attack methods. Many
researchers have proposed few-shot learning methods to
overcome the limitation of requiring large labeled training
datasets and enhance the effectiveness of WF attacks in realworld scenarios. Siriam proposed the Triplet Fingerprinting
(TF) [21] attack, which first trains a CNN model for feature
extraction using a large dataset. The extracted features are

1957

then used to train a k-NN classifier for WF classification.
Experimental results demonstrate that the method performs
well with limited data and is transferable by re-training the
k-NN classifier with a new dataset. Zhou et al. [23] proposed
the Clustered WF Attack (CWFA) method, which utilizes the
cluster assumption to efficiently perform the attack in the
presence of limited training data. NetCLR [39] employs selfsupervised contrastive learning to augment Tor network traces,
achieving robust WF accuracy and high transferability without
relying on extensive labeled data.
Data augmentation techniques can also reduce reliance on
large labeled datasets. Chen et al. [22] put forward a harmonized data augmentation (HDA) method. This method utilizes
both within-sample and between-sample transformations to
expand small training datasets. Additionally, Chen et al.
[40] introduced the TForm-RF algorithm, which generates
augmented traffic traces by extracting and randomizing traffic
patterns from cell sequences, outperforming HDA in effectiveness.
Several studies have explicitly addressed the challenge of
mixing multi-Web page traffic in WF attacks. These works
[6], [19], [20], [41], [42] propose methods for segmenting and
identifying traffic that combines tags from multiple Web pages.
Additionally, Cherubin et al. [18] introduced a novel threat
model that evaluates the practical effectiveness of WF attacks
on the Tor network. This model leverages real Tor exit node
traffic data and employs the TF approach to simulate more
realistic attack scenarios.
Website fingerprinting Defenses. In addressing the challenge of WF attacks, the research community has explored and
developed a variety of defense methods [3], [35], [43], [44],
[45], [46], [47].
One defense strategy actively modifies the temporal and
quantitative characteristics of communication traffic to complicate the correlation with specific website activities. Early
attempts, such as BuFLO [48], CS-BuFLO [46], and Tamaraw
[3], were effective in creating barriers to WF attacks but
incurred significant bandwidth and time overheads due to
fixed-rate packet delivery, adversely affecting user experience.
Subsequent research has sought to balance overhead reduction with defense effectiveness. For example, WTF-PAD [35]
utilizes an adaptive approach to insert virtual packets during
natural gaps in the actual data transmission, achieving a
defense with zero time overhead. Front [49] also employs
a zero-latency design that utilizes timestamps generated by
the Rayleigh distribution to guide the embedding of the
virtual packets. Walkie-Talkie [50] innovatively employs a
half-duplex communication model, where the target-site data
packets are mixed with non-target-site data, increasing the
difficulty of identification for attackers. RegulaTor [51] minimizes time and bandwidth overheads through a well-designed
packet-peaking adjustment strategy while maintaining defense
efficiency. TrafficSliver [52] offers another approach by
reconfiguring network architecture or employing client-side
multiplexing, which reduces traffic exposure at single observation points without directly consuming bandwidth or time
resources. More recent defenses, such as Surakav [53] and
Palette [54], enhance protection by leveraging dynamic traffic

patterns and clustering techniques. Surakav employs GANs to
generate adaptive, realistic traffic patterns, while Palette clusters similar traffic patterns into uniform groups, obfuscating
traffic within each cluster and reducing identification risks.
In addition to these traffic obfuscation approaches, adversarial perturbations present another class of defense mechanisms.
By introducing carefully crafted noise into the data, these
methods aim to mislead deep learning models and hinder
accurate classification. Typically, they [55], [56], [57], [58],
[59] employ specialized loss functions to reshape distances in
the feature space, offering alternative insights into the design
of effective defenses. However, such approaches often require
full trace data to compute the necessary perturbations and
have shown limited effectiveness against adversarially trained
models [60].
Class incremental learning. Class incremental learning (CIL) is a crucial branch of artificial intelligence that
addresses the challenges of learning new classes. This
approach ensures continuous knowledge accumulation without
forgetting previous information. In recent years, incremental
learning has achieved remarkable results in diverse fields,
including computer vision [61], [62], [63], natural language
processing [64], [65], and robotics [66], [67]. With rapid
development, numerous approaches have emerged and can be
classified into three categories: data-centric, model-centric, and
algorithm-centric.
Data-centric approaches for mitigating forgetting rely heavily on additional data. For instance, GDumb [68] enables
data replay to retain previous knowledge, while EWC [69]
applies regularization to restrict parameter updates and
ensure retention of learned tasks. FOSTER [70] uses model
extension strategies, dynamically adding new modules to
slow catastrophic forgetting, while integrating methods to
reduce redundancy. Parameter regularization techniques like
LWF [71] maintain performance on old tasks while acquiring
new knowledge through customized loss functions.
Algorithm-centric approaches focus on knowledge transfer
and model-tuning techniques. LUCIR [72] uses old model
predictions as soft labels for knowledge distillation, reducing
forgetting. In contrast, DER++ [73] combines replay buffering and knowledge distillation to optimize the update process,
significantly suppressing forgetting.

explain the data enhancement technique used in the few-shot
CIL scenario.

B. Class Incremental Learning Architecture
We propose a model architecture tailored for incremental learning of WF attacks, inspired by previous
works [28], [29], [30]. The dynamic expansion framework
(CIF) incrementally adds feature extractors and classifiers to
handle progressively increasing classification tasks. At each
incremental learning stage, feature extractors generate feature
maps, which are enhanced through a fusion module employing
the SE (Squeeze and Excitation) mechanism to highlight
critical features selectively. To address forgetting, CIF employs
knowledge distillation to integrate newly learned and existing
knowledge. Furthermore, CIF uses a re-scoring mechanism
to dynamically adjust classifier outputs, ensuring balanced
performance between new and old classes. Finally, the loss
function combines cross-entropy loss, knowledge distillation
loss, and re-scoring loss to ensure effective learning of new
tasks while retaining previously acquired knowledge.
It is shown in Fig. 2. The architecture is roughly divided
into two phases in each CIL step:
• First phase. The data for the current task is input into
the feature extractor to generate the corresponding feature
maps. During subsequent learning steps, these feature
maps are fused with those produced by the old feature
extractor. This fusion effectively integrates new and old
task knowledge, ensuring a comprehensive representation
that supports subsequent classification and recognition
tasks.
• Second phase. Feature maps are processed by the SE
attention model, which automatically learns feature channel weights to enhance informative features and suppress
irrelevant ones. Following this, the classifier re-scoring
mechanism refines the ordering and scores of the classifier outputs, improving the model’s output quality and
adaptability to diverse task requirements.

Classifier Re-scoring (CR). In incremental learning, the
model tends to focus excessively on novel classes, resulting
in output logits biased towards these newer classes. This bias
adversely impacts the model’s generalizability and its ability
to effectively transfer knowledge. To mitigate this issue, we

C. Data Representation
Our approach utilizes a Traffic Aggregation Matrix
(TAM) [25], which differs from most prior deep learningbased WF attack methods that primarily focus on sequences
of direction information in Tor traffic data. TAM aggregates
multidimensional information, including direction, volume,
and timing, providing a more comprehensive and robust set of
features.
The network traffic trace related to a website visit can be
expressed as w = (s1 , s2 , . . . , sl ), where l denotes the length
of the trace. We set l = +∞ to account for all packets. Let
si = ti , di be a pair consisting of the packet timestamp and
its direction. Here, ti and di signify the arrival time and the
direction of the i-th packet, respectively. It should be noted that
di ∈ {±1, 0}, with +1 denoting the outgoing packet direction
and −1 representing the incoming packet direction.
The specific implementation of TAM involves partitioning
the network traffic into multiple fixed-length time slots and
counting the number of outgoing and incoming packets within
each slot. Let M be the TAM of trace W, with N being the
count of time slots taken into account in the TAM. Given
that the length of each time slot is s, the maximum load time
T encompassed by a trace can be computed as T/s. In the
resulting two-dimensional matrix M, each row represents the
packet counts for either the outgoing or incoming direction,
while the columns correspond to different time windows. Each
element mij records the cumulative count of packets in the
respective direction within the time interval from (j − 1) × s
to j × s seconds.
TAM captures intermediate granularity information by
aggregating outgoing and incoming packet counts per time
slot, thereby enhancing robustness against various defense
mechanisms. Furthermore, compared to using direction
sequences alone as a traffic representation, TAM captures
a richer set of traffic characteristics, improving the model’s
generalization capabilities. This aids in maintaining model
performance during incremental learning processes.

D. Feature Extractor
We use RF [25] as a feature extractor in a dynamically scalable architecture for WF attacks. The RF model is specifically
designed based on the CNN architecture, targeting the TAM
traffic representation method. Leveraging the construction of
TAM and a multi-level convolutional network structure, the
model effectively extracts local features in both the temporal
and spatial dimensions of the traffic flow. Furthermore, it
refines robust features capable of resisting various defense
strategies.
Convolutional Blocks. In the model, 2D convolutional
blocks are initially applied to process the two-dimensional
input data, namely the TAM. The TAM contains information
about the number of data packets in different directions
(upload, download) within time slots during network communication, reflecting the interaction between clients and
servers and the variability of traffic patterns. Through two
2D convolutional layers, the model captures and extracts local
and intermediate spatiotemporal features. These layers are
combined with the ReLU activation function to enhance nonlinear expressiveness, BatchNormalization layers to ensure
stable convergence during training and prevent overfitting, and
MaxPooling layers for downsampling. The TAM is then fused
into a 1D feature map using a 2×2 maximum pooling layer,
further reducing the feature dimensionality while preserving
essential information. To extract higher-level features, the
model reduces the number of channels and reshapes the
output to expand the feature map size, followed by two 1D
convolutional blocks for deeper feature mining.
Global Average Pooling (GAP). To avoid overfitting
caused by the excessive number of parameters introduced
by traditional fully connected layers, the model adopts the
GAP [79] layer as an alternative. The GAP layer does not
introduce additional parameters but directly computes the
average of each feature map, reducing the model’s complexity
and enhancing its generalization ability. Finally, by flattening
the vector obtained from averaging each feature map and
passing it through the softmax function, the model generates a
probability distribution for different website classes, completing the classification task.

In this section, we conduct a series of experimental evaluations to investigate the performance of CIF attacks under

various experimental scenarios. Traditional deep learning
methods [12], [13], [14], [15], [36], [38] must be retrained
from scratch to satisfy the attacker’s need to dynamically
expand the set of monitoring sites. Moreover, no prior WF
attack methods have considered similar scenarios for experimental evaluation. Recent WF attack methods [19], [22], [23],
[24], [25], [40], [85], [86] also fail to address the lack of
continuous learning capability in attack models, making them
unsuitable for CIL scenarios.
In the absence of methods specifically designed for incremental learning in WF attacks, we conduct comparative
experiments using TF [21], DF [13], Var-CNN [36], and
NetCLR [39] under the same experimental setup. As a classic
few-shot WF attack method, the TF attack exhibits certain
transfer properties, enabling classification on an expanded
set of websites by retraining the k-NN classifier. Similarly,
NetCLR leverages fine-tuning to adapt to new website sets,
demonstrating transferability across different data distributions.
The deep learning-based WF attack model Var-CNN [36]
achieves superior performance compared to DF [13] by
employing extended causal convolution but lacks direct support for incremental learning. To address this, we modified
both DF and Var-CNN by incorporating a k-NN classifier,
enabling them to classify an expanded set of websites through
retraining. These modified models are referred to as DF* and
Var-CNN*, respectively. Although TF, DF*, Var-CNN*, and
NetCLR can adapt to classification tasks on new website sets,
they lack support for direct incremental learning. Each requires
fine-tuning with data from both new and stored old classes,
making them sensitive to the distribution similarity between
the pre-training and test datasets. To evaluate these methods
under different data distributions, we set up experiments
comparing TF-Syn vs. DF-Syn and TF-Div vs. DF-Div. For
Var-CNN* and NetCLR, results are presented based on pretraining and fine-tuning within the same data distribution.
We conduct CIF evaluations under four settings:
dynamic closed-world, dynamic open-world, few-shot Class
Incremental Learning, and adversarial defense measures.

TF and NetCLR, in particular, perform poorly in this scenario. TF struggles with effectively utilizing the extensive
training data from non-monitored classes, leading to severe
class imbalance and reduced accuracy. Similarly, NetCLR’s
reliance on NETAUGMENT and contrastive learning makes
it vulnerable to disruptions from the diverse traffic data in
the non-monitored category, degrading the quality of its initial
learning and subsequent fine-tuning, resulting in significant
accuracy drops.

In this section, we explore the proposed CIF attack and
suggest potential directions for future work.
The CIF model, leveraging a dynamic scaling architecture, is capable of continuously acquiring new information
from emerging website classes without the need for complete retraining, thereby exhibiting remarkable scalability. This
innovative CIF attack approach addresses the limitations of
prior WF attack methods, which lacked continuous learning
capabilities in open environments. As a result, CIF effectively
satisfies the requirements of attackers for expanding surveillance scopes and updating models in dynamic settings.
While our designs, including DF*, Var-CNN*, and TF,
can be adapted for website expansion tasks through classifier retraining, their performance is heavily constrained by
the similarity of dataset distributions. Significant discrepancies between pre-trained data and new task data often lead
to pronounced declines in accuracy. Furthermore, TF and
NetCLR exhibit severe performance degradation under highly
dynamic open-world scenarios. Unlike CIF, these methods
rely on the continuous storage of all samples across classes
and necessitate the reprocessing of the entire dataset during
each retraining cycle, making them impractical for resourceconstrained environments.
CIF has consistently demonstrated outstanding performance
across a variety of experimental scenarios. Nonetheless, further
research is essential to address the complexities inherent in
more challenging real-world applications.
Higher Few-shot Performance. Facing the rapidly changing network environment, attackers must efficiently collect the
latest network traffic traces and promptly update their attack
models. This requirement highlights the necessity for CIF
attacks to possess robust few-shot learning capabilities. In real
scenarios, we may only be able to collect limited data for some
websites in a short period (e.g., less than 10 training samples
per class). Therefore, in addition to the data enhancement
methods already designed, we need to explore how we can
better adapt to the few-shot scenario in the CIF framework
species.
Fine-Grained website fingerprinting. Current research on
WF attacks often focuses exclusively on the homepage traffic
of a website as a representative identifier for the entire site.
This simplification overlooks the diversity of subpages, which
is inconsistent with the complexity of real-world Web browsing environments. Subpages of the same website typically
share similar layout structures, follow consistent encryption
protocols, and use comparable parameter configurations. These
similarities result in high convergence in the characteristics
and client-server interaction patterns of the Web traffic they
generate.
Exploring fine-grained Web fingerprinting techniques is
essential for accurately modeling real-world scenarios and
uncovering more profound insights into user privacy. Our
research will focus on advancing the development of finegrained CIF attack techniques to better align with the practical
demands of real-world applications.

VII. C ONCLUSION
In this study, we investigate implementing WF attack methods with continuous learning capability through incremental
class learning. This approach addresses the limitations of
existing attack models, which lack continuous learning for
more challenging real-world application scenarios. We propose
CIF attacks based on a dynamically scaling architecture that
continuously learns knowledge from new classes without
catastrophic forgetting. This design enables the attack model to
scale and adapt to dynamically open real-world environments.
To gain insight into the performance of CIF attacks in reality,
we have recently set up dynamic closed-world and open-world
scenarios. The experimental results indicate that the CIF attack
achieves high accuracy during the CIL process, maintaining
an average accuracy above 97.8% in the dynamic closedworld and remaining effective in the dynamic open-world with
an average accuracy exceeding 96.7%. In addition, we also
demonstrate that the CIF attack achieves good performance
when faced with few-shot scenarios. According to these
findings, attackers can utilize new data update models to cope
with dynamically open real-world environments with limited
resources.
