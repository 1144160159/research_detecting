Fadlallah Chbib , Sherali Zeadally , Senior Member, IEEE, Ahmad Fadlallah ,

Abstract— The Cooperative Intelligent Transportation System
(C-ITS) is vital in enhancing road safety, improving traffic efficiency, and increasing user comfort for pedestrians and drivers.
However, as vehicle communications evolve, security threats
that aim to undermine critical security services, such as data
confidentiality, integrity, and privacy, have become crucial issues
that must be addressed. Privacy, the freedom from interference
or intrusion, is also considered one of the most significant
challenges in computer networks and connected vehicles. Privacy
in C-ITS can be protected by preventing both the collection
of personal information and the tracking of vehicles by malicious users. Tracking is often achieved through the periodic
transmission of Cooperative Awareness Messages (CAMs), which
contain spatio-temporal information such as position and speed.
We investigate key tracking-related critical issues in intelligent
connected vehicles. We highlight current security challenges and
attacks that lead to the unauthorized tracking of connected cars.
We discuss various defense solutions proposed in the literature
to address these challenges. We classify these solutions into
two groups: general (addressing eavesdropping, Sybil, etc.) and
pseudonym change (addressing correlation, swap, silent periods,
and mix-zones). Finally, we explore robust future strategies to
prevent tracking attacks on the C-ITS.

other data that can be linked back to an individual (e.g., credit
card numbers, telephone numbers, biometric data). Privacy is
a critical concern which must be protected in the environment of connected vehicles. Protecting tracking information
is another challenge to preserve consumers’ privacy. In V2V
communications, vehicles use different techniques to share
information and build a cooperative environment between
RSUs and vehicles. This information can be used to track
users. For example, using identification keys, the Tire Pressure
Monitoring System (TPMS) recognizes the car’s tires. Each
tire is connected to an Electronic Control Unit (ECU) inside
the intravehicle network; its ID and pressure are sent to the
corresponding ECU every 6 seconds. Tires are kept in cars
for extended periods, so TPMS messages are traceable and
used to locate the car. In [7], the authors demonstrated that a
vehicle can be traced using the IDs of its two tires. Vehicles
on the road can be recognized only with prior knowledge of
their tire IDs by collecting TPMS status messages.
Most security solutions in C-ITS applications rely on
vehicles periodically transmitting unencrypted Cooperative
Awareness Messages (CAMs). CAMs frequently save timestamps, IDentity (ID), location, and speed. The use of ID raises
the possibility that external eavesdroppers might trace cars
by joining consecutive messages with the same ID, revealing
the vehicle’s whole travel path. To prevent this, researchers
proposed providing cars with pseudonyms (instead of real
identifiers). Vehicles, however, could still be tracked due to
the spatio-temporal information. One solution to overcome
this issue is to use many pseudonyms for each car (each
of which is only used for a limited and/or context-specific
period of time) and switch between them regularly [8], [9].
This does not entirely protect the privacy of tracking information. Many challenges must still be addressed to protect
information, such as the widespread transmission of messages
(10 messages/sec for traffic safety) containing precise position
and time data and the linkability of pseudonymous location

samples with different pseudonyms. If the challenges above
are not addressed, an adversary can track vehicles in the
long term. If the adversary can cover a vast area and collect
a significant number of location samples, the adversary can
develop detailed location profiles. Such a scenario is plausible
as [10] shows and presents a scenario wherein an adversary
collects messages, namely pseudonymous location samples in
a particular area. The adversary could link data under several
pseudonyms for the specific vehicle. Thus, an attacker can
seriously threaten the privacy of the vehicular network user’s
location. Hence, vehicle tracking cannot be prevented even
when pseudonyms are changed [11]. To minimize continuous tracking, some researchers recommended using position
privacy techniques such as random silent periods [12] and
Mix-Zones [13], [14].

these attacks and address the issues highlighted, the paper
presents a complete analysis and classification of the
available defense mechanisms proposed in the literature.
• Finally, we provide some recommendations and future
perspectives for privacy-preserving schemes in C-ITS.

C. Security in Vehicular Networks
The security of the different exchanges in C-ITS has been
addressed by various standardization organizations [46], [47],
[48], [49]. The security services that must be ensured in
C-ITS for the comfort and safety of passengers and drivers
include [20], [50], [51]:
1) Authentication: is recognized as the primary line
of defense against a variety of C-ITS threats [50].
Authentication confirms the identity of communication
participants and protects sensitive information by avoiding any unauthorized access [20]. Authentication is a
critical feature that must be carefully examined during
the design and implementation stages. Key management

14620

and distribution must be accurate and efficient to satisfy
authentication requirements.
2) Integrity: guarantees that the original message is
not altered while being transmitted, preventing the
unauthorized modification, creation, or deletion of
data [52]. It is critical in vehicular networks to
verify that the message is not altered throughout its
transmission, whether accidentally by degradation
factors like fading and noise or intentionally by
an attacker. Error detection/correction codes and/or
cryptographic primitives must be introduced to achieve
this goal. Integrity should also be ensured at the
system level, where each device/component must
operate as intended. System integrity breaches primarily
result from malware injection at various entry points,
including V2X communication interfaces, in-vehicle
networks (e.g., CAN bus), infotainment systems, and
compromised diagnostic tools.
3) Availability: guarantees that such networks and applications continue functioning in the presence of malicious
conditions [53]. Improving the ability to obtain data
by/from vehicles is critical in a highly dynamic C-ITS
environment; the network should be responsive in realtime. Under typical operating conditions, it is difficult
to achieve continuous availability. When a temporary
outage occurs through one part of the network, it is
critical that connectivity activities continue and that
the vehicles remain unaware of any problems. Services
must be provided at all times [54].
4) Data confidentiality: verifies that no unauthorized
entities receive the information. Privacy is often
considered a sub-category of confidentiality, but given
its importance in vehicular networks, a separation is
made between data confidentiality and privacy [55].
In other words, data confidentiality can be ensured
through traditional encryption algorithms, while privacy
requires more complex schemes.
5) Privacy: Privacy generally includes control over
one’s information and deciding who can access or
process one’s data for whatever purpose [56]. In
C-ITS, privacy refers to the ability to protect all the
personal information related to the driver, vehicle, and
passengers, including their identity, location, and so on.
We discuss privacy aspects further in section III-D.
6) Non-repudiation: A vehicle that sent a message should
not be able to deny that it sent that message. Such a
requirement is essential when an exchange can have
financial implications (e.g., requesting a paid service).
Ensuring the security requirements above must address the
inherent characteristics of vehicular networks. Designing
security solutions for such a constrained context remains
a significant challenge. Traditional security algorithms and
protocols must be carefully chosen and adapted. Clean-slate
approaches might sometimes be needed when existing solutions fail to meet the requirements. The reliability of the
messages exchanged in V2X communications is critical, especially when dealing with safety applications. This can be
achieved by ensuring the integrity and authenticity of the

messages using asymmetric techniques and digital signature
algorithms. Recent advances in lightweight cryptography assist
in meeting the real-time constraints of C-ITS. Digital certificates are used, which usually need the deployment of a Public
Key Infrastructure (PKI)1 to manage the different credentials
(i.e., certificates) of the C-ITS entities [63].
The traditional PKI does not address privacy because a
digital certificate contains the identity of its owner in general,
which is not needed to ensure the trustworthiness of a message
in V2X communications. It is sufficient to ensure that a “valid”
participant has transmitted a message to trust it. A privacypreserving certificate (used for digital signature) should not
include any information that can be used as a link to the
identity of a particular vehicle or driver.

D. Privacy in Vehicular Networks
As we previously, privacy is a fundamental security
requirement in vehicular networks; any solution, whether
security-oriented or not, must be privacy-preserving by design.
The privacy requirements in vehicular communication systems
include [20], [64]:
1) Minimum disclosure: Minimize the amount of user information disclosed for basic functionality in vehicular
communications.
2) Anonymity: The message’s origin (i.e., the sender) must
be kept anonymous among possible senders, also known
as the message’s anonymity group. However, anonymity
is conditional on accountability (i.e., non-repudiation),
so trusted authorities should always validate identity
resolution.
3) Unlinkability: It should not be possible to link the
relations between items of interest, such as messages,
actions, and/or subjects. Any information in vehicular
networks becomes privacy-relevant when connected to
an identifiable person. Linkability can be direct or transitive (chain of linkable elements).
4) Distributed resolution authority: In a distributed
resolution system, the authorities work collaboratively
to establish the link between the anonymous credential
and the individual’s identity. The process typically
involves cryptographic techniques, such as zeroknowledge proofs or secure multi-party computation,
to ensure privacy and prevent unauthorized access to
sensitive information.
5) Perfect forward secrecy: Generates new keys for each
session so that if one session key is compromised, the
confidentiality of other sessions is not affected [65].
In C-ITS, when resolving the credential of an identity,
perfect forward secrecy is guaranteed not to expose any
information that would compromise the unlinkability of
additional credentials of the same user.

C-ITS can be classified into three categories [66]:

1) Pseudonym Generation Mechanisms: Several research
works [58], [68] have proposed techniques to generate
pseudonyms. These mechanisms can be either centralized or
distributed.
The authors of [68] proposed a pseudorandom approach
where each user is assigned a pseudorandom ID that
remains anonymous from all other vehicles. A vehicle creates
pseudonyms with the help of an RSU in its neighborhood so
that the RSU does not gain any information about the true
identity of the node. The authors also proposed a revocation
scheme that allows trusted authorities to successfully reveal the
identity of a malicious node and revoke it using revocation lists
in case of any dispute. The pseudorandom keys are generated
using Pseudonymous Authentication with Conditional Privacy
(PACP), a new mechanism proposed by the authors. When
a user registers a vehicle, the user is issued a ticket with a
unique ID used to authenticate the vehicle and generate a
pseudorandom key. While this system is useful, it has a large
overhead and is vulnerable to identity theft because malicious
nodes can copy the ticket obtained from the automobile
department.
In [58], the authors proposed a Secure and Authenticated
Key Management Protocol (SAKMP). SAKMP is a management technology that distributes public keys based on the
user’s geographic position. The key is generated using a
method that ensures each key is distinct and is based on the
position and user’s ID. GPS is used to get the position of
the users. For the method to create unique keys, a 3D location
should be built. The secret is to use the x, y, and z coordinates.
This technique offers a secure connection between the RSU
and the OBU and it depends on the position and a complex
key generation function. Thus, it would be difficult to use in
V2V communications. Like all other symmetric encryptionbased systems, a large number of keys introduces a significant
overhead, particularly for V2V communications. To ensure
secure communication in C-ITS, the authors of [62] proposed
a pseudonym-based authentication technique. They used group

2) Pseudonym Change Mechanisms: Using a single
pseudonym is not enough to protect the vehicle/driver’s privacy. When a vehicle uses one pseudonym certificate during
its lifetime, an adversary who monitors the certificate at
various locations can track its location by linking the different
messages. In other words, pseudonyms can protect identity
privacy but cannot guarantee location privacy.
A key factor in ensuring location privacy is to provide
a vehicle with a batch of pseudonyms and to change the
pseudonym in use frequently. Vehicles should be able to
download a new pseudonyms’ set prior to the exhaustion
of the current set. The authors of [48] provide more details
about the C-ITS security architecture. Most privacy-preserving
schemes adopt this approach but differ in the pseudonym
change strategy.
In [70], the authors proposed a detailed taxonomy of the
different strategies proposed in the literature. Other classifications also exist [69]. Next, we present a simple classification
that presents an overview of well-known pseudonym change
strategies, which we will address later from an attack and
defense perspective.
a) Parameter-based change: vehicles can change the
pseudonyms based on certain parameters (such as time, number of messages, and traveled distance). A parameter can have
a fixed value or a random value (fixed value with a certain
random interval around it). Fig. 5 illustrates an example where
a pseudonym is changed based on the number of messages
signed using that pseudonym. Each vehicle checks if the
messages signed with the current pseudonym are above the
threshold. If so, the pseudonym and station ID are changed,
and messages are sent with the new pseudonym.
b) Context-based change: In a context-based change
strategy, pseudonyms are altered based on specific conditions,
which can be either vehicle-centric or external to the vehicle.
Vehicle-centric conditions refer to factors related to the vehicle
itself, such as its speed exceeding a certain limit, dropping
below a particular threshold, or changing direction. On the

IV. T RACKING ATTACKS
With a market expected to be worth $68.0 billion by
2026, [79], intelligent transportation systems are attracting the
attackers’ attention (regardless of their motives). The research
literature [71], [80], [81] identified many potential attacks that,
while currently theoretical, still pose a risk. However, realworld attacks have occurred in recent years, as noted in [82].
This section focuses on a specific type of ITS attack that

targets the privacy of ITS users’ positions: tracking attacks.
Such attacks target the vehicle’s security and entail tracking
information at different times (generation, processing, transmission) [83]. We can broadly categorize attacks that target
tracking into general attacks and attacks against pseudonym
change mechanisms (e.g., silent periods, mix-zones).
A. General Tracking Attacks
General tracking attacks are those that, in addition to targeting the privacy of the location data, also allow an attacker to
track vehicles. The most widely known attacks in this category
are:
1) Eavesdropping Attacks: Eavesdropping, in general, aims
to steal confidential data. In the context of tracking, it involves
monitoring the wireless network, analyzing the packets or
messages that are transmitted, and examining their contents to
capture the position data and thus be able to track the vehicle’s
movements [67]. For example, the authors of [11] proposed
an anonymous periodic messaging vehicle tracking system in
which the attacker model eavesdrops on all network messages.
While it may be challenging for an outside attacker to cover
the entire network, this model still applies to cases where the
attacker has compromised the deployed RSUs.
2) Sybil Attacks: They are commonly used in peer-topeer networks wherein the attacker has greater control over
the network due to many “fake” users [84]. In C-ITS, in a
Sybil attack, a vehicle pretends to have multiple identities;
it uses these different identities while sending messages to
other vehicles to change the routing path. The receiving
vehicles cannot verify if the received messages are from
one vehicle or multiple vehicles [82]. From a tracking perspective, a Sybil attack could be used to direct traffic in a
specific direction. By sending multiple messages with different

14623

identities, an attacker can trick other vehicles into believing
there is traffic congestion, causing them to reroute to track
them [85], [86].
3) Malware Attacks: Vehicles in C-ITS are often equipped
with infotainment systems that usually come with pre-installed
applications but are open for installing third-party applications
on handheld devices (e.g., tablets and smartphones). This
makes it possible to install malicious software (i.e., malware), which, depending on their type and payload, can pose
significant security threats. Moreover, by exploiting certain
vulnerabilities in the infotainment system, an attacker can gain
access to the Controller Area Network (CAN) bus, which
raises safety concerns for drivers and passengers [87]. Such
attacks have been demonstrated in production vehicles as
in [88]. As in traditional computer networks, some malware
can propagate to other “vulnerable” vehicles connected to
the infected one. In a tracking attack, malware (e.g., adware,
spyware) can be used to steal location information, thereby
allowing the attacker to track the victim’s vehicle [54].
4) Correlation Attacks: A correlation attack is a type of
general attack against anonymity services that can be applied
to vehicular networks [89]. This attack involves eavesdropping
to determine the location of a station by measuring the
Radio Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI) of frames received
from the station. Frequent MAC address broadcasts give
sufficient temporal and spatial tracking precision [80]. The
station uses a periodic pseudonym update strategy similar to
that defined in the Bluetooth anonymity mode [71]. Bluetooth anonymity mode helps prevent position tracking by
frequently changing the active device address. However, given
sufficient temporal and spatial accuracy, an attacker could
correlate two pseudonyms broadcasted at different times by
the same vehicle. As the time between stations changing their
pseudonyms may be short, the temporal correlation could be
used. If the station continues in the same direction and at
the same speed as it did previously, spatial correlation can be
used. Correlation attacks get simpler as the distance between
vehicles increases, the speed of a vehicle decreases, and the
precision of determining its location increases. For instance,
the authors of [90] proposed a new MAC layer linking attack
that can correlate a vehicle’s old and new pseudonyms by
studying its transmission properties.
B. Attacks Against Parameter-Based Change Mechanisms
In [10], the authors considered the case of a time-based
pseudonym change where vehicles change their pseudonyms
every 10 seconds. They proposed a model where an
attacker can collect inter-vehicle communication messages,
notably pseudonymous position data. They investigated
how effectively an attacker can create location profiles and
demonstrated that he/she can monitor vehicles and their
drivers with near-perfect precision.
In [11], the authors proposed a different tracking technique called Nearest Neighbor Probabilistic Data Association
(NNPDA). Even with dense networks and numerous CAMs,

V. D EFENSE S CHEMES AGAINST T RACKING ATTACKS
This section presents anti-tracking methods for vehicular
networks (Table IV). We classify them using the same classification criteria for the attacks we discussed in section IV.
A. Defense Against General Attacks
Vehicular networks can be protected against general attacks
used for tracking purposes using the same “general” defense
mechanisms. For instance, eavesdropping can be prevented
by enforcing the confidentiality of sensitive data through
encryption. Sybil attacks can be thwarted by implementing
strong authentication mechanisms. In this section, we present
defense solutions that are more “customized” for vehicular
networks.
1) Defense Against Eavesdropping Attacks: Cui et al. [94]
proposed a privacy-preserving approach for car location utilizing the concepts of k-anonymity and path confusion. Cars can
create dynamic virtual locations in response to their surroundings to deceive information about their driving path, thereby
enhancing privacy. They employed the entropy of anonymity
set (a measure of uncertainty in identifying a specific car
among a collection of vehicles; a higher entropy denotes more
confidentiality) and tracking success ratio (a lower tracking
success ratio suggests that the privacy-preserving scheme is
effective at preventing accurate tracking of vehicles) as criteria
to determine the degree of privacy. The results showed that
their proposed scheme increased the entropy of the anonymity
set by 100% when compared with other schemes while
decreasing the tracking ratio to 1%.
The authors of [95] developed a privacy-preserving location
strategy that utilizes a filter to differentiate between real and
fake messages, incorporating the concept of Chaff messages.
The Chaff technique helps protect the content of transmitted
messages from eavesdroppers by adding fake data, making
it more difficult to trace or link messages to their original

senders. However, the authors noted that this approach could
still be susceptible to link attacks, potentially compromising
system security. They emphasized that message authentication
and sender identity verification improvements are essential for
safety applications to address these vulnerabilities effectively.
2) Defense Against Sybil Attacks: The authors of [96]
proposed a system for detecting and tracking sybil vehicles
through a collaborative effort among RSUs. In their approach,
three RSUs employ triangulation to determine node positions,
which are then shared between RSUs to check for conflicting
locations and identify any malicious (i.e., sybil) vehicles. They
can detect the trajectory and various identities of sybil vehicles
by creating a list of detected vehicles and tracking their
pattern of presence in each RSU’s coverage zone. Furthermore,
RSUs validate messages against sybil nodes and use digital
signatures to reduce the impact of false messages in the
network.
In [97], the authors proposed an intrusion detection system
that uses a signature-based approach and the plausibility concept for detecting fake car movement information. The concept
of plausibility [98] involves evaluating the reliability of vehicle
movement information based on various factors, such as speed,
direction, location, or behavior. By comparing the observed
data with the signatures or motion patterns of the original
vehicle, the system can determine if the information is likely
to be fake or has been manipulated. The intrusion detection
system can detect false congestion attacks (sybil attacks) and
denial of service attacks, even when a valid movement is used.
This technique can identify a single fake car.
In [99], the authors developed a presence evidence system to detect Sybil attacks using position verification. They
employed a RANdom SAmple Consensus (RANSAC)-based
method [100] to improve the car’s location estimation by
analyzing the car’s signal-strength distribution. RANSAC is
an outlier detection method commonly used in computer
vision. The presence evidence system determines the estimated
location of a claimer car that sends a beacon message at a
beacon interval for adjacent discovery.
B. Protecting Pseudonym Change Schemes
The authors of [83] proposed random encryption periods in
mix-zone. Every OBU is given a global symmetric key, that it
uses to enter an encrypted zone where a certificate update for
pseudonym change is executed. To avoid semantic connection,
which refers to the ability to link old and new pseudonyms
based on identifiable patterns such as vehicle speed, direction,
or behavior, this strategy requires cars to change lanes, direction, or speed while altering their pseudonyms, which makes
it hard to achieve this scheme in practice. Since the technique
permits any OBU to terminate encryption periods, an internal
adversary might utilize this to diminish the anonymity set,
putting user privacy at risk. Furthermore, the technique adds
significant complexity to key management: for each revocation
activity, a novel symmetric key must be provided to all legal
cars in the network.
1) Defense Against Correlation Attacks: The authors
of [101] proposed a vehicular PKI-based system that protects

user privacy by avoiding the linking of pseudonyms according
to pseudonym timing data. However, a new Media Access
Control (MAC) transmission correlated attack (an attack
that exploits patterns in MAC layer transmission to correlate
transmissions from the same vehicle) [90] can still be launched
to compromise location privacy. The authors of [90] proposed
a MAC layer Attack Resistant Pseudonym (MARP) technique
for beacon broadcasting in vehicular networks. Initially, they
identified the new MAC layer context linking attack enabled
by inconsistent pseudonym changes and periodic MAC layer
operations used in this technique and then examined the
attack using both Carrier Sense Multiple Access (CSMA) and
Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA) based protocols.
With MARP, vehicles change their pseudonyms and time
slots by negotiating with their neighbors to build mix
zones depending on their current pseudonym age. Vehicles
re-arrange their transmission slots at random when changing
pseudonyms to remain unlinkable to the adversaries. The
time is utilized for communication between vehicles and
RSUs or Trusted Authorities (TAs). This communication may
involve requesting pseudonyms or receiving the Certificate
Revocation List (CRL) from the TA. When a mix-zone
is built, vehicles become unidentifiable by cooperatively
releasing their transmission slots and old pseudonyms. In this
way, the pseudonyms are rendered unlikable.
2) Collaborative Protection Schemes: Based on real-time
variables such as traffic density, vehicle speed, and infrastructure availability, the author of [102] proposed a new
Pseudonym Change Scheme (PCS) that maximizes when,
where, and how frequently vehicles change their pseudonyms.
The problems associated with existing PCSs include predictable triggers (such as pre-defined time intervals, events that
occur at regular intervals, or fixed-distance-based triggers) that
jeopardize privacy, reliance on radio silence, and the depletion
of pseudonyms. They recommended using traffic patterns and
vehicle environments to overcome these challenges, allowing for simultaneous pseudonym changes with unpredictable,
dynamic triggers to optimize anonymity. The main contributions of [102] include an on-demand pseudonym generation
and authentication mechanism to prevent depletion, a three-tier
architecture for coordinating changes within a Road Side Unit
(RSU) coverage area, and a context-aware PCS that removes
the need for radio silence while avoiding predictable triggers.
The authors of [103] proposed two pseudonym exchange algorithms to enhance vehicle privacy by preventing adversaries
from linking a vehicle’s pseudonyms over time. The TriggerBased Exchange Algorithm initiates pseudonym exchanges
based on specific conditions, such as entering high-traffic
areas or detecting tracking attempts. Once initiated, the vehicle
sends a request to a RSU, which validates the request and
forwards it to the certificate authority for processing. The
RSU-Assisted Exchange Algorithm requires vehicles to submit
pseudonym exchange requests to the RSU, which authenticates
the requests and coordinates with the Certificate authority to
enable secure information exchange. Both algorithms sought
to make pseudonym exchanges unpredictable, thereby improving privacy while reducing the computational and storage
burden on vehicles and the certificate authority. Simulation

14627

results showed that these methods significantly enhance privacy compared to traditional pseudonym-changing schemes.
In [104], the authors proposed a pseudonym-based scheme
that integrates pseudonym-swapping and pseudonym-changing
strategies to enhance location privacy for connected car users.
This approach employed two types of pseudonyms: nonswappable and swappable, enabling secure, RSU-independent
pseudonym swaps while addressing revocation failures and
reputation attacks. During registration, each vehicle receives
one non-swappable pseudonym and several swappable ones.
Swappable pseudonyms are used for signing and authenticating beacon messages, while non-swappable ones enable
secure exchanges without RSUs. After pseudonym swaps,
vehicles report their updated vehicle-pseudonym mapping to
the certification authority via a secure transport protocol upon
connecting to the network. In [105], the authors expanded
this work by integrating both pseudonym change and swap
strategies, removing the need for preloaded pseudonyms and
utilizing reusable long-term certificates. This revision enabled
RSU-independent pseudonym swapping while still adhering
to VANET security requirements. Like the [104] approach,
vehicles are assigned both non-swappable and swappable
pseudonyms, using the former for secure exchanges and the
latter for signing messages. After each pseudonym swap,
vehicles report the updated pseudonyms to the certification
authority, enhancing the system’s security and privacy.
3) Protecting Silent Period Based Schemes: The organized location-independent radio silences, which are related
to the notions of random silent periods [71] and Swing &
Swap [106], allow vehicles to stop transmitting CAMs for an
arbitrary period of time before altering their pseudonyms.
The concept of silent period [71] (that we discussed in
section III-D.2 causes vehicles to stop sending any locationrelated information while changing the pseudonyms to add
ambiguity to the identification of the location and time of a
pseudonym change). In the initial model for the silent period,
the duration of the silent period is fixed for all cars (i.e.,
the constant silent period). The tracking technique involves
comparing the order of the Emerging Times (ET) (the earliest
time when the tracking system monitored a specific car with
its new pseudonym) of two cars with their new pseudonyms to
the Disappearing Times DT (the latest time when the tracking
system monitored a specific car with its old pseudonym) of
those with their old pseudonyms. The assumption is that if a
car disappears with an old pseudonym and re-emerges with
a new pseudonym, it is the same car. As Fig 6 shows, the
technique described in this figure assumes that the time a
car reaches a silent period is a reliable indicator of its ET.

the silent period duration is variable. If a car with identity

not be accurate if the duration of the silent period is not
fixed and can vary from car to car. For example, if A has a
shorter silent period duration than B, it may emerge earlier
than B even if its emerging time is later. To address this
issue, the authors of [71] proposed using a variable length

still be cases where the tracking system cannot link a detected
pseudonym to the correct car. For example, if the range of the
emerging times for two cars overlaps, the system may not be
able to distinguish between them based solely on the timing
of their pseudonyms. In such cases, additional information or
context may be needed to correctly identify the car associated
with a particular pseudonym. The silent period is determined
by a random variable varying between [T 2min , T 2max ] as
Fig. 11 shows. When the ranges of the emerging times of two
cars overlap, issues such as misidentification or ambiguity in
associating the pseudonym with the correct vehicle may arise.
These challenges could lead to incorrect tracking or difficulty
in linking the pseudonym to the right car. In this scenario, the
temporal relation between the ET and DT of the two cars may
become obscure, making it difficult for the tracking technique
to link the old pseudonym of a car to its new one. To address
this challenge, the authors recommend that the silent period
used for tracking should include both constant and variable
periods. The constant period helps to mix the spatial relation
between a car’s emerging and disappearing points whereas the
variable period helps to mix the temporal relation between
the car’s DT and ET. The use of a constant period helps
to mix the spatial relation between the car’s emerging and
disappearing points by adding noise to the location data. This
can help prevent an adversary from accurately identifying the
car’s path based on the sequence of its pseudonyms. The
use of a variable period helps to mix the temporal relation
between the car’s disappearing time and emerging time by
randomizing the duration of the silent period. This can prevent
an adversary from accurately inferring the car’s emerging time
based on the timing of its pseudonym. By including both
constant and variable periods in the silent period, the tracking
technique makes it more difficult for an adversary to link the
old pseudonym of a car to its new one, even in cases where the

ranges of their ETs overlap. However, it is important to note
that no tracking technique is perfect, and there may still be
cases where an adversary can link pseudonyms to individual
cars.
The authors of [107] demonstrated how to change
a pseudonym successfully and developed an effective
pseudonym-changing technique to strengthen users’ location
privacy in C-ITS. They developed a unique tracking attack
known as the cheating attack, which is designed to undermine the performance of the suggested pseudonym change
approach. In a cheating attack, the attacker creates CAMs with
the change flag set to “1” to trick other cars into thinking it
wants to alter its pseudonym when it actually does not. As a
result, a car searching for the trigger considers the attacker
among the discovered neighbors and changes the pseudonym.
Table III demonstrates how normal and malicious cars react
to a change in the pseudonym. The normal car enters a silent
period and changes its pseudonym, whereas the malicious car
(cheater) does the opposite, making it always linkable. This
behavior decreases the anonymous set size and makes the
linking of pseudonyms easier.
To counteract this threat, they proposed a cheating detection
system to identify hostile cheating vehicles.
Some approaches use a silent period jointly with other
mechanisms to ensure vehicles’ location privacy and thus
counter-tracking attacks. Sampigethaya et al. [12] addressed
the issue of permitting any vehicle to establish unlinkability
between two or more of its locations in the presence of
adversary tracking. Unlike prior location privacy approaches
in mobile networks, the authors studied the limitations provided by vehicular mobility and vehicular applications within
vehicular networks. Specifically, they examined how the movement patterns and operational constraints of vehicles, such as
limited control over routing decisions and physical mobility,
affect the feasibility and effectiveness of location privacy
solutions. They developed a solution that accounts for these
limitations and effectively protects location privacy. They
concluded that vehicle group navigation can provide location
privacy in C-ITS, and to mitigate vehicle tracking, they present
CARAVAN. This location privacy approach combines group
navigation with a random silent time augmentation technique.
The author of [108] proposed a new algorithm for managing pseudonym certificate switching periods between cars to
prevent trackers from obtaining drivers’ traces. The proposed
algorithm executes a short time using a common pseudonym
certificate before switching to a new one. During this time,
cars sign their messages on the shared pseudonym certificate.
The proposed algorithm guarantees that as many vehicles as

possible change their pseudonym certificates during the same

4) Protecting Mix-Zones Schemes: One possible real-time
location privacy strategy in transportation systems is the mix
zone. This method prevents users from tracking attacks by
disrupting the continuity of position disclosure and altering
pseudonyms. The authors of [76] proposed the MixGroup
strategy, which includes social locations and other events
where a car can face many other cars. The cars in the MixGroup communicate with one another to decide if they want
to participate in the pseudonym change procedure. In [77], the
authors developed an alternative method to construct mix-zone
wherein cars build the mix-zone using the radio silence method
if their speeds remain under a threshold point for a specific
period. Several current techniques [92], [94] study the location
and the timing for the pseudonym change. The authors of [78]
focused on devising techniques for pseudonym change with
dynamically determined mix zones. They used the age of
the pseudonym as the most important feature in changing
pseudonyms. If the pseudonym age exceeds some threshold,
it attempts to change its pseudonym. The authors of [109]
proposed a Cryptographic MIX-zone (CMIX) approach for
providing location privacy at road intersections. The RSU
securely distributes a symmetric key to any incoming car in
CMIX to create a mix zone. That symmetric key encrypts
all information exchanged in a mix-zone. Furthermore, all
cars in the mix-zone must alter their pseudonym certificates.
Due to the forced certificate change and the random direction
change of every car at crossing points, an adversary on the
roadside cannot link a certificate to a specific car thereby
providing location privacy. The aggregation of CMIX over
the vehicular network creates a mix network, which improves
location privacy.
The authors of [110] proposed a Pseudonym Change
Protocol (PCP) to enhance vehicle location privacy. This
protocol enables vehicles to independently generate legitimate
pseudonym certificates without the assistance of Road
Side Units (RSUs). Additionally, vehicles can judge the
circumstances for changing a pseudonym and get the required
information through V2V communication, thereby enhancing
the security of the pseudonym change process.
In [108] the authors proposed a new pseudonym-change
algorithm. In particular, a vehicle obtains a pool of Pseudonym
Certificates (PC) and a Common Pseudonym Certificate (CPC)
that is utilized by all cars after it has registered with a Trusted
Authority (TA). A car switches its pseudonym certificate when
it encounters one or more other vehicles while traveling. The
authors of this paper proposed a protocol that allows several
cars to switch PCs simultaneously while traveling close to one
another; however, there may be some delays. Before upgrading
their pseudonyms, all of the nearby cars briefly utilize the
CPC. In [95], the authors developed a privacy-preserving
location strategy that utilizes a filter to differentiate between
real and fake messages, incorporating the concept of Chaff
messages. However, the authors noted that this approach
could still be susceptible to link attacks. To get complete

unlinkability, the authors of [111] demonstrated the following
security and privacy flaws in [95]: attackers may insert fake

the insertion may be denied. To solve these challenges, the
authors of [111] developed a Vehicular Public-Key Infrastructure (VPKI) approach. First, they used a modified version of

without disclosing the real identity. Each vehicle’s messages

entire filter. Additionally, only RSUs send updates and sign
fresh fingerprints to prevent any modifications to the filter.
The authors of [113] proposed a privacy security mechanism
that relies on altering pseudonyms based on the car’s speed
and position within or outside established mix-zones. The
results obtained demonstrate that the proposed approach is
an effective mechanism for protecting car drivers’ location
information.
VI. F UTURE D IRECTIONS
The future of privacy in connected cars depends on the
adoption of multi-layered security techniques that integrate
decentralized systems, AI, privacy-preserving communication
protocols, and cryptographic innovations. Strong, secure systems that can prevent tracking while maintaining the benefits
of connected and autonomous mobility could be developed
using emerging technologies like blockchain, Federated Learning (FL), and quantum encryption. Collaboration between
technology companies, manufacturers, and government regulators will be crucial to ensuring that these concepts are feasible,
scalable, and widely accepted.
A. Federated Learning (FL)
Federated learning is a cutting-edge Machine-Learning
(ML) technique that enables linked vehicles to jointly build
a common prediction model while maintaining the privacy
and decentralization of their data. This technique holds great
promise for improving security and privacy in connected
automotive systems, especially when it comes to preventing
tracking [117], [118]. Federated learning allows models to be
trained across multiple vehicles or Intrusion Detection Systems
(IDS) without the need to share raw data, thus mitigating
the privacy issues associated with conventional centralized
learning methods. More precisely, in federated learning, the
model is trained locally on each vehicle’s data. Only the model
updates (called gradients) are sent to a central server. Data is
aggregated later to improve a global model, which could be
an interesting solution that complies with the General Data
Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the European Union. The
benefits of Federated Learning for connected vehicles can be
summarized as follows:
• Decentralized data processing: Instead of sending data to
a central server, federated learning keeps the data on the
individual vehicles. This minimizes the possibility that
unauthorized parties will intercept or track sensitive data,
such as the exact locations or driving patterns.

• Reduced risk of data breaches: Federated learning does

• Deployment of federated learning: Deploying federated

a robust, flexible approach that guarantees efficiency,

updates whenever a vehicles establishes a connection.
It employs adaptive weighting to prioritize contributions
from reliable sources. Privacy protection is enhanced
through Differential Privacy (DP), which, rather than
adding excessive artificial noise, leverages inherent communication noise. This approach dynamically fine-tunes
Differential Privacy (DP) mechanisms to enhance security
while preserving model performance. Gradient sparsification, quantization, and secure aggregation further
increase efficiency while reducing communication costs
and maintaining privacy. To handle data heterogeneity, vehicles also maintain personalized models, while
Roadside Units (RSUs) compile information to enhance
global updates. This guarantees a learning system that is
more robust and flexible. Ultimately, this comprehensive
strategy enables the deployment of privacy-preserving,
communication-efficient, and robust federated learning in
C-ITS, even in environments with variable connectivity.
Choosing the right framework is crucial for achieving
optimal model outcomes in federated learning. There
are numerous tools and platforms for federated learning

B. Quantum Encryption
Quantum encryption presents an innovative way of protecting communications in connected cars, addressing the pressing
need for strong privacy protection. Quantum encryption can
significantly reduce tracking concerns by providing unbreakable security, guaranteeing anonymity, and promoting collaborative intelligence without sacrificing privacy [127], [128].
• Secure communication channels: Quantum encryption
enables the safe creation and exchange of cryptographic
keys between vehicles and the infrastructure. With the
help of quantum key distribution, vehicles may create
secure channels for transmitting sensitive data (such as
position or navigation information) without running the
risk of being intercepted or tracked.
• Collaborative learning with security: Quantum encryption can enable collaborative ML among vehicles while
ensuring that individual data contributions remain private.
Vehicles can securely share insights (e.g., traffic conditions, hazards) without exposing their exact locations
or personal information. Future research could explore
integrating post-quantum cryptographic protocols with
existing vehicular networks to build resilient systems that
can withstand not only conventional cyber-attacks but also
potential quantum-based threats. This would create safer,
more effective, and privacy-preserving vehicle networks
by paving the way for a new generation of intelligent
transportation systems that are highly collaborative and
inherently secure.
• Integration of Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) in
connected vehicles ecosystem: Addressing cybersecurity
risks posed by future quantum attacks is essential as
we enter the era of quantum computing. The Internet
of Vehicles (IoV) must implement cryptographic protocols aligned with the Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC)
standards set by the National Institute of Standards
and Technology (NIST) to ensure long-term security
[129], [130]. PQC currently relies on lattice-based cryptography, which uses complex mathematical structures
formed by combining sets of vectors in large spaces.
NIST has recently published encryption and signature
standards designed to withstand quantum attacks, including FIPS 203 (Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation
Mechanism for encryption), FIPS 204 (Module-LatticeBased Digital Signature Algorithm for digital signatures),
and FIPS 205 (Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature
Algorithm for hash-based signatures). To strengthen the
security of the connected vehicle ecosystem, we must

C. Integration of Blockchain for Decentralized Privacy
Management
Blockchain provides a strong foundation for improving privacy in networks of connected cars. Many of the vulnerabilities
that permit tracking attacks can be fixed by their decentralized,
transparent, and secure nature, enabling intelligent transportation systems that are safer and more reliable [131], [132].
• Balancing audibility and privacy: Blockchain could be
employed to create immutable audit trails that record
the issuance and usage of vehicle identifiers, helping to
ensure transparency and privacy compliance. To prevent
setting up a new tracking channel, this must be done
carefully. Future research must examine how anonymity
might be maintained while preventing unauthorized tracking by utilizing blockchain’s transparency.
• Combining mix-zones and pseudonym changes with
blockchain: Traditional methods of preventing tracking
include mix-zones and changing the pseudonym. By documenting the times and locations of pseudonym changes,
blockchain can increase transparency by making sure that
these changes are coordinated and unpredictable, which
makes tracking more challenging. Future research must
examine how blockchain technology might be integrated
with current privacy procedures. This would allow for
the safe and random changing of pseudonyms without
disclosing the true identities of the cars involved.
• Accountability without centralized control: Current centralized systems can suffer from data breaches or misuse
by the central authority itself. Blockchain makes it more
difficult for any one party to abuse data for tracking
by enabling a decentralized method wherein no single
entity controls the entire network. Decentralized oversight
techniques, in which the network as a whole cooperatively enforces privacy regulations, could be explored
and implemented in future systems. Any attempt by an
entity to violate these guidelines (for example, by tracking
cars without authorization) would be documented on the
blockchain, and appropriate remedial measures may be
initiated immediately.
VII. C ONCLUSION
C-ITS is a critical component in modernizing and improving
transportation systems. It leverages advanced communication
technologies to enable vehicles, infrastructure, and users to
exchange real-time information. However, several essential
concerns of security, such as confidentiality, availability, and
privacy, must be addressed to ensure safety in C-ITS. This
paper presents an in-depth survey of recent research on
privacy, particularly regarding vehicle tracking, highlighting
current threats and challenges. We analyzed and classified
existing tracking defense solutions that have been discussed

and proposed in the current literature. Additionally, we recommended future directions to mitigate tracking attacks. Future
research on connected vehicles should adopt multi-layered
security strategies integrating decentralized systems, artificial
intelligence, and cryptographic innovations. Emerging technologies such as blockchain, Federated Learning (FL), and
quantum encryption can enhance privacy protection while
maintaining the benefits of connected mobility. We hope this
survey provides valuable insights for researchers working on
privacy and vehicle tracking within C-ITS.
