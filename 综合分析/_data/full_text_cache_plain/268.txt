Multipattern Integrated Networks With Contrastive
Pretraining for Graph Anomaly Detection
Manzhi Yang , Jian Zhang , Liyuan Lin , Jinpeng Han , Student Member, IEEE, Xiaoguang Chen ,
Zhen Wang , and Fei-Yue Wang , Fellow, IEEE

Abstract—As a challenge of practical significance, fraud detection has great potential for telecom fraud prevention, economic crime prevention, and personal property preservation.
Fraudulent activities are always buried in massive regular
transactions, making it hard to find them. Traditional rule-based
approaches need multiple domain-specific rules and multistep
verification, which limits their transferability and efficiency.
Machine learning-based methods might ignore the intricate interactions or the temporal relations among accounts. Meanwhile,
the lack of sufficient manual labels restricts their performance.
To overcome the above limitations, we present a multipattern
integrated network (MPIN) in this article to identify fraudulent
accounts in transaction networks. Specifically, MPIN considers
the interactions among nodes from three perspectives: inflows,
outflows, and their mutual influences. To learn the behavior
pattern of each node, MPIN first applies an attention mechanism
to integrate the short-term information and then learns the
long-term patterns by aggregating multiple short-term patterns.
Behavior patterns from different perspectives together with long
short-term modeling enable the model to precisely distinguish
fraudulent accounts from the normal ones. Moreover, contrastive pretraining with temporal consistency and local tightness
guarantee is adopted to alleviate the label sparsity issue and
provide the model with low-variance performance. We conducted
experiments on two real-world transaction networks, and the
results showed the effectiveness of MPIN compared with five
state-of-the-art baselines.

Fraudulent activities pose a multifaceted threat, socially eroding

The critical importance of fraud detection lies in its ability

private and public sectors. Hence, it is of practical significance
to find out those who are involved in fraudulent cases to eliminate financial losses and even prevent fraud from happening

effectiveness. Practitioners summarize fraudulent patterns and
then apply heuristic rules to filter out fraudulent activities as

rule-based detection systems are explainable and can greatly
save the cost of manual reviews. Benefiting from their effectiveness and simplicity, rule-based approaches still play important roles in industrial scenarios. For instance, in banking
transactions, transactions that involve abnormal amounts of

normal communication records. It is hard for a bunch of
handcraft-designed rules to find the fraud. Despite the above

After collecting a large amount of data from online services,

Recently, some researchers proposed to model the transactions
as networks and argued that exploiting the network structure
could help with fraud detection [9]. For example, a bunch
of graph-based algorithms is proposed to detect credit card
frauds either in real-world scenarios [10] or in cryptocurrency
transactions [11].
Although great efforts have been devoted to fraud detection,
it is still challenging for real-world applications due to the
following reasons.
1) Label scarcity and class imbalance: Machine learningbased models often require sufficient fraud labels to ensure performance, while fraud labels are always scarce
in real-world scenarios. In telecom fraud detection, most
fraudulent phone numbers are recognized based on reports from victims, and so are the fraudsters in other
scenarios. Such manual annotation is costly and laborconsuming in real-world applications. At the same time,
fraud labels are extremely unbalanced since fraudsters
only account for a small percentage of regular accounts.
As a result, the lack of sufficient and balanced labels
makes it difficult to train classification models in a supervised manner, which may lead to problems such as
over-fitting and unstable performances [1].
2) Group collaboration and temporal correlation: Fraudsters in practical scenarios do not commit fraud separately. They often work as a team, which makes the
fraudulent patterns intricate and implicit. In telecom

frauds, several phone numbers call one victim to make
up a specific scenario for the victim to commit fraud.
Similarly, chainlike transaction in financial scenarios
is also a pattern of group collaboration. Besides, such
collaborations are not finished immediately but form temporal correlation, which could not be characterized by
static networks.
To address the above challenges, we propose a multipattern integrated network (MPIN) to identify fraudulent accounts
purely based on network structures. Modeling the interactions
between entities as a temporal network, MPIN pays attention to
the directions of entity interactions and considers three different
views, i.e., inflows, outflows, and their mutual influences, to
learn interaction patterns. Inflows of a node are the interactions
initialed by other nodes, while outflows of a node represent the
interactions started by the node on its initiative. Incoming calls
and outgoing calls could be the representatives of inflows and
outflows. Meanwhile, we assume that each in-edge of a node is
associated with its out-edges and vice versa. An intuitive example is that the payment of an account is direct. Therefore, the
mutual influence of inflows and outflows should be considered
when modeling node interaction patterns. Furthermore, considering the dynamics of the network evolving, we divide the
whole network into snapshots at a given timespan Δt and merge
the short-term behavior patterns into long-term patterns. To
compensate for the lack of sufficient supervised signals caused
by label scarcity and class imbalance, contrastive pretraining
with two pretext tasks is designed to enable the model to learn
more informative representations by self-discrimination from
massive unlabeled data. The pretext tasks are designed based
on two assumptions: temporal consistency that the change of
time window has little influence on one’s behavior pattern; and
local tightness that individuals with similar interaction patterns
should be gathered. In summary, our main contributions are
listed as follows.
1) We propose an end-to-end framework, namely MPIN, to
identify the anomalous nodes in graphs. Jointly considering the directions of node interactions and temporal
correlations between them, the framework learns the long
short-term node behavior patterns for fraud detection.
2) Contrastive pretraining with two pretext tasks is
implemented to initialize model parameters by selfdiscrimination. The pretraining process, which ensures
temporal consistency and local tightness, effectively
alleviates the label scarcity problem and paves the way
for behavior pattern learning with better generalization.
3) We conduct experiments on two networks extracted from
real-world scenarios, i.e., telecommunication and cryptocurrency platforms. The experimental results show the
superior performance of the MPIN compared with the
several state-of-the-art baselines. Then, the ablation studies verify the effectiveness of each model component.
Finally, elaborate analysis and visualization shed light on
the effectiveness of the MPIN.

II. RELATED WORK
This section briefly reviews the research on fraud detection,
graph representation learning, and contrastive learning.
A. Fraud Detection
Frauds could be considered as a kind of anomaly in realworld scenarios such as credit card transactions [12], telecommunication, and news spreading [13]. Machine learning-based
methods focusing on fraud detection could roughly be divided
into feature classification [14], sequence analysis [15], and
graph-based models [16]. In credit card fraud detection, features such as transaction ID and transaction amount should be
extracted before applying machine learning methods [14], [17].
Fraud detection is a typical imbalanced classification problem.
Han et al. [18], [19] applied multimodal multiobjective optimization to detect credit card fraud. Noisy-sample-removed
under-sampling scheme (NUS) [20] to improve classification
performance in imbalanced datasets by removing noisy samples and using logistics regression, decision tree, and random
forest as base classifiers. Weighted extreme learning machine
[21] could also be used to solve the problem. Frauds could
also be detected based on sequence feature learning. DIFM
[22] captures information on field value variations and field
interactions of user activities on e-commerce platforms with
a factorization machine. The importance-aware module makes
the model an explainable one for fraud detection. Besides,
there are also many other researches [23], [24] attempt to identify frauds based on time-series features. Graph-based fraud
detection methods model the interaction between entities as
graphs and then find graph anomalies. For example, GEM
[25] constructs heterogeneous graphs of devices and activities and applies an attention-based graph neural network to
detect malicious accounts. HGAT [26] leverages high-order
graph attention representations for loan risk assessment. Focusing on loan frauds, GRC [27] integrates a role-constrained
conditional random field into a graph neural network to make
use of role information and aims to detect collusive fraud in
consumer loans.
B. Graph Representation Learning
Graph representation learning tries to map nodes in networks
from sparse high-dimensional space to dense lower dimensional
representations, making it possible to apply machine learning
models in subsequent tasks. Starting from random walk-based
methods such as DeepWalk [28] and node2vec [29] to graph
neural networks with the representative of GCN [30], GAT [31],
and GraphSage [32], a vast of research has been devoted to
learning expressive node representations on static networks. A
bunch of models developed upon GCN such as cluter-GCN
[33] and deepGCN [34] were explored to achieve fast network

5621

structure learning or deepen the model for more expressive
representations.
To date, dynamic networks also have gained much more
research interests recently [35], [36]. Temporal walk [37],
[38] extends random walk to dynamic networks to learn both
structural and temporal information from node sequences in
chronological order. Considering the dynamics of the network
evolving, DyRep [39] models two kinds of events as two interleaved Hawkees processes and learns node embedding with an
attention mechanism. Other methods [40], [41] that focus on
discrete dynamic networks are also developed to meet different
practical needs. Though the above algorithms are effective in
their fields, the latent anomalous behavior patterns are still hard
to capture. Moreover, the lack of enough labeled data further
makes it harder for them to make precise judgments.
C. Contrastive Learning
As a kind of self-supervised learning, contrastive learning
captures internal data features by maximizing the agreement of
similar samples without labels. With the success in computer vision [42], [43] and natural language processing [44], contrastive
learning is also introduced into graph representation learning.
Following the idea of Deep InfoMax [45], DGI [46] maximizes
the mutual information between patch representations and corresponding high-level summaries of graphs to learn graph representations. Improvement on heterogeneous networks [47] and
temporal networks [48] based on DGI are proposed for specific
scenarios. The main challenge for contrastive learning is to
construct proper positive samples. GraphCL [49] summarizes
four types of graph data augmentation, which shows their effectiveness in GNN pretraining. CoLA [50] adopts subgraph
embeddings as the positive sample for target node to learn node
representations. Graph representation in another latent space
could also serve as positive samples [51]. CG3 [52] utilizes both
localized and hierarchical graph convolutions to generate multiview graph representations and then implements contrastive
learning to train the model. Many other researchers [46], [53]
also employ contrastive learning on graphs to avoid over-fitting
and obtain more robust representations.

C. Learning With Contrastive Pretraining
Traditional supervised learning requires large amounts of
labeled data to ensure fair performance. However, fraudulent
accounts are hard to annotate in real-world applications. Thus,
we propose to adopt contrastive learning to pretrain the foregoing behavior pattern extraction module, and the procedure
is presented in Fig. 3. The goal of contrastive pretraining is to
make sure that each representation is distinguishable compared
with the others, and thus the model could learn distinct behavior
patterns. Following the general scheme of contrastive learning,
we need to find a positive sample, soft positive samples, and
negative samples for the target node at first.
1) Positive Sample: Under the assumption that the behavior
patterns of one account observed under different Δt should be
identical, we change Δt to Δt to obtain a new graph sequence
SG  . To increase the local structure diversity of accounts, we
further randomly remove β |ET | transactions from G  where
β ∈ (0, 1) represents the pruning rate. Based on SG  , we could
obtain h , which can regarded as the positive sample of h, for
each account following the above behavior pattern extraction
procedure.

A. Dataset
Though fraud detection is of practical significance, realworld labeled data are hard to obtain. Previous research either
manually inject anomalies into networks or regard the nodes
belonging to the minority class as anomalies. Such anomaly
definition might differ from the problems in practice. In this
article, we evaluate the proposed method on the following two
real-world datasets and one synthetic dataset, and their statistics
are summarized in Table I.
1) Telecom: Telecom is a dynamic network that consists
of 20 581 telecom records among 11 427 phone numbers. The telecom records range from 25 August 2022 to
30 September 2022 and 116 phone numbers are labeled
as fraudsters.
2) Ether [11]: Ether is one of the largest cryptocurrency
in the world second to Bitcoin. The network consists of
26 370 accounts and involves 50 998 ether transactions
between 1 January 2018 and 30 June 2018. In the network, 684 accounts are labeled as phishers which are
regarded as anomalous nodes.
3) Syn-Ether: We manually inject anomalous structures into
the Ether transaction network following. Specifically, following [56], we randomly select m accounts in the transaction network and then connect each pair of accounts
with virtual transactions if they have no history of transaction. It makes the accounts form a fully connected
anomalous structure, and the m accounts are regarded
as fraudulent accounts. We repeat the injection process k
times and finally obtain mk virtual fraudulent accounts.
In this article, we set m = 10 and k = 20.
In contrastive pretraining, every node within the input graph
is involved in the pretraining process, eliminating the need to
separate nodes into training and test sets. However, for supervised fine-tuning, we categorize the nodes into training and test
sets in a 4:1 ratio.

D. Ablation Study
1) The Influence of Different Patterns: As described above,
MPIN takes three types of interactions, i.e., inflows, outflows,
and their mutual influence, into consideration. The notable performance shown in Table II has proved the effectiveness of
MPIN. A subsequent question arises: which type of interaction
information is most important in fraudster identification? To
investigate into question, we drop the inflow pattern, the outflow
pattern, and their mutual influence in MPIN, respectively. As
presented in Table III, the model without hm behaves the worst
in anomaly detection over the three datasets, which indicates
that the mutual influence between inflows and outflows has the
most impact on model inference. It is might because that hm
contains part of the information of hin and hout , making it the
most informative one.

2) The Effectiveness of Pretraining: Since the scheme of
MPIN contains contrastive pretraining, we would like to investigate how much the pretraining contributes to the anomaly
detection. Table IV presents the performance of MPIN with
and without contrastive pretraining (denoted by MPIN-p). The
results show that the pretraining could indeed improve the detection performance in most cases. The AUC value of MPIN
always stays at a high level. The unnoticeable performance
decrease of MPIN with pretraining on Ether might be due to
the noise in model training.

E. Parameter Analysis
1) The Influence of the Amount of Training Data: In this
article, fraud detection is considered as a classification problem
with extreme class imbalance. Thus, we would like to further
investigate into the performance of MPIN with labeled data reduction. With curiosity, We vary the percentage of training data
from 20% to 80% and reevaluate MPIN on the three datasets.
Fig. 4 shows the performance of MPIN with the increment
of training data. The results on the three datasets represent
three different situations. On Ether, the performance of MPIN
does not significantly improve when training data are added,
which shows that MPIN indeed learns the patterns of labeled
fraudsters and again proves the effectiveness of the proposed
model. Compared with the performance on Ether, MPIN gradually performs better with the increment of training data, which
accords with our intuition. In this situation, MPIN needs enough
labeled data to perfect its detection ability. As for Telecom,
we find that the tendency of precision is as opposed to that
of recall, while AUC and F1 score do not change significantly.
With such a small amount of true labeled fraudsters, MPIN is
easy to find false-positive sample, resulting in the high recall
and low precision at the beginning. More labeled data could
rectify its judgment.
2) The Influence of λ: In the contrastive pretraining, we
adopt the contrastive loss Lc to learn distinct transaction patterns and the local tightness loss Lt to gather the accounts
with similar transaction patterns. We would like to explore the
influences of the two objectives on the final detection performance. As we can observe from Fig. 5, the addition of Lt indeed
improves the performance of MPIN. As λ increases, the average
performance gets better and the deviation gets smaller. When
λ > 0.3, both precision and recall decline. We argue that it is
because the model focuses more on gathering accounts into
local clustering rather than learning distinct transaction patterns
when λ gets too large. Based on the experimental results, we
suggest that [0.1, 0.3] would be a proper range for λ.
3) The Influence of β: β controls how many edges will be
removed to generate positive samples. To quantify the influence
that β has on MPIN’s performance, we vary it from 0.2 to 0.8

and observe the performance change. The results are presented
in Fig. 6. We found that the performance of MPIN does not
significantly vary when β changes. It might be because there
are multiple parallel temporal edges and only removing part of
them still could generate effective positive samples. With the
help of soft-positive samples, the performance change could be
even smaller. Empirically, [0.2, 0.8] would be an appropriate
range for λ, especially for the three datasets.
F. Behavior Pattern Visualization
Besides pursuing the performance of graph anomaly detection, we also visualize1 the behavior patterns of individuals
in different networks to investigate which kind of patterns the
model learns when performing anomaly detection. As presented
in Fig. 7, the nodes in all three datasets share similar interaction
patterns cluster together. Specifically, the fraudsters in Telecom
are distributed in three clusters while normal phone numbers
possess similar communication patterns from many other clusters. As for Ether and Syn-Ether, there is only one main cluster
formed by fraudsters in the networks. It indicates that MPIN
indeed learns exact interaction patterns. Meanwhile, we should
notice that some anomalous nodes and normal nodes are mixed
in one cluster, which may lead to the misjudgment of fraudsters.
V. CONCLUSION
In this article, we proposed an MPIN to address the problem
of fraudulent account identification in transaction networks.
Considering inflows, outflows, and the mutual influence of the
two kinds of interaction from both short-term and long-term
perspectives, MPIN could well learn the behavior patterns of
each account and then distinguish the fraudulent accounts from
normal ones. The contrastive learning process indeed stabilized
the learning process and led to a low-variance performance. The
experimental results on two real-world transaction networks
proved the superior performance of MPIN compared with several baselines. By now, the fraudulent account identification
