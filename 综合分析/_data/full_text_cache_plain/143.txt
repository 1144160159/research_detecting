ABSTRACT
As the importance of personal data privacy increases, traffic encryption has become an important topic in
network communication. In the field of network security and management, the development of encrypted
traffic classification technology has drawn attention to deep learning methods. For raw encrypted traffic, deep
learning models can realize end-to-end classification with high accuracy. However, deep learning methods do
not explain which part of the encrypted traffic is critical to classification, and that will limit their application in
some cyber security scenarios that demand high interpretability. The approach proposed in this paper features
a novel feature engineering method named ‘‘BITization’’ to determine the encoding method of features and
a sliding window technique to explore which bytes contribute the most to the classification. The accuracy of
classical machine learning methods in encrypted traffic is improved by at least 14.1% through the proposed
feature engineering approach. In the experiments, the enhanced methods achieve a 98.6% average accuracy
and a 98.5% average F1-score on the ISCX-VPN-Service, Cross-Platform-IOS, Cross-Platform-Android, and
USTC-TFC2016 datasets, from which we believe a state-of-the-art performance is achieved.

1. Introduction
Network traffic classification is a technique to identify traffic from
different applications and network services, which plays a crucial role
in network management and security. For example, in the field of
network management, traffic classification can be applied for network
quality assurance, anomaly detection, and pricing strategy [1]. In the
field of network security, traffic classification is usually used to discover
malicious traffic [2,3], which can be generated by webshell and its
management tools, such as Behinder, AntSword, etc. In recent years,
with the growing public awareness of privacy issues on the Internet [4],
encryption of network traffic using technologies such as Tor, VPN,
and TLS/SSL has become a standard practice in the Internet communication. However, how to deal with encrypted traffic has brought
new challenges to traditional traffic classification techniques and has
attracted the attention of academia and industry.
Specifically, there are still some deficiencies with the current traffic
classification method.
• The natural complexity of the encryption mechanism: the encryption procedure relies on the cryptographic algorithm and

pseudo-random mechanism to encrypt the original data, which
gives the encrypted data high security, and resistivity to crack.
• Limitations of the traditional Deep Packet Inspection (DPI) traffic
classification: the basic principle of DPI technology is to capture
the pattern of payload in packets, which is encrypted and thus
makes DPI difficult to be applied to encrypted traffic [5].
• In previous research, the traffic characterization and the application identification are rarely considered as the task of encrypted
traffic classification simultaneously. Taking both granularities of
the task into account puts a higher demand on the classification
model.
The wide application of deep learning (DL) models in the industry is
limited due to their complex architecture, high computational cost, and
poor interpretability, despite the rapid development of DL technology.
Classical Machine Learning (ML) is still the dominant technology in
traditional industries in terms of practical application. By designing
expert-features, ML models can achieve comparable accuracy to deep
learning, while consuming significantly less computational resource. As
excellent gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT) techniques [6], such

2.3. Deep learning methods
Deep learning methods directly use raw encrypted traffic as input
to achieve end-to-end traffic classification, and can avoid feature extraction process. Wang proposed a Stacked Auto-Encoder (SAE) method
to classify Hyper Text Transfer Protocol (HTTP), Simple Mail Transfer
Protocol (SMTP), and other protocol families in network traffic [18].
Sirinam et al. [19] used an architecture-specific Convolutional Neural
Network (CNN) to construct the Deep-Fingerprinting model, which
achieves 98% accuracy in Tor traffic using incoming and outgoing
packet flow as input. Liu et al. [20] proposed an end-to-end Recurrent
Neural Network (RNN) traffic classification model, which is called
FS-NET. FS-NET takes packet length sequences as input and uses a
bidirectional Gate Recurrent Unit (GRU) [21] model to learn traffic
feature representation. It achieves a 99.14% true positive rate on a
real-world dataset containing 18 applications. Lotfollahi et al. [22]
constructed DeepPacket using SAE and 1D-CNN, where DeepPacket
takes the IP packet of raw traffic as input. In their experiments, the
ISCX-VPN-Service dataset is used and the results demonstrate that
DeepPacket can achieve high accuracy of 98% and 94% on traffic
characterization and application identification tasks, respectively. Lin
et al. [23] used the Transformer model [24] to learn feature representations of the flow and packet of the raw encrypted traffic. Their model
is trained with unsupervised pre-train and fine-tuning techniques [25].
The proposed ET-BERT has an F1 of 98.9% on the ISCX-VPN-Service
dataset, which has a 5.2% improvement compared to other methods.
Akbari et al. [26] designed features with good generalization, combined
with Stacked Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) layers and CNN for
learning cryptographic protocols. For application classification task, it
achieves 99% accuracy on QUIC dataset. Yang et al. [27] innovatively
combined supervised learning with unsupervised techniques to design
a CNN network for unknown traffic detection. Their method achieves
over 90% accuracy on the top-200 application classification dataset.
Although DL methods have high accuracy in encrypted traffic classification, they are difficult to be widely used in production systems of
traditional industries because of their complex models and high overhead costs [28]. For example, in Security Incident and Event Manager
(SIEM) system of global banks, the amount of data grows massively.
The deployment requirements of DL models under such conditions
cannot be matched by the existing server capacity [29]. The classical
ML approach is a better choice for non-profit sectors such as network
and security departments, because ML models can be deployed without
DL-dedicated servers, and the requirement for computational resources
is much smaller.

• An innovative concept of ‘‘Optimal sliding window size’’, providing guidance on constructing input features to optimize the
classification performance on encrypted traffic;
• An unprecedented feature transformation method ‘‘BITization’’

K-NearestNeighbor (KNN), by 23.7% on average;
• Conduct empirical studies on public encrypted traffic datasets,

achieves the optimal F1-score (F1) and optimal accuracy in three

2. Related works
2.1. Traditional traffic classification
There are two typical types of traditional traffic classification approaches: port-based approach and payload inspection techniques. The
port-based approach uses the port number information in the transport
layer header as an important attribute for classification, and the port
is also assumed to be associated with a specific application. However,
this approach is proven to have poor performance in the circumstances
of dynamic ports [10,11]. Payload inspection techniques are generally based on analyzing the payload of the application layer of the
packet [12]. One of the well-known payload inspection techniques is
DPI. DPI matches different protocols by setting predefined patterns
and regular expressions as signatures [13,14]. However, this method is
mostly ineffective in the case of new protocols, which requires adapted
and updated corresponding patterns, as well as in handling encrypted
traffic.

3. Methods
After acquiring the encrypted traffic, IP packets in the traffic data
will be transformed by BITization and optimal sliding window methods
to create input features. The feature samples will then be input to
the ML algorithm to learn an effective encrypted traffic classification
model.

2.2. Statistical feature methods

3.1. Packets preprocessing

Taylor et al. [15,16] constructed statistical features of network
traffic to train SVM and Random Forest (RF) for application (app) classification. The model’s features included the mean, variance, maxima,
minima, and quantile of packet sizes in incoming and outgoing Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) streams respectively. The accuracy of
the model on the top 100+ most popular apps in the app store is 99%,
but its prediction results are highly dependent on the confidence of
the model. Therefore, the classification accuracy is only 72% among
all actual TCP streams. Al-Naami et al. [17] used statistical features of
temporality to capture app behavior and an average accuracy of 92.6%
is achieved in their paper. In methods based on statistical features, the
performance of the method is more dependent on the expert-designed
features. This can be attributed to the fact that the design of the features
can significantly affect the generalization ability of the model.

4. Experiment
4.1. Experimental settings
In our experiments, we choose four popular datasets in the field
of traffic classification as the object of empirical research (ISCX-VPNService,
Cross-Platform-IOS,
Cross-Platform-Android,
and
USTC-TFC2016) [23]. The application identification performance and
traffic characterization ability of our method are verified in these
datasets. The details of the datasets are detailed in the Table 1.
In this paper, Python is used as the experimental platform. Scikitlearn [30] and Pytorch [31] are used to implement the ML methods.
The ML methods include KNN, SVM, NB, SOFTMAX, CART, RF, XGBOOST, and LightGBM. The algorithm is set up as follows: 𝑛_𝑛𝑒𝑖𝑔ℎ𝑏𝑜𝑟𝑠
is set to 10 in KNN; SVM uses Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel; base
learner for RF is CART; initial learning rate in SOFTMAX is 0.01 and
the optimizer of SOFTMAX is Adam [32]. The ratio of the number of
training and test data is set to 2:1, which is accomplished by stratified
sampling.

4.4. Evaluation of algorithm performance
In the experiments, State of the Art (SOTA) methods are used to
validate the effectiveness of our improvements to ML methods, as is
shown in Tables 2 and 3. In ML methods, sliding window size 𝑁 is set
to 100; KNN, SOFTMAX, NB, and SVM models use BIT-1; decision tree
methods CART, RF, XGBOOST, and LightGBM use BIT-8.
In ML methods, SVM has the best performance among the nondecision tree models, with an average accuracy of 94.77% and an
average F1 of 94.47%. LightGBM has the best average performance
level among all models, whose F1 is optimal on all datasets. The best
model among SOTA methods is DeepPacket, whose average accuracy
and F1 are 4.42%, 4.40% worse than SVM, and 8.22%, 8.39% worse
than LightGBM. According to the experimental results, SVM and LightGBM are superior to SOTA methods in accuracy, precision, recall, and
F1, which can verify the effectiveness of ML methods with the proposed
feature engineering methods.

5. Conclusion
In this paper, a light weight feature engineering method is proposed,
which can be well combined with current ML methods, achieving
efficient classification of raw encrypted traffic. To be more specific, an
innovative concept of ‘‘optimal sliding window size’’ is proposed first
by investigating the relationship between the feature-length and classification accuracy. A light feature transformation technique ‘‘BITization’’
is also proposed, which can further improve the average classification
accuracy of classical ML classifiers. In the experiment part, an empirical
study of SOTA methods is accomplished, and it is concluded that LightGBM has the best performance in IP packet-level classification. Last,
visual analysis and a few-shot experiment are designed to intuitively
understand the classification performance with different models and
evaluate the impact of train data in encrypted traffic classification.
Given the implicit nature of malicious encrypted traffic, we will further
extend our study to implement malicious traffic detection in the future.
