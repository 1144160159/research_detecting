ABSTRACT
Nowadays, most Internet communications have adopted encrypted network access technology for privacy
protection, so encrypted traffic classification (ETC) has become a crucial research point to support network
management and ensure cyberspace security. Meanwhile, off-the-shelf deep learning (DL)-based approaches
suffer from long preprocessing time, large input size, and a trade-off between model complexity and accuracy.
There is a tough challenge to deploy them on mainstream network devices and achieve fast and accurate traffic
classification. In this paper, we design FastTraffic, a lightweight DL-based method for ETC on low-configuration
network devices. To speed up processing, we set an IP packet as the granularity of FastTraffic, truncate the
informative parts in packets as inputs, and utilize a text-like packet tokenization method. For a lightweight
and effective model, we propose an N-gram feature embedding method to represent structured and sequential
features of packets and design a three-layer MLP to complete fast classification. We compare FastTraffic with
eight state-of-the-art ETC methods on three public benchmark datasets. The experimental results show that
FastTraffic obtains better classification performance than the other seven methods with only 0.43M model
parameters. Besides, it can also achieve high throughput on low-configuration devices and consume a small
amount of computing and storage resources. Therefore, FastTraffic is a lightweight ETC method capable of
large-scale deployment on Internet devices.

1. Introduction
With the awakening of security awareness, Internet users realize the
importance of privacy protection and gradually use encrypted network
communication tools or protocols for network access. Studies from Firefox [1] reportedly counted that the percentage of U.S. users leveraging
the SSL/TLS security protocol rose from 55% in 2017 to 93% in 2022.
In addition, Virtual Private Network (VPN) and The Onion Router (Tor)
are also widely employed by users to ensure the anonymity of network
communications. While improving communication privacy, users also
hope to obtain a high-quality network experience. Internet service
providers (ISPs) are responsible for charging users based on the quality
of experience (QoE) provided. However, the introduction of encryption
technology protects the confidentiality of transmitted data, while the
adoption of obfuscation technology increases the difficulty of traffic
identification. It is difficult for ISPs to identify various types of encrypted traffic on network devices. In this context, the encrypted traffic
classification (ETC) technique is envisioned as the critical enabler of

guaranteeing the quality of service (QoS), QoE, and network security
provision [2].
As aforementioned, encrypted network environments pose significant challenges to traditional traffic classification methods, including
port-based traffic identification methods and deep packet inspection
(DPI) technology. Port-based identification methods utilize the correspondence between ports and protocols for traffic classification but
become ineffective due to the widespread use of port forwarding and
dynamic port negotiation techniques. DPI identifies specific protocol
and application data by analyzing the entire IP packet. Since it cannot
parse encrypted packet payloads, DPI cannot achieve encrypted traffic
classification [3].
So far, researchers have proposed many methods to classify encrypted traffic by leveraging artificial intelligence technologies. There
are two categories: machine learning (ML)-based and deep learning
(DL)-based. ML-based methods usually use statistical patterns of traffic
to construct traffic features, like packet path signature [4], packet

must accomplish feature engineering to select optimal features for
each specific encrypted traffic protocol or tool. This work is highly
dependent on domain expert knowledge, and the selected features are
commonly less generalization [7]. However, in real network scenarios,
it is time-consuming and prohibitive to train and implement different
ML-based ETC methods for various encrypted traffic generated by
different tools or protocols.
Given the powerful representation capability of deep neural networks, researchers put forward numerous DL-based methods for classifying encrypted traffic [8]. The merit of DL is that the model can
directly input raw traffic data and automatically perform feature extraction and representation during the supervised training process, thus
eliminating the requirement for feature engineering and expert knowledge in ML. The DL-based method is naturally suitable for early ETC
scenarios, which signifies a clear difference from ML-based approaches
that depend on complete flow or session. However, there are two shortcomings of existing DL-based methods. Firstly, they did not explore
the input size of the classification model by analyzing packet format.
In previous work, the researchers often set the input size according
to the maximum transmission unit (MTU). The unreasonable values
increase preprocessing time overhead and cause a waste of resources on
devices. Secondly, many DL-based methods pursued superior classification performance at the expense of operational efficiency. To explore
the feature of encrypted traffic, the model structure adopted by ETC
methods shifted from a single Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) [9]
or Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) [10] to a stack of CNNs and
RNNs [11,12]. Recently, Transformer [13] has also been introduced
into the ETC field owing to its outstanding performance in NLP and
CV. Although these complex models can achieve excellent classification
performance on high-configuration workstations, mainstream network
devices on the Internet cannot provide the same hardware resources,
especially high-performance graphics cards (GPUs). With the research
trend of adopting complex network models, DL-based methods occupy
more and more hardware resources. Once deployed on network devices
with limited resources, they will affect regular network functions,
which goes against the original intention of guaranteeing QoS and
QoE [14].
To deploy on mainstream network devices on the Internet and
achieve fast and accurate traffic classification, we propose FastTraffic, a
lightweight ETC method, including a traffic preprocessing method and
a classification model. The main contributions of our work lie in the
following three aspects.

The design of our classification model and its complexity analysis are
arranged in Section 4. We introduce the experimental setup in Section 5
and verify the classification performance and operational efficiency of
FastTraffic through comparative experiments in Section 6. Finally, we
conclude our work in Section 7.
2. Related work
In this section, we review the existing works in ETC and underline
the motivation of our work.
2.1. ML-based method
ML-based methods are proposed to tackle the problem that traditional traffic classification methods cannot cope with dynamic port
negotiation and encrypted payload. Feature selection and optimization
play a vital role in ML-based methods, thus researchers proposed lots
of features based on the statistical pattern of the traffic, and selected
the appropriate ML classifiers, such as support vector machine, random
forest, etc., to classify them.
In [17], the authors proposed to use only the uplink’s packet timing
information to measure VPN traffic. The cumulative packet sizes feature
(CUMUL) was first proposed in [6] to produce the Tor website fingerprinting and was widely used by subsequent researchers. NetScope [24]
was proposed to infer both Android and iOS device users’ activities
based on traffic behaviors transfer rates, packet exchanges, etc. In [18],
the authors proposed a video download feature called Bit-Per-Peak,
which is the total number of bits in a peak. Shen et al. [15] combined
the CUMUL with the packet position on the uplink-dominant stage to
construct a novel fine-grained website fingerprinting. ETC-PS [4] constructed the traffic path between the encrypted communication client
and the server via the session packet length sequence, and extracted
path signature features based on this path.
Table 1 provides a summary of ML-based methods, and their commonality is that their granularity is flow or session because achieving
the pattern of communication and extracting statistical features need
to observe the entire flow or session i.e. it is infeasible to implement
early ETC, which is time consuming. At the same time, ML-based
methods were all employed at the foundation of feature engineering,
which relied on expert knowledge. Nevertheless, researchers’ feature
selection efforts focused on specific encryption tools or protocols, and
these selected feature sets lacked generalization ability. Hence, MLbased methods cannot rapidly select uniform features for classifying
encrypted traffic and are not suitable for real network scenarios where
traffic types and sources are variable.

• Firstly, we propose a traffic preprocessing method. A suitable and
reasonable packet truncation length, 50 bytes, is determined via
protocol analysis and experiments. Besides, we perform tokenization with one byte of hexadecimal characters as one word to
reduce the input size. With this method, we effectively preprocess
the encrypted traffic and thus lay the groundwork for subsequent
data representation and reduce the time overhead from the outset.
• Secondly, we design a lightweight classification model. In the
packet embedding part, we propose N-gram feature embedding to
extract the structural and sequential information in the raw bytes,
aiming to provide a better packet representation. In the classification network part, we use a three-layer MLP to reduce the number
of model parameters. In addition, we analyze the complexity of
the FastTraffic classification model at the theoretical dimension
and prove that its model complexity is lower than state-of-the-art
methods.
• Thirdly, to assess the effectiveness of FastTraffic, we conduct
comprehensive experiments and reproduce eight contrastive ETC
methods, including two lightweight methods. We introduce four
ETC tasks from three benchmark datasets and test these methods

Researchers have followed the iterations of models in artificial
intelligence field and have also continued to propose various DL classification models. The most important advantage of the DL model is
the automated feature representation of the traffic raw data. First of
all, CNN as an effective structure for automatic extraction of features
is also applied to ETC, for example, DeepPacket [9] method is designed based on the 1DCNN model. While RNN has advantages in
processing time-series data, researchers have also proposed RNN-based
methods [10] for ETC. Secondly, individual CNN or RNN cannot fully
and comprehensively represent the raw flow or packet, so in [11,20],
the authors proposed to stack CNN with RNN to further improve the
model’s ability to represent traffic. Finally, the Transformer structure,
which is centered on the Self Attention mechanism (SA) and Multihead Attention mechanism (MHA), has a larger perceptual field than
CNN and stronger time-series data representation ability than RNN
and has obtained great success in NLP and CV fields in recent years.
Thus, Transformer has also been introduced by researchers in the ETC
field. Lin et al. [13] proposed ET-BERT, which is an excellent method.
They constructed this novel pre-training method for ETC inspired by
Transformer design in [25] and fine-tuned it for different downstream
tasks on this basis. The accuracy of ET-BERT for seven ETC tasks can
reach 97% and above.
In addition to the conventional methods listed in Table 1, researchers have proposed many emerging ETC methods. In [26], the
authors designed a framework for multi-task learning based on 1DCNN
to improve the generalization ability and resource utilization of DL
models. The idea of multi-task learning was also adopted in [27].
Meanwhile, researchers proposed DL-based multimodal ETC methods
to improve classification performance. In [28], the authors used packet
length sequences and packet load bytes as the input of two modalities fed in parallel to the model to learn the joint characterization.
In [29,30], CNN and GRU were employed to extract features from
the L4 payload and header field of packets, respectively. In [31], the
authors optimize Distiller [30] for deployment on resource-constrained
devices by pruning, quantification, and knowledge distillation from the
perspective of eXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI).
As can be observed, DL-based approaches accompanying the development of artificial intelligence, tend to create sophisticated model
structures to classify encrypted traffic, which does not comply with
online traffic classification and cannot ensure QoS. To sum up, the first
problem of DL-based methods was that the researchers did not conduct
a more reasonable exploration of the input format construction and
input size selection of the ETC model, leading to blindly consistent
processing of inputs with large sizes [9]. It increases the time overhead of preprocessing and also affects the model’s input layer design.
The second one was that the existing works, like [11,13,30], tended
to introduce complex and heavyweight neural network in pursuit of
high accuracy, which dramatically reduced the efficiency of the ETC
method and does not meet the online classification and real deployment
requirements of guaranteeing.

2.3. Motivation
Given deployment on real-world network devices, researchers have
proposed several lightweight ETC methods such as MATEC [22] and
Datanet [21]. The common thread of these studies is to reduce the
input data and simplify the model structure. Although MATEC aims
to be lightweight, it retains a complex network structure in the model
to maintain high classification performance, resulting in many model
parameters and much resource usage. Datanet adopts a four-layer MLP
to construct its classification model. It does meet the requirement
of lightweight, but its poor data expression capability has caused a
decrease in accuracy.
To address the shortcomings of previous work, we propose FastTraffic intending to deploy on mainstream network devices and achieve
fast and accurate traffic classification. The novelty of FastTraffic lies
in two points. Firstly, we put forward a traffic preprocessing method.
A suitable and reasonable packet truncation length, 50 bytes, is determined via protocol analysis and experiments. Besides, we perform
tokenization with one byte of hexadecimal characters as one word to
reduce the input size. Secondly, we design a lightweight classification
model. In the packet embedding part, we propose N-gram feature
embedding to extract the structural and sequential information in the
raw bytes, aiming to provide a better packet representation. In the classification network part, we use a three-layer MLP to reduce the number
of model parameters. Experimental results show that FastTraffic has
significant advantages over state-of-the-art ETC methods and is suitable
for deployment on network devices with limited resources.
3. Data preprocessing of FastTraffic
In this section, we first introduce three well-known public datasets
covering the typical application fields of network encrypted traffic and
then elaborate on the workflow of our preprocessing method.
3.1. Dataset
In order to guarantee the general applicability of our method, three
most cited public datasets in the field of network traffic research are
selected, including ISCX-VPN, ISCX-Tor and USTC-TFC. In the following
study, we will not only determine the truncation length by analyzing
the packets of these datasets but also evaluate the performance of
FastTraffic on their classification tasks.
(1) The ISCX-VPN dataset is provided by Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity (CIC) [32]. The network traffic is captured using
Wireshark and stored in PCAP files. The whole dataset contains
193GB real-world traffic data. All samples are set with three categories of labels, namely VPN and non-VPN, 11 types of network
service, and 15 types of network application.

3.2.2. Packet filtering
Due to the complexity of the network environment, traffic datasets
often contain a portion of packets that are irrelevant to the classification tasks and need to be filtered out. In addition, before the packets
are fed into the classification model as input data, some fields need to
be specially processed to avoid adverse effects on the model. As shown
in Fig. 1, packet filtering consists of four operations.
(1) Some packets are necessary for maintaining data communication
over the Internet but are not related to the applications and services labeled in classification tasks. Based on this consideration,
we filter out DNS and ICMP packets after traffic segmentation.
(2) Since the MAC layer only provides information about the underlying communication of the local network, it does not contain
global information for Internet transmission. This limitation of
data will affect the training result of the classification model.
Therefore, we remove the header and tail of the Ethernet frame
and reserve the IP packet as the input of FastTraffic.
(3) Due to limited conditions, experimenters usually can only deploy a few devices to connect to specified services during traffic
collection, so the number of addresses included in the dataset
cannot cover the address range of the entire Internet. To prevent
classification tricks caused by high-frequency addresses, we mask
the source and destination addresses in IP packets by 0.0.0.0.
Besides, we set all source ports to 0 to avoid the experimental
biases demonstrated in [35].
(4) As the data transmission requirements are different, the network
traffic flows in the three datasets adopt both the TCP and UDP
protocols. The length of the UDP header is shorter than that of
the TCP header. To provide uniform-length data input to the
model, we pad 12 bytes of 0𝑥00 after the UDP header. Besides,
some control packets used to maintain TCP connections are removed because they do not contain the payload of upper-layer
applications.

Fig. 1. The workflow of FastTraffic’s preprocessing.

(2) The ISCX-Tor dataset is also provided by CIC [33], which contains
22.8GB of Tor network traffic data. Since Tor aims at anonymous
communication, it adopts traffic encryption and obfuscation technologies when transmitting application data. Therefore, its traffic
identification has been a difficult and hot topic in ETC research.
In ISCX-Tor, all samples contain two categories of labels, namely
Tor and non-Tor, and 11 types of Tor application.
(3) The USTC-TFC dataset is published on GitHub by Wei Wang [34].
It is widely cited in intrusion detection studies because it provides
the traffic of 10 well-known malware. The USTC-TFC contains
3.7GB of data, and all samples are set with two categories of
labels, namely goodware and malware, and 20 types of software.

3.2.3. Truncation & byte tokenization
The length of IP packets is variable, but the classification model
based on deep learning requires input data of uniform size. Therefore,
truncating long packets and padding short packets is an essential step.
As discussed in Section 2, padding all packets to the MTU size preserves
all original data but increases the processing time overhead of the
classification model. To build a lightweight model under the premise of
ensuring classification performance, it is an important issue to choose a
reasonable truncation length  for IP packets. For the optimal value of
, we propose an interval-based searching strategy. By analyzing the
data in three datasets, we set a byte interval for  according to the
length distribution of IP packets and the format of related protocols.
In the training phase, the classification model only needs to make
hyperparameter tuning within this interval, thus reducing the number
of parameters and shortening the processing time.
Fig. 2 shows the distributions of IP packet length. In the three
datasets, 41%, 51%, and 76% of the packets are less than 100 bytes
in length, respectively. If  is set to exceed 100 bytes, many packets
need to be padded to achieve a uniform input size. In addition, we
analyze the encapsulation structure of IP packets. Compared with random data payloads, the headers at each layer of the network protocol
stack can provide more meaningful information for ETC. Through the
statistics of the network traffic, we find that most of the packets include
TLS headers in addition to IP headers and TCP/UDP headers. In the
papers [23,36], the authors have obtained good classification results
by using the first 50 bytes of the IP packets, proving that the header
information of each layer protocol is effective for ETC. Based on the
above analysis, we set the interval of  to [50, 100] to discard the
payload of long packets and retain all types of header information in
IP packets. In Section 5, the value of  will be determined within this
interval by random hyperparameter tuning.

4. FastTraffic classification model
This section first demonstrates the classification model details of our

for 2-gram is 2562 , and 3-gram reaches 2563 . Consequently, building
the complete vocabulary for 2-gram and 3-gram will incur huge computational and storage overhead, and not all 2-gram and 3-gram byte’s
combination occur in the byte sequence at high frequencies. Thus, a
simple hash bucket trick is proposed to compress the vocabulary size
based on [38] and improve the running speed. The formulas of the hash
bucket are as follows:

(1) The byte sequence of an IP packet has a more fixed and rigorous
structure than a text sequence. One, two, three, or four consecutive bytes are as fields and provide practical support for network
traffic transmission. For instance, as shown in Fig. 4, the first
three bytes of the TLS header specify the data type of the TLS
payload and the TLS protocol version. In the packet header, only
four fields are four bytes, thus avoiding excessive storage and
computation overhead, we only design sliding windows with sizes

5.2.2. Efficiency metrics
In addition to classification performance, operational efficiency is
another important dimension to evaluate ETC methods. Network traffic
classification usually consists of three steps: traffic capture, data preprocessing, and model classification. According to [41,42], the process
of traffic capture can be accelerated by FPGA-based devices, so the
bottleneck of the ETC method mainly lies in the last two steps.
To objectively compare the efficiency of these ETC methods, we
introduce two metrics, PT and IT. PT describes the preprocessing time
consumed by one packet, and IT represents the inference time of the
classification model for one sample. It is worth noting that both PT
and IT are the average results of 10,000 random samples under 20
repeated experiments. To obtain accurate results of PT and IT, we
load the models into the memory at one time. The models are resident
in the memory during each repeated experiments, thus avoiding the
additional time overhead introduced by loading from disk multiple
times. Furthermore, we define throughput (TH ) based on PT and IT.
According to Eq. (20), it represents the number of packets processed
by the classification model per second.

6.2.1. Number of model parameters
The number of parameters is an important metric that affects the
operational efficiency of a deep learning model. We use Pytorch API

6.3. Comparison in low-resource scenarios
In real network scenarios, constructing a large-scale dataset for
network applications or services requires a lot of resources and labor
costs. If an ETC method only uses a small number of labeled samples
to complete model training, it can reduce the actual operating cost.
In [13], the authors refer to it as the low-resource learning ability of
ETC methods. Accordingly, we randomly select 10%, 25%, and 50%
samples from the original dataset for experiments to evaluate the lowresource learning ability of all methods. As shown in Fig. 10, the
F1 values of FastTraffic under the above sampling ratios are 90.05%,
92.48%, and 93.70%. Although the values are all lower than the result
of training the model with all samples, they are still above 90%. Compared with FastTraffic, the F1 values of other ETC methods drop more
sharply when the training samples are insufficient. Besides, we note
that flow-level methods are more susceptible to the problem that there
are not enough training samples. Since a flow contains many packets,
flow-level methods will get fewer samples for the same bytes of traffic
data. Due to adopting a smaller identification granularity, FastTraffic
can obtain more labeled samples, thereby training a more robust model.
To sum up, it has excellent learning ability in low-resource scenarios.

7. Conclusion
In this paper, we propose FastTraffic, a lightweight method for fast
and accurate encrypted traffic classification on network devices. In the
data preprocessing, we treat each IP packet as a text-like byte sequence
and truncate the first 50 bytes of the IP packet for efficient data
representation. In the model design, we propose an N-gram embedding
method to extract the structural and sequential information in raw
bytes of packets and use a three-layer MLP to perform fast classification. Through theoretical analysis and experimental verification,
we prove that FastTraffic achieves the lightweight goal and obtains
the second-best classification performance among nine ETC methods.
Experimental results show that FastTraffic reaches a throughput of 680
pps on low-configuration network devices with low CPU and memory
usage. In a word, FastTraffic can achieve encrypted traffic classification
on mainstream network devices and obtain excellent classification
performance and high operational efficiency. In the future, we will still
aim at the lightweight ETC method and plan to deepen our research
in two directions: traffic data representation and classification model
construction.
