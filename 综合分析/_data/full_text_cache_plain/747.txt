MTG-GAN: A Masked Temporal Graph Generative
Adversarial Network for Cross-Domain System Log
Anomaly Detection
Jing Zhang, Chao Luo member, IEEE, and Rui Shao
Abstract—Anomaly detection of system logs is crucial for the
service management of large-scale information systems.
Nowadays, log anomaly detection faces two main challenges: 1)
capturing evolving temporal dependencies between log events to
adaptively tackle with emerging anomaly patterns, 2) and
maintaining high detection capabilities across varies data
distributions. Existing methods rely heavily on domain-specific
data features, making it challenging to handle the heterogeneity
and temporal dynamics of log data. This limitation restricts the
deployment of anomaly detection systems in practical
environments. In this article, a novel framework, Masked
Temporal Graph Generative Adversarial Network (MTG-GAN),
is proposed for both conventional and cross-domain log anomaly
detection. The model enhances the detection capability for
emerging abnormal patterns in system log data by introducing an
adaptive masking mechanism that combines generative
adversarial networks with graph contrastive learning.
Additionally, MTG-GAN reduces dependency on specific data
distribution and improves model generalization by using diffused
graph adjacency information deriving from temporal relevance
of event sequence, which can be conducive to improve crossdomain
detection
performance.
Experimental
results
demonstrate that MTG-GAN outperforms existing methods on
multiple real-world datasets in both conventional and crossdomain log anomaly detection.
Index Terms—Log Anomaly Detection, Generative Adversarial
Networks (GANs), Temporal Data Analysis

II. RELATED WORK
A. Conventional Log Anomaly Detection
Early research on log anomaly detection relied heavily on
rule-based and statistical approaches. Principal component
analysis (PCA), as introduced by Xu et al. [23], pioneered the
use of dimensionality reduction to identify anomalies in largescale structured log data, marking a significant advancement
in automated anomaly detection. Similarly, the clusteringbased LogCluster algorithm, developed by Vaarandi et al.,
facilitated pattern recognition in large-scale textual log
datasets, offering a straightforward yet effective means of
detecting anomalous events [24]. The introduction of deep
learning has significantly expanded the capabilities of log
anomaly detection. DeepLog, proposed by Du et al., utilized
Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) to predict future log
events, enabling real-time anomaly detection in sequential data
[1]. Expanding this approach, Meng et al. developed
LogAnomaly, which incorporates semantic embeddings to
detect sequential and quantitative anomalies, thereby
improving robustness to unseen log patterns [25]. LogRobust,
proposed by Zhang et al., addressed data instability issues with
Bi-LSTM and attention mechanisms, further enhancing
detection reliability under dynamic log environments [26].
Graph-based methods have emerged as a robust alternative for
capturing complex interdependencies between log events. Yan
et al. introduced temporal event graphs, leveraging graphbased link prediction for anomaly detection in dynamic
systems [27]. Similarly, Velickovic et al.'s Deep Graph
Infomax framework established a strong foundation for
contrastive representation learning on graphs, later adapted for
log anomaly detection [28]. Hou et al.'s GraphMAE further
innovated with a self-supervised masked graph autoencoder to
reconstruct graph features, addressing both local and global
structure consistency [29]. Self-supervised learning techniques
have also contributed to advancements in log anomaly
detection. LogFiT, by Almodovar et al., demonstrated the
effectiveness of fine-tuned language models for log analysis
without the need for labeled data [30]. Qi et al. proposed
LogEncoder, integrating contrastive representation learning
and one-class classification to effectively distinguish between
normal and anomalous sequences [31].
Despite significant progress, existing methods struggle to
balance local feature extraction and global structural
consistency, particularly in dynamic and heterogeneous

straightforward: first, turn discrete log events into a dynamic
event graphs, and then use adaptive masking as filtering
mechanism to remove the "noise" unique to each system,

Specifically, MTG-GAN is designed to confront the dual
challenges inherent in system log analysis: capturing complex
temporal dependencies and emerging anomaly patterns, and
ensuring robust generalization across diverse system
environment. The efficacy of MTG-GAN stems from the
seamless integration of several specialized components, each
chosen for its specific contribution towards these goals:
temporal graph representation, graph contrastive learning,
adaptive masking mechanisms embedded within a generative
adversarial network, and temporal convolutional networks, as
illustrated in Fig. 1.
The process begins with standard preprocessing where raw
logs are parsed by using Drain and partitioned into structured
event sequences. Crucially, the temporal event graph
construction module then transforms these linear sequences
into dynamic graph representations. This structural conversion
is foundational, moving beyond simple sequential modeling to
explicitly encode both the temporal progression and the
intricate relational dependencies between log events, thereby
establishing a richer substrate for tackling capturing complex
temporal dependencies and emerging anomaly patterns. To
extract meaningful insights from these structures, we employ
graph contrastive learning (GCL) for pre-training the graph
encoder. The objective here is not merely features extraction
but learning robust and discriminative node embeddings that
capture essential topological information. By maximizing
mutual information between augmented views of the graph,
GCL yields representations that are more resilient to noise and
minor structural variations, enhancing the basis for subsequent
temporal analysis and inherently improving feature
generalizability across different graph instances contributing
to ensure robust generalization across diverse system
environment.
At the core of MTG-GAN resides the mask-based
generative adversarial network (GAN), a module strategically
designed to synergistically address both core challenges. The
adaptive masking mechanism plays a pivotal role in
overcoming cross-system generalization barriers by actively
suppressing domain-specific spurious correlations. By
dynamically and selectively obscuring parts of the input
graph's adjacency matrix during training, it prevents the model
from overfitting to potentially spurious, domain-specific
correlations. This forces the generator and discriminator to
learn based on more fundamental, transferable structural and
temporal patterns, significantly enhancing cross-domain
generalization. Concurrently, the generative aspect of the
GAN specifically tackles the critical task of temporal
dependency modeling and anomaly pattern capture. The
generator learns to predict the subsequent event graph state,

thereby modeling the system's dynamic evolution and
capturing temporal dependencies. This predictive capability,
refined through adversarial training against the discriminator,
also endows the model with the potential to simulate complex
event interrelations and anticipate emerging or unseen
anomaly patterns. Furthermore, the adversarial process
encourages the generation of realistic yet potentially diffused
adjacency information, which further bolsters robustness
against the distributional shift characteristic of cross-domain
scenarios—a fundamental requirement for maintaining
generalizability across heterogeneous systems. Finally,
recognizing that many anomalies unfold over extended
durations, a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) is
incorporated. Operating on the sequence of graph embeddings
generated over time, the TCN is specifically designed with
causal convolutions and a large receptive field to explicitly
capture long-range temporal dependencies—a critical
capability often lacking in static or short-memory models. This
architectural design directly empowers the framework to
overcome the inherent limitations of conventional approaches
in modeling systemic behavioral evolution and detecting
multi-scale anomalies.

transform raw unstructured log data into event sequences that
reflect temporal order or logical associations.

capturing temporal dependencies in log data. We adopt an

D. Anomaly Score Evaluation Method
We propose a novel anomaly scoring framework, depicted

generative adversarial network to enable precise detection and
quantitative evaluation of diverse system anomalies by
comprehensively analyzing both local event patterns and
global structural changes. The scoring process begins with the
pre-trained generator predicting the expected adjacency matrix
𝑆𝑝𝑟𝑒𝑑 for a given input sequence, representing the anticipated
normal state.

structural impact, enabling targeted analysis.

top-k algorithm applied to the spectral analysis results, time

during adversarial training, a high 𝑆𝑤 points towards local
pattern anomalies, such as the occurrence of rare events,
incorrect event ordering within a short timeframe, or
deviations from expected contextual behavior within the

IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Datasets
1) HDFS dataset [37]: This dataset comprises log data
generated from executing Hadoop-based map-reduce jobs.
It contains 11,175,629 log messages, with each message
containing a block ID. Using session windows for
sequence partitioning, parsing yielded 558,221 normal
sequences, 16,838 anomalous sequences, and 29 event
templates.
2) BGL dataset [37]: This dataset consists of log data generated
by the BlueGene/L supercomputer system. It contains
4,747,963 log messages, partitioned using a timestampbased sliding window approach. The window size is 6 hours
with a 1-hour step size. Parsing yielded 2,969 normal
sequences, 1,723 anomalous sequences, and 379 event
templates.
3) OpenStack dataset [37]: This dataset contains log data
generated on CloudLab. It comprises 207,820 log messages,
partitioned using a timestamp-based fixed window approach.
With a window size of 1 minute, parsing yielded 1,634
normal sequences, 138 anomalous sequences, and 72 event
templates.
4) Thunderbird dataset [37]: This dataset comes from the
Thunderbird supercomputer system at Sandia National
Laboratories (SNL). It contains 211,212,192 log messages,
partitioned using a timestamp-based fixed window approach.
With a window size of 1 hour, parsing yielded 268,055
normal sequences, 70,403 anomalous sequences, and 1,147
event templates. For anomaly detection experiments, a
portion of the data was randomly sampled.
5) SWaT Dataset [38]: Provided by iTrust SUTD, this
dataset contains multivariate time series (MTS) from a

secure water treatment testbed. Our analysis used data
from 𝑁=30 critical sensors/actuators over 7 normal and 4
attack days. The continuous MTS was segmented using a
60s sliding window with a 10s stride into windowed
sequences 𝑋𝑡𝑤 . Following a graph construction
methodology analogous to that presented in [39], each
sequence 𝑋𝑡𝑤 was subsequently organized into a temporal
graph 𝐺𝑡 = (𝑉, 𝐸𝑡 ) , where 𝑉 represents the nodes
(sensors/actuators) and 𝐸𝑡 captures the dynamic
interactions inferred within that time window. The initial
node features were derived from 𝑋𝑡𝑤 .
6) WADI Dataset [40]: As an extension of SWaT, this
dataset represents a water distribution system developed
by iTrust SUTD, containing MTS data from 𝑁 = 121
sensors and actuators over 14 days of normal operation
and 2 days of anomalies. Our study utilizes 𝑁 = 75
relevant nodes and focuses on 13 specific attack cases.
The data processing methodology aligns with that of
SWaT.

D. Cross-Domain Generalization Evaluation
In the log↔log transfer scenarios (BGL↔TB), MTG-GAN
demonstrates robust performance. For the BGL→TB transfer,
it achieves a target F1 score of 0.951 and AUC of 0.937,
outperforming several established baseline and even
specialized unsupervised transfer methods like LogTAD
(Target F1=0.792) and LogGT (Target F1=0.919), while being
competitive with the supervised LogETA (Target F1=0.947).
Similarly, in the TB→BGL scenario, MTG-GAN obtains a
target F1 score of 0.892 and AUC of 0.899, again showing
strong performance compared to baselines and unsupervised
methods like LogTAD (Target F1=0.845) and LogGT (Target
F1=0.798). These results highlight the effectiveness of MTGGAN's architecture in capturing transferable patterns between
different system log environments. The adaptive masking
mechanism likely plays a crucial role by preventing overfitting
to source-specific log event patterns and forcing the model to
learn more fundamental temporal and structural dependencies,
which are more likely to generalize. Furthermore, the temporal
convolutional network's ability to model long-range
dependencies and the GAN's capability to implicitly align data
distributions contribute to this strong cross-system
performance.
We further investigate MTG-GAN's generalization
capabilities across fundamentally different data types by
conducting log↔ICS transfer experiments. Transferring from
BGL (system logs) to SWaT (ICS time-series), MTG-GAN

achieves a target F1 score of 0.818 and AUC of 0.766.
Similarly, for BGL→WADI, the target F1 is 0.795 and AUC
is 0.709. While these scores represent a noticeable drop
compared to the log↔log transfer results, they demonstrate
that MTG-GAN can achieve non-trivial generalization even
across highly disparate domains. A plausible explanation for
the performance difference lies in the nature of the node
features. In log datasets like BGL and TB, node features are
derived from semantic embeddings of log templates,
potentially capturing abstract event types (e.g., 'connection
error', 'authentication failure') that might share underlying
similarities across different software systems. In contrast,
SWaT and WADI node features represent physical sensor
readings or actuator states (e.g., water level, flow rate), which
are inherently tied to the specific physical process and lack the
direct semantic correspondence found in logs. Bridging this
semantic gap between system event logs and physical process
data presents a greater challenge for transfer learning.
Nonetheless, the fact that MTG-GAN still yields reasonable
performance suggests its graph-based structure learning and
temporal modeling can capture some universal dynamics
related to sequential behavior and node interactions, even
when node semantics differ significantly.
Finally, we evaluate transfer performance between the two
related ICS datasets, SWaT and WADI. For SWaT→WADI,
the target F1 score is 0.768 (Target AUC is 0.738). For WADI
→SWaT, the target F1 score is 0.791 (Target AUC is 0.754).

E. Ablation Study
An ablation study (illustrated in Fig. 7) quantified the
contributions of MTG-GAN's core components to model
performance. Removing the adaptive masking mechanism had
the most significant impact on performance, especially on the

directly highlights its critical role in enhancing generalization
capability by mitigating overfitting to specific data patterns.

V. CONCLUSION
This research successfully introduces and validates MTGGAN, a novel framework for cross-domain system log
anomaly detection leveraging masked temporal graph
generative adversarial networks. By innovatively integrating
adaptive masking mechanisms, temporal graph structure
learning, generative adversarial networks, and temporal
convolutional networks, MTG-GAN effectively addresses the
two core challenges in log anomaly detection: accurately
capturing evolving temporal dependencies to identify
emerging anomaly patterns, and ensuring robust generalization
capability across diverse system environments.
The core strengths of MTG-GAN stem from the synergistic
interplay of its key mechanisms. The diffused graph generation
module based on generative adversarial networks enhances the
model's capability to handle both known and unknown
anomaly patterns. The innovative adaptive masking
mechanism, by reducing dependency on specific data patterns,
substantially improves its transfer generalization capability
across different datasets. Furthermore, the integration of

spectral loss and feature perturbation contrastive loss preserves
global topological structure, captures long-range dependencies,
and provides preliminary reasoning capabilities, thereby
improving result transparency and interpretability.
Extensive experimental validation across multiple realworld datasets (including system logs and industrial control
system time-series data) has demonstrated MTG-GAN's
superior detection performance and strong cross-domain
generalization. The model particularly excels in maintaining
high recall and exhibits good stability in low-data regimes. The
model particularly excels in maintaining high recall and
exhibits good stability in low-data regimes. Critically, the
dual-component anomaly score not only enhances
generalization but also differentiates anomaly types based on
scoring signatures, offering system maintenance personnel
preliminary diagnostic insights beyond simple anomaly labels,
which holds significant practical value. Beyond these
quantitative improvements, our results suggest that effective
cross-domain log anomaly detection is achievable without
requiring extensive system-specific retraining, which has
practical implications for reducing operational overhead in
organizations managing diverse IT infrastructure.
To further broaden its applicability, future work could
explore optimizations for even greater scalability, particularly
investigating automated or adaptive hyperparameter tuning
strategies, especially for the masking mechanism, could
streamline deployment across diverse datasets. Besides,
integrating online learning or concept drift adaptation
mechanisms presents a promising direction for ensuring
sustained optimal performance in highly dynamic operational
settings. Continued validation and potential adaptation across
an even wider spectrum of domains, such as Internet of Things
or network security logs, will further underscore its versatility.
More broadly, this work demonstrates the feasibility of
building anomaly detection systems that can transfer
knowledge across heterogeneous environments—a capability
increasingly important as organizations adopt multi-cloud and
hybrid infrastructure. The ability to deploy a single model
across different systems without extensive reconfiguration
addresses a key barrier to practical adoption of learning-based
anomaly detection in industry settings.
