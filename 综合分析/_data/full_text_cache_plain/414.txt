ABSTRACT
With the development of security, confidentiality, and data privacy technologies, the classification of finegrained encrypted traffic has become increasingly important. Nowadays, existing deep learning methods,
including CNN, LSTM, and transformer, have shown impressive classification performance. However, many of
these methods merely utilize the raw packet bytes to generate traffic representations, resulting in the potential
loss of crucial information, such as dynamic traffic patterns and changes in protocols. In this paper, we propose
a dual-modal fusion-based multi-classification model for encrypted traffic recognition, called EncryptoVision.
Firstly, we transform the encrypted traffic data into three-channel images and incorporate a triplet attention
mechanism to enhance the interaction among the three channels. Then, we use the multi-head self-attention
mechanism to expand the model’s global receptive field, allowing it to capture more detailed spatial feature
information. Additionally, we also leverage the learning abilities of the transformer encoder to extract temporal
feature information from the traffic for long-term time series prediction. Next, we use the spatial–temporal
fusion features to obtain the fine-grained features for multi-classification. Experimental results show that our
model outperforms state-of-the-art models in classification performance across four real-world encrypted traffic
datasets.

1. Introduction
In the era of big data, people are increasingly concerned about their
security and privacy in cyberspace. Encryption technology has been
widely applied in communication and various applications, such as
QUIC, VPN and Tor [1]. These applications and services can strengthen
the anonymity and security. But sometimes, malicious users use the
encryption and privacy protection technologies to launch the DDoS
attack or exploit the dark web to conduct illegal transactions [2]. In
view of this, it is necessary to study the regulation and analysis of
encrypted network traffic. Nowadays, encrypted traffic identification
exists some challenges, such as visibility issues, dynamic patterns,
protocol diversity, privacy and compliance and feature extraction. Especially, the combination of dynamic patterns and protocol diversity
reduced the effectiveness of feature extraction and multi-applications
classifications.
Recently, there exist some machine learning methods, such as support vector machines (SVM), decision trees, deep learning and transformer technologies to address encrypted traffic classification problems. In the presented methods, some models based on neural networks

(convolutional neural network (CNN), long short-term memory network (LSTM) and transformer) significantly improve the accuracy of
encrypted traffic classification [3]. These methods can automatically
learn traffic representations and exhibit strong robustness. However,
most of these methods focus primarily on optimizing the model itself, lacking improvements in the modeling approach for encrypted
traffic. The most critical encrypted component in encrypted traffic is
the application layer data, which refers to the actual content transmitted by users while using network services (such as file uploads
or downloads). Existing traffic representation methods can result in
the information from other packets being overwhelmed by a single
excessively long packet [4]. Besides, some malicious encrypted flows
may include obfuscation information in the first packet to conceal
subsequent attack behaviors. What is more, existing methods are inadequate in extracting spatial features, resulting in a limited in-depth
analysis of encrypted traffic. For example, the receptive field of CNN is
constrained by the fixed size of the convolutional kernels, while the
transformer-based methods divide traffic flow images into fixed-size
patches and treat them as sequence elements. These feature extraction

2. Related works
This section provides an overview of research on encrypted network
traffic classification, including machine learning-based, deep learningbased, and transformer-based methods.
2.1. Machine learning-based methods
Some encrypted traffic classification methods such as port-based
[10], protocol-based [11] and content-based [12] methods have limited
flexibility and accuracy, and therefore are not successful in recognizing
various encrypted traffic. After that, machine learning methods [13],
including supervised learning [14] and reinforcement learning [15] are
applied to identify various encrypted traffic. During this period, some
machine learning-based models [13] achieved better classification and
recognition results, including but not limited to naive bayes [16], support vector machines (SVM) [17] and decision trees [18]. Each model
has its own merits, such as the simplicity and speed of naive bayes,
the interpretability of decision trees, and the strong generalization
ability of SVM. In most cases, machine learning utilizes supervised
learning techniques that require pre-labeled training datasets. These
datasets are used to train the models to differentiate various traffic
categories. Although machine learning-based traffic classification has
made progress, it still faces challenges related to the complexity of
feature engineering and the need for manual intervention. It also
requires greater flexibility and resilience to handle highly encrypted
traffic and continuously evolving network applications.

∙ The proposed model, called EncryptoVision, is designed to identify different types of encrypted network traffic. It takes threedimensional traffic images and byte sequences generated from
encrypted traffic as input, extracts spatial and temporal features
from these two modalities, and then generates spatial–temporal
features for classification.
∙ This paper proposes a traffic representation method that transforms encrypted traffic into a three-channel images. This approach effectively leverages packet-level or flow-level features
within network flows. Additionally, we employed a triplet attention for data augmentation, allowing the model to learn more
meaningful internal representations of the traffic image.
∙ The experiment evaluated the performance of the model and
the improvement of the model’s classification performance with
the triplet attention. The experimental results on four publicly
available encrypted traffic datasets demonstrate that transforming
encrypted traffic into three-channel images and incorporating
the triplet attention mechanism notably enhances the model’s
classification performance.

2.2. Deep learning-based methods
The emergence of deep learning technologies [19] has significantly
reduced the need for manual feature engineering in traffic classification. Models such as CNN [20,21], recurrent neural network (RNN)
[22], LSTM [23] can immediately learn effective feature representations from encrypted traffic without the requirement for manual feature
construction. Deep learning models can effectively capture intricate
patterns and relationships in encrypted network traffic, enabling them
to outperform other approaches in traffic classification tasks. Some
researchers have combined attention mechanisms with classic deep
learning models, leading to the development of methods such as CNN
with attention [24], LSTM with attention [25], and others. The incorporation of attention mechanism has significantly improved the capacity
of models to focus on crucial features and extract important features
within the encrypted traffic data. The advancement of attention mechanisms has led to the development of neural networks that utilize
channel attention mechanisms, such as SENet [26] and CBAM [27],
which have achieved remarkable results in various object identification
and image classification tasks. Channel attention mechanisms aim to
explicitly represent the interdependencies across feature channels, automatically capturing the relevance of each feature through a learning
process. Misra et al. [28] improved the triplet attention mechanism
by enhancing data interaction among channels through pooling and
convolution techniques.

The highlight of our work lies in the feature extraction method, as
shown in Fig. 1. In the figure, the network flow consists of header
information and the payload, which is represented by the leftmost
rounded rectangle. We can extract the flow-level or packet-level matrix
from the network traffic. The previous feature extraction technologies
includes the natural language processing (NLP) and CNN methods. The
NLP methods can transform packet segments into 16-bit token units,
such as 0 × 234d or 0 × 1dd0. The CNN methods can convert the
traffic data into feature maps. Our method can consider both spatial and
temporal features, and combine them to obtain a more comprehensive
feature representation.

2.3. Transformer-based methods

3. Our model

Due to the transformer model’s ability to effectively capture longrange dependencies and process sequential data [29], researchers have
proposed many transformer-based methods recently. Cheng et al. [30]
proposed a lightweight neural network specifically designed for online
encrypted traffic classification. In [31], a novel transformer-based representation approach was proposed. In the data preprocessing stage, the
encrypted traffic data was converted into single-channel images. The
payloads were transformed into a format suitable for efficient learning
by the transformer model through appropriate encoding techniques. Lin
et al. [9] developed a technique that utilized a pre-trained transformer
model to capture long-range dependencies and contextual interactions
in byte sequences. This technique aims to analyze the behavioral patterns of encrypted network traffic in order to improve the accuracy of
traffic classification. Zhao et al. [6] proposed a training method that
randomly masked specific sections of the input packets during the pretraining phase. The training method can help the model understand the
contextual relationships and traffic patterns within the byte sequences.
This strategy significantly enhances the model’s ability to express the
inherent relationships and patterns within the traffic data. Liu et al. [7]
proposed an encrypted traffic classification model based on vision
transformer (ViT), which includes PVT and STFE modules for extracting
global features and spatiotemporal features. The model also introduces
a dynamic weighting mechanism to avoid the issue of unbalanced
weight distribution during feature fusion.
Regarding the aforementioned challenges, we proposed a dualmodal fusion-based multi-classification model for encrypted network
traffic and applications. By incorporating a triplet attention, we enhance the cross-dimensional interactions between the traffic data and
mitigated the loss of spatial information caused by traffic reconstruction and patch segmentation. Furthermore, we used a transformer encoder to directly extract the temporal features from the encrypted traffic byte sequences. Finally, we combined the extracted spatial features
and temporal features to obtain the spatial–temporal fusion features for
multi-classification.

In this section, we introduce the structure of our proposed model,
as well as the detailed processing steps for encrypted traffic, including encrypted network traffic preprocessing, spatial feature extraction,
temporal feature extraction, feature fusion, and classification.
3.1. Encrypted network traffic preprocessing
The varying lengths and uninformative fields of the traffic data
make it essential to preprocess the raw encrypted traffic before model
training. The former three datasets used in this paper—USTC-TFC2016
[32], ISCX-VPN2016 [33], and CIC-IOT2022 [34]—are provided in
.pcap file, containing all information about network flows and packets.
In contrast, CESNET-QUIC22 [35] is provided in .csv file and contains
only flow information and partial packet data. To effectively leverage
the information in the traffic data and standardize the generation
size of traffic images, we begin by performing traffic cleaning and
segmentation on the .pcap files. This process is illustrated in the data
preprocessing module of Fig. 2.
3.1.1. Traffic segmentation and three-dimensional matrix construction
For the USTC-TFC2016, ISCX-VPN2016 and CIC-IOT2022 datasets,
it is essential to clean the raw traffic data before segmenting the raw
traffic. We have used Wireshark to filter some noise traffic about the
control and auxiliary protocols, such as ARP, STUN, DNS, ICMP, and
so on. This is because the packets do not contain application payloads,
and they are only used to support and maintain the network operation.
Next, we can use the SplitCap to segment the raw traffic into the different network flows based on ‘‘network sessions’’. Every network flow
contains some data packets with the same TCP or UDP five-tuple packet
header. Additionally, for the USTC-TFC2016, ISCX-VPN2016 and CICIOT2022 datasets, we have to deal with the segmented flow files with
their filename extension .pcap. If a flow has more than three packets,
we can directly extract the first three packets [𝑝𝑖1 , 𝑝𝑖2 , 𝑝𝑖3 ]. Otherwise,
we have to add the fixed-format zero data firstly, and then extract the

1. Gaussian noise addition
In the process of flattening the three-channel traffic image 𝑀 into a
one-dimensional byte sequence 𝐵, we added Gaussian noise to the sequence for several reasons. Firstly, noise is inevitable in network traffic
data (due to transmission errors, packet loss, or signal interference). By
introducing Gaussian noise, we can help the model better adapt to noisy
environments in the real world, improving its generalization ability.
Secondly, by adding an appropriate amount of Gaussian noise, we can
‘‘perturb’’ the data distribution to some extent, encouraging the model
to focus more on the overall structure of the data rather than local
details, thereby reducing feature loss caused by dimensional changes.
To calculate the Gaussian noise corresponding to each sequence, we
obtained the probability density function 𝑓 (𝑥) based on the mean 𝜇 and
standard deviation 𝜎 of the sequence. Next, we randomly sample from
the interval [0, 1) to generate a uniform distribution random number
sequence with the same length as the byte sequence. After that, the
sequence is transformed into a Gaussian distribution sequence 𝐺 using
the Box–Muller transformation. The process is shown in Eq. (8).

4.2.1. Patch size 𝑆
In the transformer architecture, the patch size is a very important
hyperparameter. It affects the model’s ability to perceive image details
and global structure. A smaller patch size can capture more local details
but also increases computational costs. Conversely, a larger patch size
focuses more on global structure and large-scale features, but may
lose some fine-grained information. Therefore, we conduct experiments
with different patch sizes 𝑆 of 2, 4, and 8, while keeping the embedding
dimension 𝐷 at 256. The experimental results are shown in Fig. 7.
From the changes in classification accuracy and loss values of the
model under different patch sizes, it can be seen that the patch size
does not have a very significant impact on the final classification
performance. This is because, compared to images captured in the real
world, the three-channel images constructed using the method in this
paper are not only much smaller in size but also contain less rich
features than those in the real world. Therefore, for these small-sized
traffic images in this study, regardless of the chosen patch size, the
model can map them into a similar two-dimensional space through
linear mapping and dimensional embedding, encompassing most of the
feature information in the images.

recall (𝑅) and 𝐹1 -score (𝐹1 ) are defined for every target class. The
equations for these evaluation metrics are shown in Table 3.
Precision represents the proportion of samples predicted as positive by the model that are truly positive, reflecting the reliability of
network traffic identification. Low precision indicates the inefficient
use of network resources or unnecessary interference. Recall evaluates
whether all true positive samples in the dataset are detected, reflecting
the sensitivity of traffic classification. Low recall indicates threats to
network security or service quality decrease. 𝐹1 -score can be considered as the harmonic mean of model precision and recall, balancing
both metrics, reflecting the comprehensive performance of the traffic
identification and classification method, with higher values indicating
better performance.

introduction of these models and the reasons for selecting them as
comparison models are shown as below.
AppScanner is a ML-based method using statistical features for traffic classification. 1D-CNN is the earliest end-to-end traffic classification
model and is also the first to propose converting traffic into images
for classification. CNN-LSTM adds an LSTM model on top of 1D-CNN
to extract features from any three consecutive packets in the flow. FSNet uses raw traffic information as sequential input to extract relevant
features to improve classification performance. L2-BiTCN-CNN utilizes
bidirectional TCN and CNN to extract temporal and spatial features
from the traffic, and performs feature fusion to obtain global features
for classification. ET-BERT uses the Datagram2Token method to convert session flows in network traffic into tokens, similar to those in NLP,
for model pre-training and fine-tuning. YaTC introduces an innovative
traffic transformation method (referred to as T3 in this paper) for
classification. ATVITSC converts traffic into a single-channel image and
uses bidirectional LSTM and transformer encoder to extract features
from the traffic. These three models are based on the transformer
architecture.

5. Conclusions and future work
References
This paper proposes a dual-modal fusion-based multi-classification
model called EncryptoVision, which introduces channel attention
mechanism into the ViT model for recognizing encrypted network
traffic. The model transforms the encrypted traffic into three-channel
images and uses triplet attention to enhance the interactions of the
data in three channels, enabling the model to extract more fine-grained
features. Next, multi-head self attention mechanism is applied to extract
spatial and temporal features. Additionally, a spatial–temporal feature
fusion technique is proposed to get the global feature for classification. The USTC-TFC2016, ISCX-VPN2016, CIC-IOT2022 and CESNETQUIC22 datasets are used to conduct rigorous tests to evaluate the EncryptoVision model. The proposed model achieves 𝐹1 -scores of 0.979,
0.988, 0.974 and 0.911 in the encrypted traffic multi-classification task
on the respective datasets. This method outperforms state-of-the-art
approaches in terms of classification performance.
Our work has two limitations and related future work. First, we
tested our model using only four publicly available network traffic
datasets. However, in real-world network environments, the volume
and classifications of encrypted traffic are not predetermined. Thus,
future studies should focus on enhancing the model’s generalization
ability and robustness across diverse datasets. Second, while we have
demonstrated the use of a transformer encoder to extract temporal characteristics from the traffic data, this approach significantly increases the
model’s parameter count and prolongs the training process. In future
work, we plan to develop a hybrid approach that combines a reservoir
network with a simplified transformer encoder to extract temporal
features while further reducing the parameter count.
