Abstract
This study proposes an innovative method for recognizing network attack patterns
and issuing early warnings that combines multimodal data processing with advanced
artificial intelligence technology. By comprehensively analyzing multiple data
sources, including traffic, logs, and events, and applying deep learning models, the
efficient identification of complex network attack behaviors is achieved. At the same
time, ensemble learning and knowledge graphs are introduced to enhance the
system’s stability and accuracy, as well as improve model interpretability, providing
an intuitive support tool for network security managers. Experimental results show
that, compared to traditional algorithms, this new method has achieved significant
progress in key performance indicators, particularly in addressing high-dimensional
and data imbalance problems. This demonstrates that integrating multimodal data
with AI technology is a practical approach to addressing contemporary network
security challenges. Future work will focus on optimizing existing models, exploring
additional data types, and enhancing the system’s adaptability to dynamic network
environments, thereby continuously improving the effectiveness of network attack
detection and response efficiency.

1.1 Research gaps

Current network attack detection methods face several critical limitations. Traditional
rule-based and signature-based systems effectively detect known attacks but fail to identify zero-day exploits, advanced persistent threats (APTs), and rapidly evolving attack
variants. Machine learning and deep learning approaches improve detection of previously seen patterns but are often constrained by single-source data usage, high falsepositive rates, and limited adaptability in dynamic network environments. Moreover,
these AI-based models generally function as black boxes, providing little interpretability
for security analysts. The proposed framework overcomes these challenges by leveraging multimodal fusion, ensemble learning, and knowledge-graph reasoning, enabling
precise detection of complex attacks, interpretable alerts, and seamless integration into
analyst workflows.

2 Related work
Recent literature highlights the growing use of knowledge graphs and machine learning
for cybersecurity. Knowledge graph approaches model entities and their relationships
to capture semantic attack patterns, enabling interpretable intrusion detection. Machine
learning techniques, including ensemble methods and deep learning, have been applied
to traffic, log, and event data for automated threat detection. Several studies combine
these approaches to improve both detection accuracy and interpretability. These Works
demonstrate hybrid frameworks, while others explore semantic reasoning to detect lowfrequency or novel attacks. This review establishes the context for the proposed multimodal, ensemble, and knowledge graph–based framework.
Although traditional intrusion detection systems such as signature-based and anomaly-based systems have been effective with traditional attacks, they can easily deal with
low-frequency attacks or novel attacks and do not offer much interpretability. Recent
AI and machine learning models enhance detection accuracy but are mostly black-box
and, therefore, it is hard to comprehend how analysts have been influenced to raise an
alarm or follow an attack path. The above limitations underscore the requirement to
have frameworks that are robust and generalizable in detection with semantic interpretability, which drives the fusion of multimodal fusion, ensemble learning, and knowledge
graph-based reasoning to the proposed system.
The proposed framework aligns with research in explainable AI (XAI) by providing interpretable attack paths through knowledge graph reasoning, enabling analysts
to understand and verify alerts. It also complements cyber threat intelligence (CTI)
approaches by linking events across logs, traffic, and known attack patterns to identify
emerging threats. By bridging these fields, the system not only improves detection accuracy but also enhances analyst trust and situational awareness, expanding its relevance
beyond traditional intrusion detection to broader cybersecurity intelligence and decision-support applications.

2.1 Network attack identification technology

Traditional IDS cannot handle new and diversified network threats as network attacks
get more complicated. Signature-based detection in IDS identifies known attack patterns using established criteria. However, signature-based detection has severe drawbacks. They cannot handle zero-day, variant, and freshly discovered unknown attacks
[2]. They can only identify known attack patterns. Researchers have gradually used ML
and DL approaches to solve this challenge.
Machine learning eliminates rule-based approaches by learning attributes from historical data to identify network assaults. The first investigations used supervised learning
algorithms like SVM, decision trees, and K-nearest neighbors. These methods classify
attack and routine traffic using models. SVM is popular for network traffic classification and intrusion detection [3]. It excels in processing high-dimensional data, especially
nonlinear issues. SVM relies on data annotation and is susceptible to aberrant samples,
which might cause model divergence.
The ensemble learning method Random Forest (RF) is commonly used to detect network attacks [4]. RF builds numerous decision trees to improve classification accuracy
and handle data imbalance. RF handles complicated data effectively, but its model is hard
to grasp and slow to train. As network data grows, DL approaches are recommended as a
better alternative. CNNs and RNNs have improved network security recently [5]. CNN’s
effectiveness in image processing led to its use in network traffic analysis. CNN learns
features and models data without manual feature extraction. Due to its sequence data
processing capabilities, RNN, notably the Long Short-Term Memory Network (LSTM),
is chosen for processing network traffic time series data [6].
Deep learning technology can automatically extract features from vast volumes of raw
data without any intervention. Many network attack detection research have used deep
learning recently. The literature suggests a DNN-based IDS model that automatically
learns features without feature engineering, enhancing accuracy [7]. In network traffic
classification and anomaly detection, CNNs and autoencoders are commonly used [8].
Some academics have proposed a reinforcement learning-based network intrusion
detection approach using deep reinforcement learning (DRL). According to the literature, DRL-based network security defense can automatically optimize attack detection
and response techniques through real-time learning and environmental interaction
[9]. Deep learning approaches can detect hidden attack patterns in large-scale data and
adapt to unforeseen threats.
2.2 Application of multimodal data

Traditional network attack detection uses one data source, like traffic or log data. However, one data source rarely provides enough information to fully identify network
threats. Mixing multimodal data has become a research hotspot. Multimodal data combines traffic, log, event, and other data sources to improve network attack identification.
Network traffic data is a popular network security data source. Each network packet
or flow’s source IP, destination IP, port number, protocol type, and packet size are listed.
Researchers can study attacker activity by examining network traffic. DDoS attacks focus
traffic on a single IP address or port, while SQL injection attacks cause irregular request
patterns [10].

In recent years, deep learning algorithms have improved traffic data analysis. Many
research have used convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to automatically extract features and classify traffic data to improve traffic pattern recognition [11]. The literature
suggested a CNN-LSTM hybrid model for complicated network attack detection. This
technique captures traffic temporal and spatial aspects to facilitate attack identification
[12].
Log data records system and network events such logins, errors, and file access. Log
data shows attackers’ activities and crucial attack clues. Frequent login failures may indicate brute force attacks, while abnormal file access may signal internal dangers. To detect
network attacks, many research have used log data and deep learning for automated
analysis, in addition to feature engineering. Literature suggests an LSTM-based model
for system log aberrant behavior analysis. This approach can identify several attacks
using log data time series patterns. Log data training lets the deep learning model find
undiscovered attack patterns [13]. Network devices, IDS, operating systems, and other
monitoring technologies generate event data. Event data might indicate system health
and abnormalities. Researchers can easily discover network attack behaviors by examining event data. Event, traffic, and log data are integrated to identify attacks in several
dimensions.
2.3 Attack pattern identification and early warning

Recent advances in artificial intelligence and intensive learning have improved network
attack pattern recognition technology. Deep learning may recognize diverse attack patterns by automatically extracting complicated aspects from large-scale data.
Deep learning-based attack pattern identification algorithms have advanced in recent
years, especially when using CNN, RNN, and LSTM neural network models for attack
detection, which are accurate and efficient. CNNs can automatically extract features
from raw traffic data and classify and identify intrusions, according to the literature. This
approach detects DDoS and port scanning attacks well due to its feature learning capabilities [14]. When analyzing network traffic time series data, RNN and LSTM also have
substantial effects. The literature suggests an LSTM-based network attack pattern recognition model. Learning from historical traffic data, this model can predict and warn
of network attack trends. The attack warning system is essential to network attack detection. It can alert security administrators early in an attack, giving them time to respond.
Most attack warning systems use rules or machine learning models, although they typically miss new attacks [15]. Researchers have used deep learning to increase warning
accuracy and timeliness. The paper proposed a deep learning attack warning system.
This method uses CNNs and LSTMs [16] to extract temporal and geographical information from network traffic and log data to detect network threats in real time. This strategy produces more accurate early alerts and can identify unknown assaults faster than
rule-based warning systems. Optimizing the assault warning method with RL is another
key direction. The research suggested a DRL-based network security defense system that
enhances attack detection and warning tactics through self-learning and environment
interaction [17]. The attack identification strategy is continuously adjusted using an RL
model to handle complicated and dynamic network threats. The warning system can be
automatically adjusted to improve network protection adaptability and efficiency.

2.4 Multimodal cybersecurity

Innovative research has designed intrusion detection with multimodal technologies that
combine heterogeneous sources of data. Xu et al. [18] designed a multimodal fusionbased few-shot intrusion detection model, which improves detection accuracy in sparse
labeled data settings through cross-modal learning of representation. Although the
approach is demonstrated to be flexible in sparse data settings, its scalability in largescale real-world implementations has not been explored. Alternatively, Shi et al. [19]
proposed a multimodal hybrid parallel intrusion detection model that handles different
modalities in parallel with enhanced accuracy in different attack scenarios. Nevertheless,
the parallel training architecture tremendously necessitates extra computational burden,
which is not easily deployable for resource-scarce networks.
Supplementing these works, Peppes et al. [20] proposed a GAN-based multimodal
threat identification system such that synthetic data are synthesized to address class
imbalance and enhance the recognition precision. Though such advantages are available,
GAN-synthesized data poses issues of data drift and the possibility of overfitting since
synthetic distributions do not necessarily map to actual attack patterns. By and large,
these advancements showcase the potential of multimodal cybersecurity models but
raise issues with scalability, efficiency, and realistic data.
In general, multimodal cybersecurity studies exhibit promising advances toward
detection effectiveness and robustness, but challenges including computational expenditure, data fabrication bias, and scalability limitation remain to be addressed. Those
limitations necessitate a scalable multimodal framework capable of compromising
detection effectiveness in favor of deployment efficiency in an extensive variety of network environments.
2.5 Knowledge graph applications in cybersecurity

Knowledge graphs (KGs) may improve cybersecurity semantic reasoning and interpretability. Sikos [21] detailed how cybersecurity knowledge graphs may combine threat
intelligence, monitor attack chains, and facilitate vulnerability analysis. This theoretically rich ontology survey does not present real-time detection cases. In expanding this
region, Hu et al. [22] proposed LLM-TIKG, which uses massive language models to
semiautomatically create knowledge graphs from unstructured cyber reports with minimum entity and relation extraction. LLM outputs can cause hallucinations or false associations, compromising detection credibility.
In a related study, Alharbi et al. [23] developed an autonomous method for generating KGs from heterogeneous data sources that allows continuous graph refining to react
to developing threat profiles. However, coordinating contradictory or redundant information across sources can lower graph quality and analysis. Knowledge graphs improve
cyber situational awareness, however the studies show validation, consistency, and
deployment weaknesses.
In dynamic, heterogeneous cloud computing, Mitropoulou et al. [24] examined massive monitoring data for anomaly detection. The intricacy of classical anomaly detection
makes real-time detection difficult. The authors created a knowledge network with cloud
resources and relationships and generated vector embeddings with GraphSAGE. CBLOF
and Isolation Forest unsupervised anomaly detection techniques used these embeddings
to detect overuse. After discovering irregularities, CBLOF optimized cloud operations

better than Isolation Forest. Simulation-only evaluation, resource utilization irregularities, and production-scale cloud deployment scalability difficulties are limitations.
Alharbi et al. [23] combined CVE and CWE cybersecurity data with text reports, a
complex and time-consuming task. An autonomous cybersecurity knowledge graph
pipeline was proposed. From structured data, NLP extracts entities and relations, logical
rules infer new relationships, and centrality and community identification tools evaluate the network to generate a property graph. CKG proof-of-concept with hundreds of
nodes found structural flaws and permitted complicated searches not achievable with
raw data. The proof-of-concept nature, inaccurate NLP technology for cybersecurity
texts, and logical rule–based reasoning scaling limits for complex or probabilistic linkages are downsides.
In summary, knowledge graphs increase cybersecurity semantic reasoning and interpretability, but data consistency, real-time adaptability, and operational verification
concerns plague existing systems. This requires a hybrid strategy that combines knowledge graph reasoning with machine learning models for accurate and explainable attack
detection.

3 Proposal and implementation of research methods
The proposed system begins with data preprocessing and feature extraction, then designs
the ensemble learning and knowledge-graph modules, multimodal fusion technique,
and semantically enhanced early-warning mechanism. Ensemble learning and knowledge graphs increase network attack pattern recognition accuracy and interpretability.
Multiple models working together in ensemble learning strengthens the system, while a
knowledge graph gives the model greater contextual information, making it smarter and
more accurate in its judgments. Traditional statistical attack detection approaches are
straining to handle complex attack patterns as network security threats evolve. Ensemble learning and knowledge graph give a strong technical framework for detecting and
warning of network threats, especially novel and sophisticated ones. Ensemble learning
increases model performance by merging many classifiers, while a knowledge graph provides organized, semantic information to boost attack detection accuracy and robustness. This article details how to combine the two to improve network attack detection
and warning.
The proposed method addresses the challenge of precisely detecting sophisticated and
evolving cyberattacks that evade conventional rule-based IDS and unimodal approaches.
By merging multimodal sources of information (traffic, logs, and events), ensemble
learning, and knowledge-graph reasoning, the method presents two primary contributions: (1) strong fusion of features across heterogeneous modalities towards enhanced
detection under imbalance, and (2) semantic enrichment by knowledge-graph embeddings for enhanced support for reasoning and interpretability over attack trajectories.
To ensure that the architecture is visually correct, the method architecture diagram
has been enhanced to indicate data flow paths from multimodal input through feature
extraction (CNN/LSTM/Autoencoder), to knowledge graph building, then fusion and
ensemble classification, followed by the final presentation of attack detection and early
warnings. The enhanced diagram clearly indicates component-to-component interactions, as well as sequential information flow, outlining how individual modules contribute to the overall detection pipeline.
The research starts with multimodal input construction: system calls, network packets,
and event logs are session-id-aligned and timestamp-synchronized (dataset summary in
Table 4) and undergo cleansing (normalization, denoising, deduplication, categorical
one-hot encoding) as well as oversampling and class weighting to address imbalance.
Raw input is converted into fixed-length embedding by modality-specific encoders: a
convolutional encoder for packet/flow feature, an LSTM-based sequence encoder for log
streams, and an autoencoder for sparse event vectors. Outputs of encoders are in the
“Encoder → Vector” block of Fig. 1 and form the numerical core of subsequent semantic
processing.
Second, concept recognition pulls entities (IP, user, service, and signature) and relation candidates (originates_from, targets, causes) from encoded logs to fill a domain KG.
Entity resolution and relation typing follow, followed by path-finding and graph reasoning to detect probable attack chains (e.g., scan → exploit → escalation) and calculate
path-similarity scores (e.g., Jaccard scores). The KG is integrated into a low-dimensional

3.1 Preprocessing pipeline

All raw inputs pass through an identical pipeline to maintain modality uniformity.
Packet/flow records are de-duplicated, and bad records are dropped. Categorical features (protocol, TCP flags) are one-hot encoded, or target encoded, depending on their
cardinality; high-cardinality categorical fields (such as source IP) support frequencybased bucketing or hashing. Continuous features (packet size, byte counts, duration,
inter-arrival times) are Min–Max scaled to [0,1] unless specified otherwise, StandardScaler (zero mean, unit variance) in zero mean experiments. Timestamps are synchronized across all sources and employed to construct per-session records (session
key = 5-tuple + time window). Missing values are treated using modality-specific techniques: median imputation for continuous features, an “unknown” token for categorical
features, and whole missing modalities are marked and processed by the fusion layer.
To address real-world conditions where data streams may be incomplete, noisy, or
unsynchronized, the framework includes an adaptive data-quality management layer.
This component performs missing-value estimation using window-based interpolation and model-driven prediction, applies noise filtering and outlier removal, and aligns
asynchronous modalities using event-based temporal normalization. These steps allow

the fusion module to remain functional even when one or more modalities experience
degradation or partial data loss. Once the raw data has been cleaned and normalized, the
processed features are fed into the ensemble and knowledge-graph modules for semantic enrichment and classification. The following subsection describes how traffic, log,
and event features are extracted and represented before being combined into a unified
multimodal vector for classification.
3.2 Feature extraction

Traffic feature engineering: Aggregation is performed at the flow level with the sliding window (default window = 60 s; tunable). For every window, calculate statistical
descriptors: packet_count, byte_count, mean_packet_size, std_packet_size, min/max,
inter-arrival_time_mean, inter-arrival_time_std, flow_duration, unique_dst_ports, ratio_
tcp_udp, and entropy over destination IPs/ports. Protocol-specific features (e.g., TCP
flag counts, SYN/ACK ratios) are calculated. Feature transformations like log(1 + x) are
also applied to highly skewed counts before scaling.
Log feature engineering: Log lines are preprocessed to extract the structured fields
(timestamp, source user, process, operation, status code). Sequence windows are established per session/user with an upper bound sequence length (default: 100); padded for
shorter sequences and truncated from the oldest entries for longer sequences. Tokenization utilizes a vocabulary constructed from the training dataset; infrequent tokens are
remapped to an < UNK> token. Categorical tokens used as LSTM input are embedded
(embedding_dim = 128). Other engineered features are failed_login_counts (last 5-min
window), unique_resource_access_count, and temporal differences between consecutive
events.
Event feature engineering: Event logs (host events, IDS alerts) are represented as
sparse vectors: one-hot for event type, ordinal mapping for severity (e.g., low = 1,
medium = 2, high = 3), and derived from the timestamp (time_of_day, day_of_week).
An autoencoder-based compression (bottleneck dimension = 64) is employed to project
high-dimensional, sparse event vectors into dense embeddings before fusion.
The framework deals with the noise or partially complete data by using a mixture of
preprocessing and model level robustness. Missing themes on traffic, log or event features are filled in with modality-specific imputation techniques, such as mean/mode
imputation of continuous/categorical datas and forward/backward filling imputation
of time series. The statistical thresholds and robust scaling are used to remove outliers
and outlier measurements. On the model level, ensemble learning reduces the effect of
corrupted inputs by combining the prediction of a set of base learners, and knowledge
graph reasoning verifies the events among semantic network, and rules out spurious
anomalies. This hybrid methodology will provide a promising detection that remains
robust even in the presence of incomplete or noisy data of real-world cybersecurity.
3.3 Feature selection and dimensionality reduction

Early feature subsets are pre-filtered using variance thresholding and mutual information. For numeric high-dimensional sets, recursive feature elimination with light estimator (e.g., linear SVM) is an optional step. To visualize and ablate, PCA is performed
(n_components = 50), and t-SNE is used for qualitative examination only.

3.4 Multimodal fusion strategy (implemented primary method)

The multimodal fusion method used here mainly consists of an early fusion approach
with joint concatenation. Standalone modality embeddings for traffic, log, and
event data are computed first and concatenated to produce one modality vector u = [utraf f ic ∥ ulog ∥ uevent ], which is a 384-dimensional vector representation.
The vector is then fused with the 128-dimensional knowledge graph embedding v , to
provide the fused representation z = [u ∥ v] with a size of 512. To promote representational capacity and avoid overfitting, the concatenated vector is fed through a project layer consisting of a fully connected layer with 256 hidden units, ReLU activation,
batch normalization, and dropout. The resultant representation is fed to each of the base
learners in the ensemble for classification.
Besides concatenation, alternative fusion techniques have been proposed for ablation
analysis. Late fusion trains modality-specific classifiers separately and aggregates their
predictions using weighted averaging or a meta-learner. Attention-based fusion computationally estimates importance weights for modality embeddings adaptively and generates a weighted sum to highlight the most informative sources. Gated fusion introduces
learnable gating coefficients gm = σ (Wm um + bm ) to control each modality’s contribution flexibly. A comparison with the traditional concatenation method enables systematic examination of their relative merits and guarantees that the resulting design is
data-driven.
Normalization after fusion is performed by either LayerNorm or L2 scaling to bring
the inputs into a stable state, which is then fed into classifiers. The importance of features is approximated using permutation-based techniques and SHAP values, which
provide explainability of modality contributions and identify the ten most important features per modality. The interpretability metrics not only show the multimodal fusion’s
prediction capability but also its analytical utility to cybersecurity professionals.
For reproducibility reasons, the implementation prescribes all the settings during
training. Default hyperparameters include an embedding dimension of 128, a batch size
of 128, a learning rate of 0.001 using the Adam optimizer, a maximum of 50 epochs, and
an early stopping patience of five epochs. There are five classifiers within the ensemble,
and random seeds for data split and initialization are monitored. Experiments were conducted in Python 3.10 using either PyTorch or TensorFlow, along with the corresponding libraries that support them in scikit-learn. The hardware specifications, including

3.6 Knowledge graph

A Knowledge Graph is a graphical structure used to represent entities and their relationships, providing rich semantic information. It consists of nodes and edges, where nodes
represent entities (such as attack types, attackers, and network devices) and edges represent relationships between entities (such as attack targets, attack paths, and attacker
behaviors). In network attack identification, knowledge graphs can help us describe the
similarities and correlations between attack patterns, and further enhance the ability to
identify complex attack behaviors.
Knowledge graphs within the system, as conceived, are built through the entity extraction of objects such as IP addresses, user accounts, services, and alarms from multimodal data streams, and connecting them through relations like ‘originates_from’,
‘targets’, and ‘causes’. Such an operation builds a semantic network that not only captures
individual instances but also their temporal and cause-and-effect relationships. The
graph is then mapped into a low-dimensional vector space and merged with traffic, log,
and event embeddings, allowing the classifier to leverage both contextual and statistical
information.
In the task of identifying network attacks, the key to building a knowledge graph lies in
defining the relationships between nodes and edges. For example, the attacker’s behavior, the target of the attack, the technical path of the attack, and so on, can be regarded
as nodes in the graph, and the logical relationships between them (such as the attack
path, attack means, and attack target) are viewed as edges. The following are some common attack patterns and their representation in the knowledge graph:

2. MITM (Man-in-the-Middle Attack) The attacker conducts the attack by intercepting

subsequently lead to the spread of malware. By analyzing the relationships in the knowledge graph, we can identify potential threats associated with attack behaviors.

3.8 Graph reasoning

In the task of network attack identification, graph reasoning can infer new attack paths
by analyzing existing attack paths.
Assume that an attack path is known P1 = {v1 , v2 , . . . , vn }, graph reasoning can
calculate the path P1 and other paths (such as P2 ,P3 . These similar paths may have
appeared in the past attack history, or they may be potential attack paths that have not
appeared but have a high degree of similarity. If a path P1 indicates that an attacker
carried out a DDoS attack after a network scan, the path P2 may indicate that another
attacker launched an attack in a similar network environment using the same or similar means. By calculating the Jaccard similarity between P1 paths P2 , we can determine
their similarity and thus infer that the two paths may belong to the same attack pattern.
Based on the reasoning of existing paths, graph reasoning can not only identify known
attack patterns, but also help predict new attack paths. A In the past, P1 attacks were
launched by paths, and graph reasoning can be used to compare the calculated paths
P1 with other attack paths (e.g. P2 , P3 etc.), and infer the attacker A It is possible that
attacks on other targets may be launched along similar paths. Through path reasoning,
the system can automatically identify potential attack behaviors and help the security
defense system respond in advance.

In terms of advantages, first, the new algorithm can fully utilize information from multiple data sources, thereby improving the accuracy of attack identification through feature extraction and fusion. Secondly, compared with other algorithms, it can maintain
high detection accuracy even with limited training data, which is particularly important
for practical application scenarios with limited data resources or difficulty in obtaining
a large amount of labeled data. Furthermore, the algorithm’s consistently superior performance in the face of various types of attacks, such as DoS, SQL injection, and APT,
demonstrates its strong generalization ability and capacity to cope with complex attacks.
The results indicate multimodal integration and semantic reasoning have more than
incremental improvement contributions. By overcoming data imbalance and low interpretability challenges, the framework transforms detection systems from local classifiers

into adaptive analytical tools that not only accurately classify but also proactively deliver

Although the proposed architecture exhibits high performance on benchmark data,
some drawbacks are worth noting for a realistic assessment of its deployability. For one,
the computational cost associated with multimodal fusion and knowledge graph embedding can limit its deployment on resource-limited platforms. Building and maintaining
a knowledge graph in real-time requires substantial processing and memory resources,
which can slow down the system’s response in high-traffic situations.
Second, the model presumes synchronized multimodal data sources (traffic, logs, and
events) to access. In most real-world environments, these data may be noisy, incomplete,
or logged infrequently, thereby limiting the performance of the fusion method. Handling
missing or corrupted modality remains an open problem and might require adaptive
imputation or modality-agnostic networks. Third, scalability is an issue when operating
the system on extremely large enterprise or ISP-sized networks. While ensemble learning and graph reasoning improve detection accuracy, they are more computationally
intensive and add to training and inference latency as compared to single-model methods. The balance between accuracy and latency will be crucial to the success of realworld deployments.
From a deployment perspective, integration with current Security Information and
Event Management (SIEM) systems and intrusion detection streams must be carefully
optimized to avoid alert fatigue. The use of graph reasoning by the system for explainability is beneficial; however, the interpretability of graph output may necessitate specialized training for analysts. Through all these challenges, the approach has robust
pragmatic applicability. It provides actionable, understandable early alerts that can
help security analysts prioritize threats, especially for complex or new forms of attacks.
Moreover, its modularity enables it to be tailored for hybrid cloud and edge use cases,
providing avenues for scalable deployment in today’s cybersecurity landscape.
To validate the robustness and universality of the proposed model, the study extended
the evaluation beyond performance testing on a single dataset. We used two dissimilar benchmark datasets, CICIDS2017 and NSL-KDD, both of which contain extremely
different traffic behaviors, attack types, and collection windows. CICIDS2017 contains
realistic contemporary attack scenarios (e.g., DDoS, Botnet, Infiltration), whereas NSLKDD, although outdated, is still commonly used in benchmarking due to its well-defined
DoS, Probe, U2R, and R2L coverage. With the continually improved performance on
both test sets (Tables 5, 6, 7), the proposed model demonstrates robust adaptability
across various network conditions.
Furthermore, the multimodal data fusion strategy—combining traffic streams, system
logs, and event information—was particularly developed to simulate real-deployment
conditions. As a result, its robustness against dataset-tailored biases and usability in
production-grade cybersecurity frameworks are evidenced. Testing will, in the future, be
augmented with real-time traffic within enterprise-grade environments for broader realworld generalizability testing.

The static nature of fixed models can limit their effectiveness in detecting novel or
evolving attack patterns, as they are unable to adapt to previously unseen threats. To
overcome this limitation, the model can be incrementally updated using techniques
such as online learning and federated learning. Online learning allows the model to continuously learn from new incoming data, enabling timely adaptation to emerging attack
patterns. Federated learning facilitates decentralized updates from multiple sources
while preserving data privacy, making it suitable for distributed environments. These
approaches ensure that the model remains up-to-date and responsive without requiring
full retraining, enhancing its capability to handle evolving threats effectively.
5.1 Limitation

Despite the strong performance of the proposed framework, several limitations affect
its practical deployment. The computational overhead of multimodal fusion, ensemble
learning, and knowledge graph embedding increases processing and memory requirements, potentially restricting deployment on resource-constrained platforms. The
model also relies on synchronized and complete multimodal inputs (traffic, logs, and
events), and missing, noisy, or infrequently logged data can degrade performance. Scalability remains a concern when applied to large-scale enterprise or ISP networks due to
increased training and inference latency. Real-time detection may be hindered by these
computational costs, limiting timely response in high-traffic scenarios. Additionally,
fixed models may struggle with novel or evolving attack patterns, necessitating incremental, online, or federated learning for continuous adaptation. Finally, while knowledge
graph reasoning enhances interpretability, specialized training may be required for analysts to fully utilize the outputs.

6 Conclusion
This paper presents a high-tech multimodal AI system in detecting network intrusion
and advanced warning regarding the growing complexity of modern cyber threats with
multimodal analyses of network traffic, system logs, and event data. Through the use of
deep learning architectures (CNNs to predict spatial patterns, LSTM to predict temporal dependencies, and Autoencoders to predict anomalies) and ensemble processes and

knowledge graph reasoning, the proposed system can be successful in pattern recognition of various attack vectors when evaluated on CICIDS2017, e.g. DoS, SQL injection,
brute force, and insider threat. Experimental outcomes indicate statistically significant
improvement over baselines– improvements on F1-shows of 4.5–11.2% ( p = 0.001,
Cohens d = 0.6211.2), mean per-class Precision/F1 of approximately 0.65 and strong
scale with training set size - especially in high dimensional, imbalanced datasets, where
traditional models fail.
6.1 Limitations

Although it has good detection performance, the framework cannot scale to enterprise
size deployments due to computational bottlenecks that exist because of multimodal
fusion and knowledge graph operations. The inference on-the-fly is limited by the model
latency, which requires lightweight frameworks or edge acceleration. The efficacy of
fixed pre-trained models concerning zero-day attacks is also lower and calls towards the
dynamic adaptation.
6.2 Future work

The next generation will focus on scalability through using knowledge graphs by pruning and extending federated learning; real-time implementation through online/incremental training and on-stream integration; and resilience through adversarial training
and cross-domain transfer (IoT, cloud, ICS). The roadmap should involve a gradual process where: (612 months) an optimization of the fusion; (16 months) the streaming and
heterogeneous testing, (23 months) the federated/adversarial validation. Such improvements will result in a deployable, interpretable dynamic network defense system, and
demonstrate theoretical promise in practice, as the basis of cybersecurity resilience.
