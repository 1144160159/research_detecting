Meihui Zhong , Ting Zhong , Member, IEEE, Yong Wang , Kai Chen , Senior Member, IEEE,

Abstract—Encrypted Malicious Traffic Detection (EMTD) is
challenging due to the limitations of inspecting encrypted content
and the scarcity of labeled data. Graph Contrastive Learning
(GCL) offers a promising direction by representing traffic as
graphs, yet existing approaches struggle to capture global relationships and remain vulnerable to noise. To address these, we
propose KerCL, a Kernel-Enhanced Contrastive Learning framework for robust EMTD. KerCL introduces kernel-based graph
convolution to capture both local and global traffic dependencies,
enhancing the ability to understand the long-range context of
encrypted traffic. Furthermore, we propose a dependency-guided
graph reconstruction strategy, which restructures adjacency
matrices based on learned kernel similarities, ensuring structural
integrity while improving diversity in augmented views. To reduce
noise-induced semantic drift, we introduce a novel contrastive
dependency alignment mechanism. It leverages the HilbertSchmidt independence criterion to enforce semantic consistency
across augmented views while preserving stability of traffic
representations. Experiments on several benchmarks show that
KerCL surpasses previous methods in consistency, robustness,
and noise tolerance. On the DoHBrw dataset with 50% noise
and only 2,000 labels, KerCL improves the F1-score by over
28% compared with the best baseline, showcasing its effectiveness
under noisy, label-sparse network conditions.
Index Terms—Encrypted malicious detection, Kernel-based
graph convolution, Hilbert–Schmidt independence criterion,
graph contrastive learning, self-supervised learning.

To this end, we propose the Kernel-Enhanced Contrastive
Learning framework (KerCL), a novel graph learning
approach designed to enhance EMTD. Technically, this models
global flow dependencies of through kernels, reconstructs the
source graph to generate contrastive views, and introduces
a guided alignment mechanism to mitigate noise interference, ultimately improving detection accuracy and robustness.
Structurally, it comprises three key components: global dependency modeling, dependency-guided graph reconstruction, and
semantic alignment.
Specifically, global dependency modeling first learns a
kernel matrix that reflects relations within the original flow
graph. Relying on high-order feature propagation, it infers
node relations from feature similarity rather than from a

A. Encrypted Malicious Traffic Detection
EMTD has become central to network defense as encryption
protocols proliferate. Existing approaches fall into three broad
classes.
(1) Rule-based Methods. These methods rely on constructing blacklists of IP addresses, certificates, or ports associated
with known attackers [20], [21]. Although fast and easy
to deploy, they are inherently static; adversaries can evade
them by frequently changing observable features, and their
effectiveness therefore decays in dynamic environments.
(2) Statistics-based Methods. These approaches learn from
flow-level statistics such as packet size, inter-arrival time, and
session duration. For instance, XGBoost [22] uses sparse sensing and weighted quantile sketches to model high-dimensional

identify and mitigate ambiguous traffic effects. However, such
methods are vulnerable to traffic variation and noise, which
lead to elevated false-positive and false-negative rates when
data are sparse or noisy.
(3) Deep Learning-based Methods. These methods offer
powerful alternatives by learning complex representations
from raw network data [24], [25]. They can be further classified by feature extraction strategies: Packet-level models, such
as Deep Fingerprinting [26] treats website trace as a sequence
of packets, leveraging sequential learning method to classify
traffic flows using raw packet data. FS-Net [27] similarly
processes raw packet data, mining potential sequence features
from streams, and incorporating the reconstruction mechanism
to improve the features effectiveness. Graph-based methods,
including GraphDApp [24], TrafficGCN [28], ST-Graph [29]
and EC-GCN [30], transform sequences into graphs. They
represent dependencies among entities (e.g., IPs, sessions, or
flows) for effective representation learning. And image-based
methods, such as TAM-CNN [31] and ATVITSC [25], treat

B. Semi-Supervised Learning

Encrypted network traffic comprises data packets exchanged
between hosts within a network. This traffic can be analyzed
at multiple granularities, from individual packets to aggregated
entities such as flows, sessions, or channels. We adopt a
flow-based setting: a flow is a sequence of packets sharing
a common 4-tuple: source IP, destination IP, source port,
destination port.
We model encrypted network traffic as a graph G =
(V, E, A, H). Nodes vi ∈ V represent hosts identified by
IP addresses. Edges E are established using heuristics such
as flows sharing the same destination IP address and port.
The binary adjacency matrix A ∈ {0, 1}n×n encodes these
connections, where Aij = 1 indicates an edge and Aij = 0
otherwise. Each node is associated with a feature vector
in the initial node-feature matrix constructed from raw perflow/session features H ∈ Rn×d , where each row contains
a d-dimensional feature vector, and this matrix serves as the
input to the propagation stage. Feature fields include common
flow statistics such as packet counts, flow duration, and interpacket intervals. We consider a C-class classification problem
with label set Y = {1, . . . , C}. For each labeled node vi ,
yi ∈ Y denotes its class, and we use c ∈ Y to index classes.
Building on prior graph-based approaches to EMTD [12],
[37], we implement a semi-supervised transductive learning
framework. Given G with nl labeled nodes, our goal is to
learn f (·) to predict the label distribution ŷt for an unlabeled
node vt by leveraging both labeled and unlabeled nodes.

Semi-Supervised Learning (SSL) has gained traction as a
means of extracting supervisory signals from unlabeled data,
offering a promising direction for tasks like EMTD. SSL
is particularly valuable in graph-based tasks, where large
amounts of unlabeled data can be utilized to learn effective
representations. Recent studies in EMTD have explored several SSL frameworks, categorized as follows:
(1) Data-Level Augmentation. Models such as TrafCL
[32] randomly drops packets to generate augmented views.
MTC-MAE [33] constructs a unsupervised pretext task with
a random masking strategy to get augmentation views. FeCoGraph [17], a label-aware federated GCL framework, enhances
flow embedding discriminability by optimizing intra-class and
inter-class distances; These methods effectively exploit data
variability but risk semantic loss under noisy conditions due to
unguided augmentations, limiting their robustness in EMTD’s
label-scarce, noisy environments.
(2) Representation-Level Augmentation. Some methods
inject perturbations into inputs to regularize representation distributions, generating semantically diverse views. For instance,
TCG-IDS [34] proposes three graph contrastive strategies:
temporal contrasting to capture node evolution over time,
asymmetric contrasting to model relationships between nodes,
and random masking of node or edge features during training
to force the model to learn from residual data; While boosting representation flexibility, these heuristic perturbations can
amplify noise distortions, exacerbating semantic inconsistency
in noisy encrypted traffic, as highlighted in our earlier analysis.
(3) Local-Global Augmentations. CoMDet [35] introduces
an inter-modal contrastive learning approach, enhancing feature representations by maximizing mutual information across
modalities. PeerG [36] employs graph contrastive learning to

ensuring scalability and resilience to noise.

embeddings with good initial separability, we introduce
a self-supervised semantic alignment step to make these
representations more robust to noise. This improved
robustness, in turn, further enhances the final class separability.

that incorporates semantic alignment through the HilbertSchmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC) [38]. This method
ensures that the representations from the original graph and
its augmented view remain consistent with the underlying
semantics, addressing challenges such as semantic drift
and representation collapse, which are often encountered in
self-supervised learning on graphs.

A. Experimental Setup
Datasets. We benchmark our proposed KerCL against the
following approaches.
(i) TONIoT [39]: This dataset represents a new generation
of Internet of Things (IoT) and Industrial Internet of Things
(IIoT) traffic. It comprises 796,380 normal and 21,542,641
malicious instances with 10 distinct types, encompassing 39
statistical features;
(ii) DoHBrw [40]: This dataset is designed for the analysis,
testing, and evaluation of DNS over HTTPS (DoH) encrypted
traffic. This dataset offers 19,807 normal and 354,996 malicious instances, each characterized by 28 statistical features;
(iii) CICIDS [41]: This dataset contains both normal communication patterns and 14 distinct attack scenarios, with
931,873 normal instances and 18,028 malicious instances.
Each sample is described by 70 features, extracted using the
CICFlowMeter-V3 [42].
Baselines. To benchmark our KerCL, we choose the following approaches for evaluation:
(i) Supervised based EMTD, including
MTSecurity [7]. It integrates raw byte-level features (via
Transformers) and graph-based traffic interaction features (via
GNNs) to enhance encrypted traffic representations, capturing
sequence and network patterns despite payload invisibility.
(ii) Unsupervised based EMTD, including
Kitsune [2]. It detects anomalies using unsupervised learning on raw traffic, adapted here for supervised classification;
ContraMTD [12]. It models both sequence and topological
relationships in host interaction graphs using proposed SEC
and DE-GAT mechanisms. It employs contrastive learning
with vertical and horizontal views of traffic behavior. This
enables comprehensive modeling of normal network behavior
across different views.
(iii) Pre-train based EMTD, including:
ET-BERT [43]. It utilizes the raw traffic data of the first
n packets of flow as their input. It transforms the datagram
of bytes to a language-like token and uses BERT pre-training
methods for learning the contextualized datagram presentation
from large unlabeled datasets. After that, it uses fine-tuning as
its tuning method to transfer the well-learned presentation to
downstream tasks;
Flow-MAE [44]. It improves upon ET-BERT framework by
adopting an asymmetric encoder-decoder architecture, which
is similar to masked autoencoder (MAE). This architecture

generates representations based on byte streams and uses a
lightweight decoder to reconstruct the masked bytes;
YaTC [45]. It utilizes the raw traffic data of the first n packets of flow as their input. It also uses the MAE paradigm as its
pre-training method by reconstructing the masked traffic. After
that, it uses fine-tuning as its tuning method for downstream
tasks as well;
TrafCL [32]. It enhances session-level data by creating augmented, incomplete views of the sessions. These incomplete
views are then input into a contrastive learning framework,
enabling better representation learning from partial data;
FeCoGraph [17]. It is a label-aware federated GCL framework optimizing intra-class and inter-class distances for flow
embedding discriminability.
Metrics. Our model aims to classify encrypted traffic into
normal and various malicious types. Given the inherent class
imbalance in malicious and normal traffic, we evaluate our
KerCL and other using accuracy (AC), precision (PR), and
F1-score (F1).
Testbeds.. Our experiments are conducted on a platform
with an Intel i5-14600KF 3.50 GHz CPU, an NVIDIA
GeForce GTX 4060 Ti GPU, and 32GB of system memory.
We implemented graph-based encoders for baselines models
using the PyTorch Geometric (PyG) library. Additionally, since
Kitsune cannot handle high-rate traffic, we evaluate it with
offline experiments on the same testbed.
Implementation Details. To further evaluate the robustness
of KerCL, all experiments were conducted under a noise
level of 0.3 to simulate real-world noisy environments. Unless
otherwise specified, Gaussian noise was applied to the original
data, and the number of training nodes was limited, with 1,000
labeled samples per category used. The Adam optimizer is
employed for model training. Early stopping was employed,
halting training if no parameter improvement was observed for
50 consecutive epochs.

C. Robustness of the Model (Q2)
To evaluate the robustness of the proposed KerCL framework, we conducted comprehensive experiments across

various noise levels and label-scarce conditions. Robustness
in EMTD is critical due to the inherent noise and incomplete
session information present in real-world network environments. We designed experiments focusing on two key aspects:
effectiveness under limited labeled data and resistance to noise
perturbation.
We first evaluated KerCL’s performance under label-scarce
scenarios by varying the number of labeled samples per
class during training process. As shown in Figure 3, KerCL
achieves superior performance compared to other pretrainingbased models. With only 100 labeled samples per class,
KerCL maintains high classification F1-score. This highlights
KerCL’s ability to captured the key semantic information in
the traffic graph through its kernel-enhanced global dependency modeling, and contrastive learning based on sematic
alignment further utilized the data effectively.
Next, we assessed KerCL’s resistance to noise by introducing synthetic noise (randomly corrupting node features
and edge connections) at increasing rates. Figure 4 illustrates
performance trends of KerCL and baselines under increasing
noise levels on DoHBrw dataset. Notably, KerCL consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, maintaining high
accuracy and F1-scores even when subjected to substantial data corruption. Additionally, after 100 training runs,
KerCL exhibits significantly lower variance compared to other
methods.
From a theoretical perspective, the robustness of KerCL
arises from the following: 1) the HSIC-based alignment,
which constrains the learned representations by maximizing
mutual dependence between original and augmented views,
preserving semantic consistency despite noisy augmentations;
2) the kernel enhanced global dependency modeling module,
which captures global dependencies while minimizing the
impact of local feature noise, providing an implicit smoothing
effect that avoids over-smoothing issues in traditional GNNs;
3) and the combined contrastive and HSIC losses that reduce
the generalization bound by ensuring diverse yet semantically
aligned representations, thereby improving performance under
both noise-free and noisy conditions.

V. C ONCLUSION AND F UTURE W ORK
In this work, we propose KerCL, a novel kernel-enhanced
contrastive learning framework tailored for EMTD. By integrating global dependency modeling, kernel-based graph
reconstruction, and HSIC-guided semantic alignment, KerCL
effectively addresses challenges posed by limited labels,
insufficient structural context, and noise sensitivity in graphbased learning. Experimental evaluations on three datasets
demonstrate that KerCL outperforms state-of-the-art methods,
particularly in noisy environments and scenarios with limited
labeled data. Our future work will focus on reducing this
complexity. Furthermore, we plan to validate our method’s
robustness against more sophisticated adaptive threats and
extend its applicability by incorporating temporal dynamics
and protocol-agnostic modeling for emerging traffic patterns.
