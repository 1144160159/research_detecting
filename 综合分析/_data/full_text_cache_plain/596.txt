Adaptive Graph Filtering Neural Network
for Graph Anomaly Detection
Zhizhe Liu , Shuai Zheng , Yeyu Yan, Zhenfeng Zhu , and Yao Zhao , Fellow, IEEE

Abstract—Graph anomaly detection (GAD) plays an important
role in improving public safety and product quality and has attracted a great deal of interest in recent years. Although a wide
range of progress has been achieved recently, the following challenges still remain: (1) abnormal nodes mixed in the normal node
subgraph and (2) global-consistency filtering to different features.
To overcome these challenges, we propose AGFNN, a novel adaptive
graph filtering neural network designed to handle diverse mixed
local patterns and feature variations, thereby improving model
fitting from both the node and feature perspectives. Specifically,
to enhance the discriminative capacity of the node representation, channel-wise feature adaptive filtering is proposed to learn
a specific filter for each feature in a progressive way, which first
performs multi-frequency filtering and then adaptively captures
the importance of different frequency components for each feature.
Meanwhile, to better fit the complex local subgraphs, the node’s
preference for multi-frequency information can be self-adjusted by
learning node-aware bias, which is also equal to learning a specific
filter for each node. Extensive experiments on real-world graph
datasets demonstrate that AGFNN outperforms the state-of-the-art
methods.
Index Terms—Graph anomaly detection, graph filtering, graph
representation, graph neural networks.

II. RELATED WORKS
A. Graph Anomaly Detection
Recently, with the success of graph neural networks (GNNs)
in a variety of graph learning tasks [16], [17], a large number of
studies have explored using GNNs for graph anomaly detection
(GAD), as shown in Table I. According to the perspective of
information processing, existing GAD methods can be roughly
categorized into spatial-based and spectral-based approaches.
Specifically, we further categorize the existing spatial-based
GAD methods into three classes.
1) Graph reconstruction-based methods. Among spatialbased approaches, one representative paradigm is graph reconstruction methods [7], [23], [24], [25], [26], [38], [39],
[40], which aim to reconstruct the graph topology or node
attributes and then detect anomalies based on reconstruction
errors. This line of work assumes that abnormal nodes, due
to their inconsistency with the overall graph structure, are
more difficult to reconstruct than normal ones. Early efforts
mainly employed autoencoder-based architectures. For instance,
DOMINANT [23] designed a deep autoencoder framework that
simultaneously reconstructs the node attributes and adjacency

To further enhance the representation learning capacity, subsequent studies incorporated higher-order or community-level

reconstruction strategies tailored for complex or noisy graphs.
GAD-NR [25] reconstructed not only a node’s attributes but
also its entire neighborhood, thus capturing both local and
contextual discrepancies. To mitigate overfitting and alleviate
the homophily trap, ADA-GAD [40] proposed a two-stage
framework consisting of anomaly-denoised augmentation and
distribution regularization. Meanwhile, G3AD [26] introduced
auxiliary networks with correlation constraints to stabilize representation learning and reduce inconsistency issues.
2) Aggregation mechanism-based methods. Another important line of spatial-based GAD methods focuses on improving
the aggregation mechanism in GNNs. These approaches [7],
[18], [27], [28], [29], [30], [41] aim to refine the neighbor
aggregation process so that the model can better distinguish
informative neighbors and reduce the negative effects caused by
abnormal or irrelevant nodes during message passing. To optimize the GNN aggregation process, CARE-GNN [18] devised a
label-aware similarity measure to identify informative neighbors
and employed reinforcement learning to adaptively select the
optimal number of neighbors for aggregation. PCGNN [27] further introduced a label-balanced sampler to construct subgraphs
for mini-batch training, ensuring balanced aggregation across
classes. To handle both homophilic and heterophilic connections, H2-FDetector [28] developed an information aggregation
strategy that enables homophilic edges to propagate similar
information while allowing heterophilic edges to deliver distinct
information. More recently, PMP [29] adopted a node-specific
aggregation function to separately aggregate neighbors from
different classes, further enhancing the adaptability of the aggregation process.
3) Graph contrastive learning-based methods. Recently,
graph contrastive learning [31], [32], [33], [42], [43], [44], [45]

has been introduced into the GAD task to improve node representation by maximizing the agreement between semantically
similar nodes or subgraphs while distinguishing abnormal patterns. These methods generally design contrastive objectives or
sampling strategies to capture the inherent consistency of normal
nodes and expose anomalies through inconsistent representations. CoLA [42] introduced a sampling strategy to construct
contrastive instance pairs and exploited local structural information from the graph to learn normal patterns. Building on this
idea, NLGAD [43] employed multi-scale contrastive learning
to enhance the modeling of normal behavioral patterns across
different structural levels. Differently, SL-GAD [31] constructed
multiple contextual subgraphs (views) around a target node and
integrated a generative attribute regression module with a multiview contrastive learning framework for anomaly detection. To
further refine the contrastive process, GCCAD [32] designed
a GNN encoder capable of inferring and removing suspicious
links during message passing, thus improving the reliability
of contrastive learning. Meanwhile, ARISE [33] proposed a
region proposal module to identify high-density substructures
and leverage subgraph-level information for contrastive representation learning. Recently, GRADATE [44] developed a
multi-view and multi-scale contrastive learning framework that
performs subgraph-to-subgraph contrast to capture both global
and local anomaly cues.
Spectral-based GAD methods. Different from spatial-based
methods, spectral-based GAD [21], [22], [34], [35], [36], [37],
[46], [47] approaches focus on analyzing graph signals in the
frequency domain. Since abnormal nodes usually exhibit dissimilar features from their neighbors, such behavior corresponds
to high-frequency components in the spectral space. This observation motivates researchers to design spectral filtering strategies that can effectively capture and combine information from
both low- and high-frequency domains for anomaly detection.
IDGL [46] designed an adaptive dual-channel convolution filter that combines low- and high-frequency information from
neighboring nodes to both assimilate nodes with assortative
connections and distinguish those with disassortative edges.
Similarly, AMNet [22] employed a multi-frequency filter group
to jointly learn representations from different spectral bands.
Tang et al. [21] observed a “right-shift” phenomenon in the
spectral distribution caused by anomalies and proposed a beta

wavelet-based GNN with band-pass filtering capability to address this issue. By dividing the spectrum into multiple mixedfrequency bands, Xu et al. [34] developed a hybrid filtering
module to alleviate the effects of heterophily. Based on Chebyshev polynomial filtering, Wang et al. [36] proposed a semantic
fusion-based node representation framework to integrate information from various frequency components. CIE-GAD [35]
introduced a spectral convolution mechanism with node-level attention fusion to adaptively capture multifrequency signals. Furthermore, FAGAD [37] developed a frequency self-adaptation
framework that dynamically fuses signals across multiple frequency bands to enhance representation discriminability.
B. Spectral-Based Graph Neural Networks
The main idea of spectral-based GNNs [48], [49], [50], [51],
[52], [53], [54] is to design spectral graph filters in the spectral domain, utilizing the theory of graph signal processing.
Chebnet [48] employed Chebyshev polynomials to approximate a filter, and in this work, GCN [55] adopted a firstorder approximation to further simplify ChebNet. To tackle
the over-smooth problem contained in the original GCN, Dong
et al. [49] designed AdaGNN, which achieved adaptive frequency response filters for different feature channels to capture
the varying importance of different frequency components. Similarly, FAGCN [56] proposed to adaptively integrate different
frequency signals by a self-gating mechanism in the aggregation process. Recently, to improve the reliability of the learned
filters, the BernNet [50] was proposed to estimate an arbitrary
graph filter by a K-order Bernstein polynomial approximation.
Furthermore, Zheng et al. [51] proposed node-oriented spectral
filtering for a graph neural network to capture precise local node
positioning via the generalized translated operator.

A. Feature Adaptive Filtering.
As we observed previously, different features present inconsistent variations within a subgraph, and as a result, the
global-consistency filtering to different features may greatly
limit the discriminative capability of the node representation. To
tackle this issue, a natural solution would be to learn a specific
filter directly for arbitrary features. However, it is not trivial
as it would require expensive eigenvalue decomposition. As
a preliminary attempt, a novel channel-wise feature adaptive
filtering (CFAF) module is proposed to achieve the above goal
in a progressive way. CFAF first performs multi-frequency filtering and then adaptively captures the importance of different
frequency components for each channel. Therefore, a specific

that the simple aggregation mechanism described above limits
the capability of the model to fit complex local subgraphs.
Therefore, we propose to learn node-aware bias {αi }M
i=1 to
adjust the importance of different frequency signals to the node
representation adaptively, where αi ∈ Rn is the importance of
the representation Zi to all nodes V.

As previously discussed, our objective is to implement adaptive filtering for features in a progressive manner. This can
be achieved by combining the multi-frequency filter group to
estimate the channel-specific filter. To this end, the filter group
F needs to encompass a range of high-quality spectral property
filters. This diverse set of filters aids in reducing the complexity
associated with estimating the specific filter for each channel.
Specifically, to reduce the computational overhead, we adopt a
simple but elegant way where the filter group is composed of a
low-pass filter, a band-pass filter, and a high-pass filter, i.e., M =
3. Specifically, each of the above filters plays a complementary
role in detecting distinct types of anomalies. The low-pass filter
is particularly effective for detecting global structural anomalies,
such as nodes that deviate significantly from the overall network
patterns. In contrast, the band-pass filter excels at identifying

A. Experimental Setup
Datasets and baselines: Eight benchmark GAD datasets
are adopted for conducting extensive experiments, including YelpChi [6], Amazon [63], Elliptic [8], T-Finance [21],
Weibo [64], Reddit [65], Tolokers [66], and Questions [66].
The statistical information of all datasets is presented in Table II. We compare AGFNN with three categories of various
methods: 1) four classical GNN methods, including GCN [55],
GAT [67], GIN [68] and GraphSAGE [69]; 2) two representative spectral filtering methods, including ChebNet [48] and
BernNet [50]. 3) four representative graph-based anomaly detection methods, including spectral filtering-based AMNet [22],
BWGNN [21], data augmentation-based ConsisGAD [70], and
message passing-based PMP [29]. Meanwhile, two widely
adopted metrics are selected to evaluate the performance of all
methods, including F1-macro and AUROC. The F1-macro is an
unweighted average of the F1 scores of the normal and abnormal
categories and thus can be independent of the unbalanced ratio
between the two classes.
Implementation details: To comprehensively evaluate our
method, both the supervised scenario (40%) and the semisupervised scenario (1%) settings are adopted in our experiments, while the remaining data are divided into the validation
and test datasets in the ratio of 1:2. On the Elliptic, Amazon,
Weibo, Reddit, Questions, and Tolokers datasets, the hidden
dimensions f1 and f2 are set to 64. For the T-Finance and
YelpChi (1%) datasets, the hidden dimensions f1 and f2 are
set to 32, while in the YelpChi (40%) dataset, they are set to
128. The polynomial order K is set to 4 for all datasets, with
the exception of the elliptic dataset, where K is increased to
