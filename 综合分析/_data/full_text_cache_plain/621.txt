CAFFT: Cross-Attention Feature Fusion
Transformer for Risky Driving
Behavior Recognition
Sheng Wang , Jun Liang , Xinqi Yu , Wensa Wang , Chaofeng Pan, and Long Chen

Abstract—According to the results of a survey administered
by the World Health Organization, the primary cause of the
majority of traffic accidents is risky driving behavior. Consequently, the identification of risky driving behaviors is imperative
for ensuring road safety. In order to effectively address the
limitations of existing methods in modeling long-term dependencies, integrating external scenario information, and adapting to
different driver personalities, this paper proposes a Transformerbased cross-attention feature fusion model for risky driving
behavior recognition, referred to as CAFFT. This model utilizes
a hierarchical Transformer encoder structure to capture finegrained changes and long-term dependencies in driving behavior.
Furthermore, the cross-attention mechanism, a cross-modal
attention mechanism, is introduced to effectively fuse driving
behavior features with weather-related external scenario features,
thereby rendering the research scenario more aligned with realworld driving conditions. Finally, through cross-validation and
cross-test set generalization experiments, the universality and
stability of the method are verified. The experimental results
on real driving datasets demonstrate that the CAFFT model
achieves substantial performance enhancements in recognizing
risky driving behavior. This improvement leads to enhanced
accuracy and reliability in recognizing risky driving behavior,
thereby providing substantial support for the development of
intelligent driving assistance systems.
Index Terms—Risky driving behavior, deep learning, multimodal feature fusion, situational awareness, driving safety.

II. R ELATED W ORK
A. Risky Driving Behavior Recognition
There are two main types of data used to identify risky
driving behavior. One is image data, which is mainly used to
identify distracted and fatigued driving by analyzing images of
the driver in the vehicle [9], [10]. The other is time-series data
containing driving behavior information, such as trajectory
data and driving operation data, which is mainly used to
identify risky driving behavior caused by the driver’s improper
operation of the vehicle, such as sudden acceleration, sudden
deceleration, and continuous lane changes [11], [12]. This
section focuses on risky driving behavior detection methods
based on time-series data. In recent years, researchers have
proposed various risky driving behavior detection methods
based on time-series data, which can be roughly divided into
three categories: statistical classification, traditional machine
learning, and deep learning.
Statistical classification methods were among the first methods used to detect risky driving behavior. Such methods
typically first extract statistical features from driving behavior
data, such as average speed, maximum acceleration, steering
wheel rotation frequency, etc., and then determine whether the

5821

driving behavior is risky based on set thresholds. For example,
Zhang et al. [13] proposed a statistical rule-based method to
identify risky driving behavior by analyzing the behavioral
characteristics of drivers, such as speed, acceleration, and
braking, to determine whether they are driving while fatigued.
This method is simple, easy to understand, and computationally efficient, but it relies on manually designed features and
has difficulty capturing complex patterns in driving behavior
data.
In order to overcome the limitations of statistical classification methods, researchers began to use traditional machine
learning methods for the recognition of risky driving behavior.
Machine learning models, including support vector machines
(SVM), decision trees, and naive Bayes, have been extensively
employed in the domain of risky driving behavior recognition [14], [15], [16]. For instance, Yu et al. [14] proposed
an SVM-based model for identifying risky driving behavior.
This model analyzes behavioral characteristics such as the
driver’s speed, acceleration, and steering wheel rotation to
determine whether the driver is in a dangerous driving state.
Compared with statistical classification methods, traditional
machine learning methods can automatically learn patterns in
driving behavior data and achieve higher recognition accuracy.
However, traditional machine learning models still rely on
manually designed features and have difficulty processing
high-dimensional, nonlinear driving behavior data.
The advent of deep learning technology has prompted
researchers to adopt deep learning models for the recognition
of risky driving behavior. Deep learning models, including
recurrent neural networks (RNNs), long short-term memory networks (LSTMs), and convolutional neural networks
(CNNs), have been extensively employed in the domain of
risky driving behavior recognition [3], [6]. For instance,
Yang et al. [17] proposed a behavior prediction model,
comprising a CNN and a two-layer LSTM, which integrates vehicle lane-change feasibility and lateral offset rate of
change. This model effectively identifies high-risk lane-change
behaviors. A comparison of deep learning-based models with
traditional machine learning methods reveals that the former
can automatically learn high-dimensional, nonlinear features in
driving behavior data, achieving higher recognition accuracy.
Nevertheless, these models continue to exhibit limitations in
their capacity to manage long-range dependencies.
B. Multimodal Fusion
In real-world scenarios, information from a single source
frequently exhibits limitations, failing to satisfy the system’s
requirements for perception capabilities and decision-making
precision. Consequently, the integration of data from diverse
modalities and sources to construct more comprehensive and
stable systems has emerged as a pivotal area of research
interest. Multimodal data fusion is the key technology to
address this issue, aiming to integrate data from different
modalities and leverage their complementarity and redundancy
to enhance the overall system performance [18]. Multimodal
data fusion has been widely applied in various fields, such as
image captioning [19] (combining image content with textual
descriptions), video understanding (fusing visual, auditory,

and textual information), and sentiment analysis (combining
speech intonation with textual content) [20], [21], [22].
Similarly, risky driving behavior is shaped not only by
driver state but also by scenario-level contextual factors and
uncertainty. Therefore, the effective integration of multi-source
heterogeneous data, such as vehicle driving data, environmental perception data, and driver physiological data, is crucial
for improving recognition accuracy.
In recent years, multi-modal data fusion technology has
seen widespread application in the recognition of risky driving
behavior. For instance, certain researchers have integrated
vehicle speed, acceleration, and other driving data with
environmental data, such as weather conditions and road conditions, to enhance the model’s adaptability to complex driving
scenarios [23], [24]. Concurrently, other researchers have
endeavored to integrate driver physiological data, including
facial expressions and eye movements, with vehicle operation
data to enhance the precision of identifying driver fatigue
or distracted driving [25], For instance, Wang et al. [26]
investigated three distinct human-machine interaction (HMI)
modes—control, warning, and guidance—and discovered that
varying information presentation significantly alters drivers’
acceleration behavior, strategy selection, and merging position
in highway merging scenarios. This directly substantiates the
substantial impact of the external interactive environment as a
contextual factor on driving behavior.
According to different fusion strategies, existing multimodal
risky driving behavior recognition methods can be roughly
divided into the following categories: Based on early fusion
methods, Haris and Glowacz [27] spliced vehicle driving data
and environmental information, and then input them into a
deep learning model for training. Based on late fusion methods, Liu et al. [28] extracted vehicle trajectory features and
environmental factors such as weather, and then fused them
by weighted averaging. Based on the attention mechanism,
Mou et al. [29] proposed a multi-modal fusion method based
on the attention mechanism to detect driver fatigue.
While multi-modal data fusion methods have demonstrated
progress in identifying risky driving behavior, the relative
importance of each data source varies across different driving
scenarios. Existing methods do not incorporate situational
factors as important factors affecting driving behavior into
the model, making it difficult to maintain good performance
in complex and changing real driving environments. Concurrently, the prevailing methodologies predominantly emphasize
the integration of static features, while disregarding the recognition that driving behavior is inherently sequential. This
oversight results in an inability to capture the temporal
dependencies inherent in driving behavior data, consequently
hindering the discernment of dynamic variations in driving
behavior.
C. Transformers in Time Series
The Transformer model was first proposed by
Vaswani et al. [30]. This model has been instrumental
in the field of natural language processing (NLP) due to
its pioneering introduction of self-attention mechanisms and
an encoder-decoder architecture. In contrast to conventional

recurrent neural networks (RNNs), the Transformer model
relies exclusively on self-attention mechanisms to process
sequence data in parallel, thereby effectively capturing longrange dependencies. The self-attention mechanism enables the
system to discern associations between disparate positions in
a sequence, thereby facilitating a comprehensive perception
of sequence information. Due to the high computational
complexity of the self-attention mechanism, researchers have
proposed various improved self-attention mechanisms, such
as sparse attention [31] and window attention [32].
In recent years, transformer models have demonstrated
notable advancements in time series tasks, including time
series prediction, anomaly detection, and classification [33],
[34]. A comparison of Transformer models with traditional
time series models (e.g., ARIMA and LSTM) reveals their
superiority in capturing long-term dependencies and enhancing
computational efficiency through parallel computing. In the
field of time series prediction, Li et al. [35] have proposed a
Transformer-based time series prediction model that has been
shown to improve prediction accuracy by learning the longterm trends of time series data. Zhou et al. [31] proposed a
Transformer-based time series prediction model that reduces
computational complexity and improves the efficiency of
long-sequence prediction by introducing the ProbSparse selfattention mechanism. In the context of time series anomaly
detection, Tuli et al. [36] have proposed a Transformer-based
anomaly detection model that identifies abnormal events by
learning the patterns of normal time series.
Despite the noteworthy advancements of Transformer models in time series tasks, their utilization in the domain of risky
driving behavior recognition remains a relatively recent development. In a recent study, You et al. [37] used Transformer
models to learn feature representations in driving behavior
data and input driving behavior sequences into Transformer
models to achieve driving behavior classification. Wang et al.
[38] proposed a novel approach that integrates the transformers
and convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to generate input
vector representations of driving behavior. These representations are then fed into a classifier, with the objective of
identifying whether the driver is distracted. Zhang et al. [39]
developed an effective method for extracting features from
input images using Swin Transformers. They then enhanced
the discriminative power of different categories in the feature
space, achieving high accuracy in distracted driving behavior
recognition tasks.
However, the majority of extant Transformer-based methods
for recognizing risky driving behavior, such as Informer and
the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT), tend to treat driving
behavior data as a whole, thereby overlooking the fine-grained
temporal fluctuations inherent in driving behavior. Concurrently, divergent assessments of driving peril may emerge
under different driving scenarios and external conditions.
These general-purpose Transformer architectures primarily
focus on long-range dependency modeling or temporal feature selection within a single modality, without explicitly
modeling scenario-dependent modulation of driving behavior.
As a result, existing methods struggle to adequately account
for situational variability across different scenarios and are

difficult to adapt to the heterogeneous requirements of realworld risky driving behavior recognition.
III. S YMBOLS , D EFINITIONS , AND TASK D ESCRIPTION
The dataset we used encompasses three distinct sets of timerelated data related to vehicles, namely speed, acceleration,
and angular velocity. In addition, the dataset incorporates
situational factors relevant to driving behavior, such as weather
information. In this section, we delineate several of the definitions and symbols employed in the risky driving behavior
recognition model. Furthermore, we describe the task of
recognizing risky driving behavior by integrating situational
information.
A. Symbols and Definitions
Definition 1 (Trip P): A trip P represents the driving
trajectory data of a vehicle from a dataset during the entire
driving process. It consists of the state Pt of the vehicle at
multiple instantaneous points. The state Pt of the vehicle
at each instant includes speed information vt , acceleration
information at , angular velocity information ωt , and longitude
and latitude information. V ∈ RT , A ∈ RT , and W ∈ RT are
the continuous speed information, acceleration information,
and angular velocity information during the entire trip P,
respectively. T represents the time spent by the vehicle during
trip P, representing the length of the time series. The interval
between every two timestamps is one second.
Definition 2 (Driving Process DP ): The driving process
DP is represented by the driving status of the vehicle within
the selected sliding time window (the size of the sliding time
window is L) for L seconds, i.e., DP ⊆ P. Then, trip P
encompasses N = (T − L)/S + 1 (S is the sliding step length)
driving processes.
Definition 3 (Situational Factors SF): Situational factors
(SF) refer to situational environmental factors that affect vehicle driving behavior, mainly considering weather information.
It includes information such as weather type, wind direction,
wind speed, and temperature. Based on the different data types,
the One-Hot encoding method and normalization method are
used to process this information to obtain a weather data
matrix C∈RT ×D that can be processed by the model (T is the
length of the time series and D is the weather data dimension
at each moment).
B. Task Description
The task of identifying risky driving behavior is described
below. Input: Time series containing vehicle trajectory information (time information t, speed information vt , acceleration
information at , angular velocity information ωt , and longitude
and latitude information); situational factors SF (weather data
matrix C) that affect driving behavior recognition. Output:
Risk driving behavior recognition results P̃, which include all
risky driving behaviors during the entire trip P.
IV. M ETHODS
As illustrated in Fig. 1, the CAFFT model is comprised
of three primary modules: a driving sequence feature learning

5823

module, a weather feature learning module, and a cross-modal
dynamic fusion module.
Due to its excellent performance in capturing long-range
dependencies and interactions, Transformer is well suited for
time series analysis. Consequently, the driving sequence feature learning module is comprised of a hierarchically designed
Transformer encoder. This module not only fully captures the
fine-grained information of driving behavior but also considers
the long-term dependency characteristics of driving behavior.
Additionally, it has been designed to address the issue of
misjudgment and omission in assessing driving behavior risk.
Specifically, we use Transformers to extract historical driving
state information as basic features. Incorporating the selfattention mechanism, we integrate driving process features
under varying window sizes, thereby complementing and
enhancing each other. This approach prevents misjudgment
and the omission of driving behavior risks caused by the
division of the driving process, thus enhancing the accuracy
of feature extraction. In the weather feature learning module, an effective extraction method was designed to identify
weather features related to driving behavior. This method
combines One-Hot encoding, numerical normalization, and a
one-dimensional convolutional network. Furthermore, in the
cross-modal dynamic fusion module, we integrate driving
behavior features with weather features through a crossattention mechanism to characterize the impact of situational
information on driving behavior during the driving process.
This approach aligns more closely with real driving scenarios
and facilitates the identification of risky driving behavior in
the real world.
A. Data Preprocessing and Window Alignment
To effectively identify risky driving behaviors within trajectory data, we first performed outlier detection and removal
from the raw dataset. Specifically, we excluded instances
where vehicles were stationary for extended periods, anomalous readings caused by GPS signal drift, and brief movements
insufficient for driving behavior analysis. Subsequently, a
team comprising data analysts and data quality coordinators
manually verified and analyzed the trajectory data files for
annotation.
“High-risk” driving behavior was defined as the presence
of one or more of the following four conditions: overspeeding, abrupt longitudinal maneuvers, sharp turns, and fatigued
driving. Specifically:
(a) Overspeeding: Vehicle speed exceeding 80 km/h or
40 km/h (in foggy conditions) for a duration greater than
3 seconds.
(b) Abrupt Longitudinal Maneuver: An instantaneous
acceleration ≥ 0.3µg or ≤ −0.3µg occurring
between adjacent sampling points within a
3-second interval, where the friction coefficient
µ is inferred from aligned weather data, with
µ ∈ {0.9 (clear weather) , 0.5 (rain/snowy weather)}.
(c) Sharp Turn: Angular velocity > π/6 rad/s,

can vary in duration. Consequently, a single-grain driving
process extraction is inadequate for accurately describing all
driving behaviors. As demonstrated in Fig. 3, when a limited
time window is selected, the resulting driving behavior may
be deemed safe. However, when an extended time window is

In the local encoder, multi-granularity driving behavior time

driving process from the preprocessed driving trajectory data.
The global sparse attention of the global encoder is utilized to
capture the global dependencies of the driving process and to

behavior feature learning.

the fine-grained fluctuations and long-term dependencies of
driving behavior. This structure contains two main encoder

C. Weather Feature Learning
In order to effectively extract weather features H related to
driving behavior, a feature extraction method was designed
that combines One-Hot encoding, numerical normalization,
and one-dimensional convolutional networks(1D-CNN). This
section will provide a detailed exposition of the preprocessing
of meteorological data and the extraction of features.
Due to the different characteristic attributes of weather
types, wind direction, wind speed, and temperature, different
encoding strategies were adopted to better adapt to the subsequent one-dimensional convolutional network processing.
1) Feature Encoding: Weather type: Weather type is a
discrete variable. To enable it to be processed by the model, it
is encoded using the One-Hot encoding method. We simplify
weather types into four distinct categories (e.g., sunny, hazy,
rainy, snowy). Each weather type is encoded as a sparse
binary vector of length 4, where the element corresponding
to the weather type is 1, and all other elements are 0. This
encoding effectively represents the category information of
weather types.
Wind direction: As with the classification of weather types,
the direction of wind is encoded through the implementation
of the One-Hot encoding method. The eight wind directions
are represented by the following letters: east, west, etc. up to
northeast and northwest. The direction of the wind is encoded
as an 8-length sparse binary vector.
Wind strength: Wind strength is also a discrete variable,
and it is encoded using the One-Hot encoding method. According to the national standard “Wind Force Scale” published by
China in 2012, wind strength is divided into 18 levels [41].
However, given that the experimental data utilized in this study
excludes wind speeds above level 10, the wind strength is
encoded as a sparse binary vector of length 11.
Temperature: Temperature is a continuous numerical variable. To promote the acceleration of model convergence, the
normalization of the temperature data is employed. Specifically, the temperature data is linearly scaled to the interval [0,
1]. The normalization formula is as follows:

VI. C ONCLUSION AND D ISCUSSION
In light of the numerous challenges associated with identifying hazardous driving behaviors, this paper puts forth a
Transformer-based cross-attention feature fusion dangerous
driving behavior recognition model, designated as CAFFT.
Additionally, it assesses the efficacy of the model on a real
dataset.
The CAFFT model is composed of three primary modules:
a driving sequence feature learning module, a weather feature
learning module, and a cross-modal dynamic fusion module.
In the driving sequence feature learning module, firstly, to
circumvent the issue of misjudged results in the driving process segmentation, disparate sizes of sliding time windows are
employed for segmentation. Secondly, to capture the long-term
dependencies and detailed information of the driving behavior, a hierarchical Transformer encoder structure is designed,
where the ProbSparse Attention mechanism is introduced in
the global encoder to capture the long-term dependencies of
the driving behavior, and the window Attention mechanism is
introduced in the local encoder to capture the fine-grained fluctuations. In the weather feature learning module, an effective
extraction method was designed to identify weather features
related to driving behavior. This method combines One-Hot
encoding, numerical normalization, and a one-dimensional
convolutional network. In the cross-modal dynamic fusion
module, we also fuse driving behavioral features with weather
features through a cross-attention mechanism to characterize
the effect of situational factors on driving behavior during the
vehicle’s driving process. Concurrently, in order to enhance
the model’s capacity to adapt to the distinct characteristics of
different drivers, we implemented three-fold cross-validation
during the training process to augment the model’s generalization capability. We subsequently verified the model’s ability
to adapt to individualized differences among different drivers
through cross-test-set generalization tests.
To this end, experimental validation on a real driving dataset
is conducted to evaluate the performance of the proposed
method through a series of experiments, including model

performance validation, ablation experiments, driving process
extraction granularity analysis experiments, and cross-test-set
generalization test experiments. In comparison with benchmark methods, CAFFT achieved an F2-score of 0.858 (95%
CI: 0.840-0.875) and a PR-AUC of 0.892 on a real-world
driving dataset. This represents a 1.2-1.9 percentage point
improvement in F2-score over powerful Transformer-based
models such as Informer and TFT. Statistical significance
analysis using the Nadeau–Bengio corrected paired t-test confirmed that these improvements are not attributable to random
variability (p-values <1e-4 for all major baselines). These
results indicate that the proposed multi-granularity hierarchical encoding and cross-modal fusion of weather information
effectively reduce false negatives in risky driving behavior
identification. The performance of each component of the
model was verified by means of ablation experiments. In
the granularity analysis experiment, the performance of the
model at different granularities is verified. In the cross-test
set generalization test experiments, the CAFFT model demonstrated a superior capacity to adapt to the driver’s unique
characteristics.
Future research can be conducted in the following areas.
Firstly, risky driving behavior recognition is inherently
scenario-dependent, as identical driving maneuvers may correspond to different risk levels under varying operational
conditions and human response capabilities. Therefore, further investigation of contextual factors and their combined
effects is necessary to better reflect real-world driving scenarios. Secondly, the personal physiological and psychological
factors of drivers also affect driving behavior performance.
A promising avenue for enhancing the recognition performance of driving behavior involves the integration of these
heterogeneous data with existing research. This necessitates
interdisciplinary research, such as the integration of psychology and physiological information disciplines. Thirdly, given
that dangerous driving behavior recognition is a research
topic that contributes to driving safety, active intervention
based on recognition results is a necessary research direction.
Subsequent research endeavors may explore the development
of methods to proactively intervene in drivers, with these
interventions being informed by the findings from driving
behavior recognition. Finally, Given the critical nature of road
traffic safety, with consequences that are often life-or-death,
the timely application of the researched technology is of
the utmost importance. However, this assertion is subject to
various influences and challenges, including the maturity and
reliability of the technology, the system’s resilience to attacks,
and the efficacy of data protection mechanisms. Addressing
these issues can more effectively prevent and handle traffic
accidents, further improving road traffic safety.
