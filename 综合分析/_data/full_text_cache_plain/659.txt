Efficient Intrusion Detection for Edge Network via
Multi-Stage Few-Shot Class-Incremental Learning
Zhen Yin , Wei Wang , Member, IEEE, Yanyu Cheng , Senior Member, IEEE, Yiliang Liu , Member, IEEE,
and Xiaozhen Lu , Member, IEEE

Abstract—The emerging increasingly sophisticated, intelligent,
and stealthy network attacks pose more severe security threats
to the edge network. In particular, the emergence of novel intelligent attacks makes it a challenging issue to obtain sufficient
attack samples, and thus classical deep learning-driven intrusion
detection frameworks (IDSs) become ineffective. To tackle this
issue, we introduce a novel intrusion detection framework leveraging few-shot class-incremental learning (FSCIL) capabilities to
achieve robust detection of emerging threats with few samples. This
approach pre-trains a backbone traffic classification model and
employs few-shot training with prototypical networks. To further
reduce catastrophic forgetting while improving both accuracy and
system robustness, we incrementally fine-tune the classification
model with supervised contrastive learning, and also realize rapid
adaptation to new attacks. Evaluations on the intrusion detection
datasets CIC-IDS2017 and USTC-TF2016 demonstrate that our
framework consistently outperforms baseline models for emerging
attacks detection with few attack samples while preserving effective
recognition of known threats.
Index Terms—Network intrusion detection, few-shot classincremental learning (FSCIL), edge network, deep learning.

further increases misclassification rates and degrades detection
accuracy.

II. RELATED WORK
This section begins with an overview of security attacks in
edge networks, followed by a review of related work on intrusion
detection and few-shot class-incremental learning approaches.
A. Security Attacks in Edge Network
6G networks target Tbps-scale data rates, Kbps/Hz spectral
efficiency, and μs-scale latency to support novel applications [1],
e.g., holographic communication [19] and digital twins [20].
Massive data from these applications overburden the inefficient
and high-latency model of traditional cloud computing, driving
the deployment of numerous servers at the network edge [21].
Diverse, heterogeneous, and untrusted devices accessing open
6G edge networks expand the attack surface, increasing vulnerability to denial of service (DoS) attacks, zero-day attacks, and
similar threats, thereby intensifying cybersecurity risks [22].
IoT networks serve as the most critical application scenario
for edge computing in 6G edge networks, yet the computational and communication constraints of IoT devices hinder
effective security deployment [23]. Given the massive scale of
IoT deployments, data transmission through open wireless edge
networks becomes vulnerable to security attacks, particularly
forgery, man-in-the-middle, and replay attacks that compromise
data reliability [24]. Meanwhile, these vulnerable devices can
be coordinated by attackers to launch DDoS attacks that overwhelm edge servers through excessive service requests, while
simultaneously targeting IoT devices themselves to cause device
failures and service outages [25]. Additionally, malware poses
a significant threat to IoT edge networks, as infected devices

can be hijacked to disrupt remote control functions and compromise service availability [26]. Compromised IoT devices and
edge servers may also be exploited for unauthorized computational tasks such as cryptocurrency mining, imposing additional
electricity costs on legitimate users. The heterogeneous nature
of IoT environments with diverse operating systems makes
comprehensive malware protection particularly challenging to
implement [27].
To achieve the 6G vision of pervasive intelligence and internet
of intelligence, native AI integration at the network architecture
level becomes essential due to the inherent requirements of AI
for distributed processing, real-time responsiveness, and robust
security mechanisms [28]. This evolution naturally drives artificial intelligence (AI) capabilities toward the network edge,
enabling edge AI to serve as an innovative technology that integrates sensing, communication, computation, and intelligence to
enhance network efficiency and security. However, this integration introduces new vulnerabilities, particularly data poisoning
attacks where malicious users inject false data to corrupt AI
models during both training and inference phases [29]. During
training, poisoned data can degrade model accuracy or misdirect
the learning process, while during inference, corrupted inputs
cause trained models to produce erroneous outputs, ultimately
leading to network performance degradation across edge computing, caching, and intelligence services [30].
The inherent vulnerabilities of edge networks, including their
open, distributed architecture and integration of diverse IoT
devices and AI systems, create a complex threat landscape
with multiple attack vectors [31]. Meanwhile, 6G applications
such as remote surgery and critical infrastructure control impose unique and stringent security and privacy requirements
that demand ultrahigh reliability [32]. Therefore, comprehensive defense strategies must be established to overcome the
disconnect between these security vulnerabilities and stringent
safety standards. The IDS serves as a vital component in this
security framework, enabling real-time identification of malicious behavior and rapid threat mitigation to maintain the robust
performance required for edge services [33].
B. Intrusion Detection
Anderson [34] first systematically defined intrusion threats
as unauthorized hostile activities targeting data confidentiality, integrity, or system availability. Denning [4] subsequently
proposed the first intrusion detection model, introducing a
host-based approach that detects security violations by analyzing audit data for irregular system behaviors. Since then,
researchers have conducted extensive research on intrusion detection systems. Based on different data sources [35], Intrusion detection systems are typically classified into three main
types: host-based systems (HIDS) [36], network-based systems
(NIDS) [37], and wireless-based systems (WIDS) [38]. HIDS
monitor system logs, file integrity, and local activities to detect
malicious behavior on individual hosts, but their protection is
limited to the specific machine where they are installed [39].
NIDS are deployed throughout the network infrastructure to
analyze traffic for network device security, analyzing various

4691

protocol layers including network, transport, and application
protocols to identify suspicious network activities [40]. WIDS
monitor wireless communication channels and analyze protocol
behaviors to detect malicious access points and wireless-specific
attacks, though they primarily focus on wireless layer protocols
rather than application layer activities [41].
Despite monitoring different data sources, these IDS types
employ similar detection techniques that are broadly categorized [42] into signature-based [43] and anomaly-based approaches [44]. Signature-based detection maintains a database
of known attack patterns, each representing the digital fingerprint of a specific malicious activity. When monitoring network
traffic or system events, the IDS performs pattern matching by
comparing incoming data against these predefined signatures to
identify potential threats [45]. For instance, Saelee et al. [46]
improved signature-based detection through ensemble methods with boolean weighted evaluation for enhanced accuracy.
Kunmar et al. [47] demonstrated the practical application of
signature-based detection by implementing Snort IDS with the
DARPA dataset to analyze real-time network traffic and detect
abnormal transmission patterns. However, this method faces limitations in complex and dynamic network environments, as it can
only detect previously identified attacks and remains ineffective
against novel threats, zero-day exploits, and polymorphic attacks
that deliberately alter their characteristics to avoid signature
matching [48].
In contrast, anomaly-based detection builds reference patterns of legitimate network activity and triggers alerts when
activities deviate significantly from these patterns, enabling
detection of unknown attacks that lack existing signatures [49].
Early anomaly-based detection approaches primarily relied on
traditional statistical methods [50] or domain expertise [51]
to model normal behavior. Nevertheless, with the increasing
complexity of network environments, these conventional approaches became challenging to scale and required extensive
manual tuning to establish accurate baselines. With advances
in machine learning techniques, researchers began exploring
machine learning approaches which contain both supervised and
unsupervised methods for anomaly-based intrusion detection.
Supervised learning trains classifiers using labeled normal and
anomalous network traces to detect and categorize intrusions,
with widely adopted methods including decision tree (DT) [52],
[53], [54], [55], support vector machine (SVM) [56], [57],
[58], [59], [60], K-nearest neighbors (KNN) [61], [62], [63],
naive bayes (NB) [64], [65], [66], [67] and random forest
(RF) [68], [69], [70]. Uwazie et al. [71] systematically compared RF, KNN, SVM for intrusion detection, evaluating their
performances across various standard datasets. Unsupervised
learning identifies normal behavior patterns and triggers alerts
when network activity deviates beyond predefined thresholds,
with effective approaches including K-means clustering [72],
[73], hierarchical clustering [74], [75] and density-based spatial
clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN) [76], [77].
However, traditional machine learning methods have limitations in automatically extracting meaningful features from complex network data and may struggle with the high-dimensional
nature of modern network traffic [78]. Deep learning (DL)

4692

techniques have emerged as a promising approach, employing
deep neural architectures to extract complex patterns directly
from raw network traffic without manual feature engineering [79]. Various deep learning architectures have shown remarkable effectiveness in intrusion detection applications [80].
Auto-encoders (AE) [81], [82], [83] are particularly effective
for anomaly detection as they create efficient encodings of
normal network operations and highlight anomalous activities
that differ from baseline behavior. Generative adversarial networks (GAN) [84], [85], [86], [87] are adept at generating
synthetic attack samples to augment limited training datasets
and improve the robustness of intrusion detection models. Convolutional neural networks (CNN) [88], [89], [90], [91] are
proficient at extracting local patterns from spatial features of
network traffic, capable of identifying malicious behavioral
characteristics hidden in packet sequences. Recurrent neural
networks (RNN) [92], [93], [94] and their variants, such as long
short-term memory (LSTM) [95], [96], bidirectional long shortterm memory (BiLSTM) [97], [98] and gated recurrent unit
(GRU) [99], [100] are particularly suitable for handling temporal
characteristics of network traffic, capturing the dependencies
and patterns of attack behaviors. Furthermore, researchers have
also explored hybrid approaches combining different architectures such as CNN-RNN [101], [102], CNN-LSTM [103], [104],
CNN-BiLSTM [105], [106] and CNN-GRU [107], [108]. Nevertheless, as network environments become increasingly complex
and novel attacks emerge with limited samples, traditional deep
learning models struggle to adapt quickly [109]. Furthermore,
existing approaches often sacrifice overall system performance
and reliability when attempting to handle class imbalance in
these dynamic environments [110], motivating the exploration
of few-shot incremental learning for intrusion detection.
C. Few-Shot Class-Incremental Learning
Continual learning is a machine learning paradigm designed
to enable models to acquire new knowledge incrementally
while preserving previously learned information. In real-world
applications, the limited availability of samples often renders
traditional continual learning methods ineffective, as scarce
labeled data during incremental learning sessions typically
triggers model overfitting on novel classes and erosion of
previous knowledge. Since Tao et al. [15] initially proposed
few-shot class-incremental learning for computer vision, many
researchers have extended this paradigm to various applications. Existing FSCIL approaches are generally classified into
forward-compatible methods, which train on base classes and
keep the model frozen during incremental sessions, and incremental fine-tuning methods, which adjust the model in incremental sessions [111].
Forward-compatible methods aim to enhance base class separability in the embedding space, reducing interference from
new classes added incrementally. Notably, Peng et al. [112]
employ a CosFace penalty strategy to achieve tight within-class
grouping and distinct between-class margins, enabling effective
integration of new classes within the embedded feature space.
Zhou et al. [113] propose Forward Compatible Training (FACT)

IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON NETWORK SCIENCE AND ENGINEERING, VOL. 13, 2026

for FSCIL, leveraging simulated prototypes to optimize existing
class encodings while preserving capacity for future classes. Oh
et al. [114] introduce the CLOSER framework, which reduces
inter-class distances in FSCIL to enhance the balance between
discriminability and transferability.
Incremental fine-tuning methods adjust the encoder during incremental sessions to enhance flexibility for learning new tasks,
thereby improving adaptability. Mazumder et al. [115] select
non-critical parameters for updates during incremental learning.
Zhao et al. [116] selectively update model components at varying
rates to effectively balance adaptation to new knowledge with
retention of prior knowledge. Kang at el. [117] apply the lottery
ticket hypothesis to identify a subnetwork of critical parameters that remains frozen, while other parameters are fine-tuned
during incremental sessions. Cheraghian et al. [118] develop a
semantically-enhanced knowledge distillation framework [119]
that employs word embeddings as linguistic guidance to improve
the distillation process and mitigate memory degradation in
few-shot incremental scenarios.
D. Few-Shot Class-Incremental Learning for Intrusion
Detection
As shown in Table I, we comprehensively review key literature
on few-shot learning, continual learning, and few-shot class
incremental learning in IDS. Since Xu et al. [12] introduced
meta-learning to IDS in 2020, researchers have increasingly focused on advancing the generalization ability of trained intrusion
detection models to novel attacks.
Continual Learning enables IDS to rapidly adapt to new
attack categories with adequate samples in dynamic threat environments while minimizing catastrophic forgetting of prior
knowledge. Data et al. [120] proposed a tree-like deep feedforward neural network (T-DFNN) model that supports incremental
learning of new categories via dynamic branch expansion. Jin
et al. [123] explored federated incremental learning, allowing
IDS models to update efficiently in data-siloed environments
to address new threats. Cerasuolo et al. [122] compared various continual learning methods in IDS applications, providing
insights for technology selection.
Few-shot learning enables IDS models to learn features from
few samples using meta-learning or metric-based methods, wellsuited for scenarios with limited attack samples. For instance,
[12], [14] utilized model-agnostic meta-learning (MAML), [13],
[121] employed metric-based prototype networks, and [124]
adopted contrastive triplet deep learning networks. However,
few-shot learning in IDS primarily faces catastrophic forgetting,
where adapting to new attack categories impairs prior detection
performance.
Novel attack samples in cybersecurity practice are typically
scarce, necessitating IDS to adapt rapidly to new attack types
while preserving accuracy for known categories. Few-shot class
incremental learning integrates continual learning and few-shot
learning to learn new attack categories from limited samples
while retaining prior knowledge, making it an optimal solution
for online IDS. Currently, few studies have explored the application of FSCIL to intrusion detection, as discussed below.

A. System Model
We consider an intrusion detection scenario in a wireless edge network environment. The IDS is pre-trained on
known attack categories Cp using a large dataset with sufficient labeled samples. After deployment, the system encounters
new attack categories Cv with only limited labeled samples
available.
In this setting, the IDS must rapidly fine-tune to accurately
classify novel attacks using few samples from Cv , while retaining knowledge of prior attack categories Cp . Since new
attacks emerge continuously, we partition samples of Cv into

For implementation, monitoring software deployed across the

B. Data Preprocessing
Before intrusion detection, raw traffic data requires preprocessing, including cleaning, segmentation, and normalization.
We segment traffic into bidirectional flows using the five-tuple
(source IP address, destination IP address, source port, destination port, and protocol type) to enable precise modeling of
network interactions and characterization of malicious traffic
behaviors. Additionally, in traffic segmentation, we employ
timeout and periodic reset strategies referencing [17]. The
timeout strategy terminates inactive links after 64s, while the
periodic reset strategy resets segmentation when flows become
excessively large after 120 s.
Following segmentation, feature extraction methods are employed on the segmented traffic data to derive robust representations for intrusion detection. Raw-byte methods extract features
from packet headers or payloads, offering detailed protocol
representations and detecting subtle anomalies effectively. We
select the first M packets from each session and extract the
initial L bytes of each packet. Following [127], where M = 6-14
and L = 100-200 are used with abundant training data, we
adapt these parameters in our case. Specifically, we follow the
effective practices outlined in [12], [17], and set M = 16 and
L = 256. The extracted bytes are padded with zeros if necessary
to reach L bytes, with MAC and IP addresses anonymized to
mitigate privacy leakage and improve model robustness across
varied networks. Each packet’s 256 bytes are reshaped into
a 4 × 64 sub-matrix, and the 16 packets are concatenated in
temporal order to construct a 64 × 64 matrix representation,
thereby preserving the sequential dependencies within the traffic
flow. These bytes are then normalized (divided by 255) to
form bidirectional flow feature vectors. This approach captures
critical protocol headers (e.g., TCP/UDP fields) and flow patterns from early packets, minimizing computation while supporting early threat detection and enhancing real-time analysis
performance.

pre-training, few-shot learning and few-shot class-incremental
fine-tuning. By efficiently utilizing limited samples, this method
improves adaptability and precision against emerging threats
while mitigating catastrophic forgetting to maintain accuracy
for established attack types.

prior knowledge and prevent overfitting. Given the limited resources of edge networks, we select a CNN as the backbone network. Specifically, we input the preprocessed bidirectional flow
byte features of abundant base class samples into the backbone

architecture. The detailed structure of the CNN architecture we

V. SIMULATION RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
A. Dataset
As far as we know, no standardized benchmark dataset exists
for incremental few-shot learning in intrusion detection [135].
For real intrusion detection scenarios, the selected datasets must
have:
1) normal and diverse malicious traffic classes reflecting realworld network conditions;
2) PCAP format preserving raw network traffic bytes;
3) a few labeled data per traffic type to support incremental
few-shot learning paradigms.
We select two open datasets, i.e., CIC-IDS2017 [136] and
USTC-TFC2016 [10], to evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed method.
CICIDS20171 serves as an established benchmark in the
field of intrusion detection, having been developed by the
Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity and extensively utilized by
researchers worldwide. Capturing five days of network traffic
from July 3 to July 7, 2017, it encompasses both benign flows
and 14 types of contemporary malicious traffic, including DDoS,
Web Attacks, Botnets, etc. The dataset provides raw packet
captures in PCAP format, retaining the original traffic bytes,
alongside CSV files with over 80 extracted flow features generated by CICFlowMeter [137], each labeled with timestamps,
1

https://www.unb.ca/cic/datasets/ids-2017.html

source/destination IPs, ports, protocols, and attack types. Due
to the limited number of samples for certain attack types and
labeling inconsistencies in specific instances [12], we select 10
attack types with sufficient sample sizes, employing raw traffic
data in PCAP format as input for experimental evaluation of the
proposed scheme. This selection enhances the dataset’s suitability for evaluating our system’s performance across diverse attack
scenarios. For incremental few-shot learning experiments, we
structured the CICIDS2017 dataset into session sets, as shown in
Table II. In Session 0, consisting of benign traffic and 6 malicious
traffic types, we allocated 5,000 labeled training samples per
traffic type to contain sufficient data. For incremental learning,
sessions 1 and 2 each includes 5 labeled samples per type to
simulate few-shot learning scenarios. Performance evaluation
after each session uses 500 test samples per type.
The USTC-TFC2016 dataset,2 developed by researchers at
the University of Science and Technology of China (USTC),
represents a comprehensive resource designed for advanced research in encrypted traffic classification and intrusion detection.
Captured in a real-world network environment and stored in
PCAP format with a compressed size of approximately 3.71GB,
it encompasses both benign and malicious traffic flows. The
dataset includes 10 types of benign traffic, generated using the
IXIA BPS [138] network traffic simulation tool, covering applications such as BitTorrent, Gmail, Skype, etc. Additionally, it
contains 10 types of malicious traffic, collected researchers at the
Czech Technical University (CTU) [139] from public websites
in real network environments between 2011 and 2015, including
malware such as Cridex, Geodo, Htbot, etc. This diverse dataset
enables robust evaluation of intrusion detection systems under
realistic conditions. For incremental few-shot learning, all benign traffic in the USTC-TFC2016 dataset is grouped into one
class, combined with 6 randomly selected malicious traffic types
as base classes, each with 5,000 labeled training samples. The
remaining four malicious types serve as incremental classes,
each with 5 training samples. During evaluation, each class,
whether base or incremental, consists of 500 test samples to
ensure a balanced assessment. The few shot incremental session
setup for the USTC-TFC2016 dataset is shown in Table III.

D. Experiment Results and Analysis
1) Ablation Analysis: To validate the effectiveness of each
component, we conduct an ablation study, systematically evaluating the impact of individual modules on overall performance,
with results presented in Table V . Specifically, on the CICIDS2017 dataset, training solely with base classes results in
a performance degradation (PD) as high as 9.72% after two
sessions of incremental learning. At this stage, the model is not
exposed to new category samples, leading to a low recognition
rate for new categories. Introducing pretraining reduces PD
to 5.84% , demonstrating its ability to enable the model to
acquire robust knowledge, effectively learning old categories
while enhancing adaptability to new tasks. Furthermore, incorporating few-shot incremental fine-tuning substantially enhances model effectiveness. By freezing the backbone network
and fine-tuning the fully connected layers, the model effectively
learns the knowledge of new category samples while retaining
the knowledge of old categories. Consequently, on the CICIDS2017 dataset, the PD decreases to 1.22% , with accuracies
across sessions 0 to 2 reaching 99.86% , 99.04% , and 98.64%
, respectively. Similarly, on the USTC-TF2016 dataset, training
with only few-shot base classes results in a PD of 20.71% , but
with all components, PD drops to 7.99% , further validating our
proposed three-stage few-shot incremental learning intrusion
detection system. Notably, the USTC-TF2016 dataset shows reduced detection accuracy compared to CIC-IDS2017, probably
because it provides complete PCAP files for each traffic category
over a period. Although primarily aligned with their labels, these
files contain mixed traffic types which cause labeling errors in
bidirectional flows. In contrast, CIC-IDS2017 utilizes manual
label calibration with IP and time segment data [112], ensuring
higher accuracy. Despite such label noise, our model sustains
optimal classification performance, highlighting its robustness
and adaptability.

VI. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we propose a multi-stage few-shot classincremental learning framework for edge network intrusion
detection. It integrates pre-training, CosFace-based training,
and SupCon-based contrastive learning to enable rapid new
class adaptation and mitigate old class forgetting in fewshot scenarios. Compared to existing methods, our approach
delivers superior incremental detection performance on the
CIC-IDS2017 and USTC-TFC2016 datasets. However, the reliance of our scheme on known samples limits the ability to
detect unseen attacks. In future work, we will explore an efficient
FSCIL model for generalized open-set and zero-shot intrusion
detection of novel attacks, as well as addressing imbalanced
sample distributions in edge networks.
