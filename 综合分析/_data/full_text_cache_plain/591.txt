A Unified Framework for Robust Encrypted
Malicious Traffic Detection in Adverse
Environments via Graph Structure Learning
Jianjin Zhao , Zhiwei Cui , Junsong Fu , Meng Shen , Member, IEEE, and Qi Li

Abstract—The widespread adoption of encryption protocols enables attackers to conceal malicious activities within encrypted
traffic, rendering traditional detection methods ineffective. Graph
Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a promising solution
by modeling network objects and their interactions within graph
representations to capture the collaborative behavioral patterns of
complex threat activities. However, the well-performed premise of
GNNs does not always hold in adverse environments, leading to
unsatisfactory performance, suffering from three critical issues including (1) incomplete information analysis, where heterogeneous
relations among network objects are often overlooked (2) lack of solutions for evasion techniques, as existing methods focus on robust
representation learning but fail to correct adversarial distortions,
and (3) limited robustness evaluation, relying on synthetic feature
perturbations rather than raw traffic manipulations in line with
real-world attacks. To address these issues, we propose RETA, a
unified framework for robust encrypted malicious traffic detection
via graph structure learning. First, RETA unifies heterogeneous
subgraphs capturing semantic metapaths and homogeneous subgraphs modeling behavioral similarities among encrypted sessions
and takes a tailored Heterogeneous Graph Attention Network
(HAN) encoder for neighborhood information aggregation. Then,
it employs a unified graph structure learning framework to correct
noisy relations induced by evasion techniques through channel
attention-based aggregation and Bayesian inference-based estimation. Following an iterative manner, RETA mutually improves
relation modeling and detection robustness. Finally, RETA simulates various realistic adverse conditions by modifying raw traffic
captures, ensuring comprehensive robustness evaluations against
network fluctuations and adversarial attacks. Extensive experiments demonstrate the superior robustness of RETA, significantly
improving detection performance in adverse environments. Even
under extreme adverse conditions (i.e., 30% packet loss rate and 5
perturbation edges), RETA still shows significant advantages, delivering 8.94% and 4.85% accuracy improvements over the baseline
models on average.
Index Terms—Encrypted traffic, malicious traffic detection,
graph structure learning.

I. INTRODUCTION
ITH the rapid proliferation of smart devices and internet
applications, global network traffic has experienced explosive growth. To ensure secure data transmission, encrypted
traffic becomes the mainstream form of network communications. However, the widespread deployment of encryption techniques also expands the attack surface, as attackers increasingly
conceal threat activities (e.g., malicious payload transmission,
covert channel construction, and sensitive data theft) within
encrypted traffic. According to [1], between October 2023 and
September 2024, the Zscaler cloud blocked 32.1 billion attacks
embedded in SSL/TLS-encrypted traffic, accounting to 87.2%
of all threats.
The ubiquitous adoption of encryption protocols presents significant challenges to cyberspace security. Traditional detection
methods based on deep packet inspection and signature/rule
matching are gradually becoming obsolete due to their inability
to inspect encrypted payloads [2]. Consequently, research has
shifted toward advanced machine learning and deep learning
algorithms, leveraging feature engineering and representation
learning techniques to extract discriminative patterns for threat
detection. Recent advancements have primarily focused on exploring correlation analysis to capture the collaborative behavioral patterns of complex threat activities.
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) [3], [4], [5], as an emerging
representation learning technique, have achieved considerable
progresses in encrypted malicious traffic detection by modeling
fine-grained network objects and integrating complicated relations into graph representations [6], [7]. Various graph construction paradigms at packet/session/host levels have been proposed
to model complex behavioral patterns for distinct tasks. Specifically, Shen et al. [8] represent packet-level interactions between
clients and servers as traffic interaction graphs for decentralized
application identification. Zhao et al. [9] construct encrypted
traffic graphs to model service and behavior correlations among
encrypted sessions for malicious flow detection. Lo et al. [10]
formulate endpoint communications between hosts as network
graphs for intrusion detection. Existing studies demonstrate that
topological patterns extracted by GNNs are highly effective for
threat detection.
However, prior studies [11], [12] have revealed that the strong
expressive power of GNNs heavily relies on the premise that
the observed graph topology is reliable and consistent with

Several studies have explored reliable graph construction

inter-dependencies between flows traversing the network as

Fu et al. [17], [18] construct a compact flow interaction graph
to detect abnormal interaction patterns against typical evasion
techniques. Zhao et al. [19] construct a relational multigraph to
realize robust malicious flow detection via discriminative relation incorporation and misleading relation correction. Despite
their effectiveness, three critical deficiencies persist in current
graph-based approaches when applied to adverse environments.
r Incomplete Information Analysis: Existing methods commonly neglect the rich interactions among various network
objects (e.g., fingerprints and certificates) of encrypted
sessions, which reflect the reliability of web services to
a certain extent.
r Lack of Solutions for Evasion Techniques: Evasion techniques obscure both features and interactions, while existing studies focus solely on deriving robust representations
rather than correcting noisy relations, leading to unsatisfactory performance in adverse environments.
r Limited Robustness Evaluation: Few methods systematically simulate real-world adversarial attacks for robustness
assessment. Simply modifying feature vectors rather than
raw traffic captures is not in line with actual attacks, limiting their practical deployment.
To address these limitations, we propose RETA, a unified
graph structure learning framework for robust encrypted malicious traffic detection in adverse environments. Specifically,
RETA first constructs a comprehensive graph comprising heterogeneous and homogeneous subgraphs to model semantic
metapaths and behavioral similarities among encrypted sessions.
Then it takes a tailored Heterogeneous Graph Attention Network
(HAN) encoder that to uniformly aggregate metapath-based and
similarity-based neighborhood information, obtaining contextual session representations. To mitigate the impact of noisy
interactions induced by evasion techniques, it retrieves session
embeddings from intermediate layers to generate observations as
guidance. RETA leverages channel attention-based aggregation
and Bayesian inference-based estimation to reconcile conflicted
observations, deriving an optimized graph with relation correction and completion. By iteratively refining the graph structure

II. RELATED WORK
In this section, we review and summarize the relevant studies in the following three aspects: encrypted malicious traffic
detection, graph neural network, and graph structure learning.
A. Encrypted Malicious Traffic Detection
The core challenges in encrypted malicious traffic detection
arise from fundamental constraints in representing encrypted
traffic. The encryption of payloads obscures semantic information, while the sparsity of available metadata results in
high-dimensional feature distributions. These limitations hinder
traditional detection methods, which not only suffer from combinatorial explosion but also struggle with capturing informative
semantic association. Consequently, research in this area has
shifted from experience-driven Machine Learning (ML)-based
methods to data-driven Deep Learning (DL)-based paradigms.
We first review ML-based and DL-based methods, followed
by an overview of recent GNN-based advancements leveraging
graph modeling to capture collaborative behavioral patterns for
robust detection.
ML-based: Early research primarily relies on expert knowledge, employing manually designed feature sets combined with

machine learning algorithms for detection. The technical solutions at this stage focus on extracting network flow metadata,
constructing a basic feature space by parsing packet feature sequences (e.g., lengths, arrival times), IP/TCP header fields (e.g.,
five-tuple, TTL values), and TLS handshake information (e.g.,
version, cipher suites). To further enhance representation capabilities, researchers introduced analytical metrics such as mean,
variance, and quantiles to generate statistical features like packet
length distribution and time interval variance, thereby achieving
quantitative descriptions of traffic behaviors. Anderson et al. [20]
conduct pioneering work in this field, proposing a supervised
ML-based method that integrates TLS handshake metadata with
DNS and HTTP contextual information, successfully enabling
efficient detection of TLS-encrypted malware traffic. Subsequent works [21], [22], [23] refine flow-level feature design,
while Fu et al. [24], [25] introduce frequency-domain representations to enhance feature stability. More recent advances
explore richer structures: Yu et al. [26] employ attributed graphs
with graph kernels for robust detection under data imbalance;
Fu et al. [27] analyze packet length semantics to detect tunneled
flooding attacks. Despite notable progress, ML-based methods
remain highly dependent on manual feature design and show significant performance degradation under dynamic and evolving
network environments.
DL-based: To mitigate manual feature reliance, DL-based
methods map raw traffic into images, sequences, or graphs,
enabling automated representation learning. Wang et al. [28]
first apply representation learning to raw traffic data, classifying encrypted traffic by mapping it into images. In sequence
modeling, recurrent neural networks (RNNs), Transformers, and
other sequence models are widely used to capture long-range
dependencies in packet feature sequences. Djaidja et al. [29]
introduce an early intrusion detection method combining attention mechanisms and RNNs, achieving efficient early classification of encrypted malicious flows before session termination. In graph structure modeling, Shen et al. [8] proposed
a decentralized application identification method, abstracting
packet length sequences into graph structures for classification,
further expanding the forms of encrypted traffic representation.
These methods effectively reduce reliance on manual feature
design and minimize information loss. Yang et al. [30] develop
TrafCL, a contrastive learning framework leveraging session
augmentation and co-attention mechanisms to robustly detect incomplete or obfuscated malicious encrypted traffic. Fu et al. [31]
design pVoxel, an unsupervised method based on point cloud
analysis that significantly reduces false positives of ML-based
detection models by capturing the topological structure of traffic
feature space. Zhang et al. [32] propose AOC-IDS, an online
adaptive IDS using autoencoders with a contrastive learning
loss and a labor-free continual adaptation framework, achieving
strong adaptability to dynamic network environments. Alotaibi
et al. [33] propose Mateen, an adaptive ensemble learning framework that enhances the adaptability of deep autoencoders to
evolving benign network behaviors, achieving large gains in
practical dynamic environments. Zhao et al. [34] design Trident,
a universal framework for unknown and fine-grained encrypted
traffic detection, using one-class learning and an ensemble of

GNN-based: The recent efforts in encrypted malicious traffic

dynamic changes in encrypted traffic and shared library interference by extracting packet header information. Fu et al. [38]
introduce ST-Graph, which jointly exploits spatial, temporal,

precision and robustness in encrypted malware traffic detection
across real-world deployments. Qiu et al. [39] propose a twostep feature decoupling and dynamic graph diffusion scheme,
effectively resolving the entanglement of traffic feature distributions in network intrusion detection. Ding et al. [40] design
a meta-learning graph attention network-based IoT intrusion

large-scale IoT networks through offline reinforcement learning

traffic detection system based on flow interaction graph structure
analysis, identifying unknown threats without labeled data. Mao
et al. [41] propose a label-aware federated graph contrastive
learning framework, significantly improving network intrusion
detection performance in few-shot scenarios by introducing
linear graph processing for flow embeddings combined with
federated learning. Wu et al. [42] propose TCG-IDS, a selfsupervised IDS framework based on temporal contrastive graph
learning, achieving superior performance and low false positive
rates under multi-class network intrusion detection scenarios.
Zhang et al. [43] design MH-Net, which constructs multi-view
heterogeneous traffic graphs and employs contrastive learning
to enhance encrypted traffic classification under complex correlations across traffic bytes. Cui et al. [44] propose FG-SAT,
which builds flow graphs and uses GraphSAGE and GAT for
encrypted traffic classification under environment shifts, and
employs feature selection based on Jensen-Shannon divergence
to improve robustness. However, it is important to emphasize that existing methods have yet to fully account for data
noise interference caused by network fluctuations and evasion
techniques.

B. Graph Neural Networks
To tackle non-Euclidean data with more expressive power,
GNNs are proposed to learn the complex relations and the
rich inter-dependencies among entities in the graph domain.
Due to their strong performance and interpretability, GNNs
have recently been widely applied in various fields (e.g., social networks [45], recommendation systems [46] and security
applications [47]). Among foundational GNN architectures,
Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) [48] bases on first-order
approximations of spectral graph convolutions to aggregate
neighborhood information and enlarge the receptive field for
each node, achieving promising performance in many tasks.
However, GCN has only been applied in transductive setting
with fixed graphs. To fix this issue, Hamilton et al. [49] propose

GraphSAGE that generalizes GCN to inductive learning. Instead of simple convolutions, aggregator functions are trained to
generate embeddings from nodes’ local neighborhoods. GraphSAGE can effectively trade off performance and runtime by
sampling node neighborhoods. Velickovic et al. [50] introduce
an attention mechanism to GNNs and proposed Graph Attention
Network (GAT), which can implicitly specify different weights
to different neighbors without knowing the graph structure. For
graph-level classification, Li et al. [51] incorporate hierarchical transitive alignment of nodes and transforms variable-sized
graphs into fixed-size aligned density matrices to encode both
topological structures and quantum interference patterns into
graph representations. Generally, most GNNs depend on the
homophily assumption that nodes belonging to the same class
are more likely to be connected. GNNs for heterophilic graphs
are gaining increasing research attention [52] to enhance graph
learning with heterophily. Li et al. [53] introduce Haar-type
graph framelets with permutation equivariance, sparsity, and
efficient computation for heterophilous graphs.
Moreover, heterogeneous graphs, also called Heterogeneous
Information networks (HINs) with multi-typed nodes and edges,
contain more abundant information and are more in line with the
real-world scenario. Wang et al. [54] propose Heterogeneous
Graph Attention Network (HAN) to perform a hierarchical
attention mechanism respectively on node level and semantic
level to aggregate metapath-based neighbors and obtain the
final node embeddings. To consider the intermediate nodes in
metapaths, Fu et al. [55] propose Metapath Aggregated Graph
Neural Network (MAGNN). It first transforms heterogeneous
node embeddings into the same dimension, then applies intrametapath and inner-metapath aggregations with an attention
mechanism to learn the node embeddings.
This work focuses on adapting advanced graph representation
learning techniques to security applications rather than developing new GNN architectures. Specifically, we propose a general
HGNN-powered framework for encrypted malicious traffic detection and take HAN as an example to present its implementation. Notably, our framework supports seamless substitution of
alternative state-of-the-art HGNN models for node classification
tasks, preserving methodological generality while maintaining
detection performance. This approach positions heterogeneous
graph modeling as a flexible solution adaptable to evolving threat
detection requirements.
C. Graph Structure Learning
Graph Structure Learning (GSL) [56] aims to optimize the
incomplete and noisy graph structures for better performance
of GNN on downstream tasks. Chen et al. [57] first propose an
Iterative Deep Graph Learning (IDGL) framework to optimize
graph structures. Specifically, IDGL utilizes cosine similarity as
the edge weights and controls the sparsification of the generated
graph with a preset threshold. Wang et al. [58] propose Graph
structure Estimation neural Network (GEN). GEN firstly hypothesizes that the optimal graph structure conforms to Stochastic Block Model (SBM) [59]. Then GEN generates a set of
multi-view observations as guidance to compute the posterior

probability of the optimal graph based on Bayesian inference.
Finally, GEN optimizes the parameters based on ExpectationMaximization (EM) algorithm, achieving mutual optimizations
of both graph structure and GNN parameters. Zhao et al. [60]
extend GSL to HGNN and propose a Heterogeneous Graph
Structure Learning framework, namely HGSL. Based on IDGL,
HGSL introduces feature propagation graphs and utilizes metapath2vec [61] to generate semantic graphs as supplementation.
In each heterogeneous relation, feature similarity graphs, feature
propagation graphs, and semantic graphs are aggregated by
channel attention operation and fused to generate the optimal
heterogeneous graph. Motivated by these studies, we introduce
graph structure learning to mitigate the perturbations introduced
by potential evasion techniques in adverse environments, mutually improve relation modeling and detection robustness for
encrypted malicious traffic detection.

A. Threat Model
In this work, we consider two types of adversaries: passive
and proactive. The passive adversary prioritize disruption over
evading detection, causing packet loss, retransmission, and outof-order delivery, like general network fluctuations. The loss
and distortion of available information affect detection performance. The proactive adversary actively manipulates malicious
traffic to evade detection by exploiting vulnerabilities inherent
in learning-based models.
To systematically evaluate the impact of these adversaries,
we consider two corresponding attack scenarios: (1) packet loss
due to network fluctuations or Denial of Services (DoS) attacks
and (2) graph structure perturbations via adversarial attacks.
In the former scenario, we randomly drop packets at a certain
rate, simulating the passive adversary. As for the latter scenario,
we add perturbation relations to modify the graph structure,
simulating the proactive adversary.
B. TLS Handshake
TLS protocol is widely employed to encrypt the communications between web applications and servers. Previous studies
primarily leverage the parameters in the unencrypted TLS handshake messages negotiated for subsequent encrypted communications. The details of the informative TLS handshake messages
are as follows:
ClientHello message: Before the encrypted communications,
the client sends a ClientHello message to the server to initiate
a TLS handshake, which contains the maximum version of
SSL/TLS protocol that the client supports, a 32-bytes ClientHello random byte string, the list of cipher suites supported,
the list of compression algorithms supported and some optional
extension.

IV. METHODOLOGY
A. Framework Overview
In this section, we present the high-level design of RETA.
Generally, correlation analysis effectively mitigates the feature
noise introduced by network fluctuations, while various evasion techniques further obfuscate the relations among network
objects, leading to unsatisfactory detection performance. Thus,
in RETA, we construct a graph to comprehensively model the
relations among encrypted sessions, and adaptively correct those
noisy ones via graph structure learning in a unified way. Following an iterative manner, it realizes mutual optimization with
noisy relation correction and detection robustness improvement.
Fig. 1 shows four key components of RETA, i.e., graph construction, observation generation, graph derivation, and iterative
optimization.
Graph Construction: RETA collects and splits raw traffic
captures into encrypted sessions for graph construction. Meanwhile, it parses unencrypted TLS handshake messages and perpacket features to characterize both client-side and server-side
configurations and network behaviors. In the graph, it utilizes
network objects (i.e., sessions, fingerprints and certificates) as
nodes, and establishes connections by modeling semantic and
behavioral relations among sessions. The graph can be further
subdivided into homogeneous and heterogeneous subgraphs by
relations. More details about graph construction are presented
in Section IV-B.
Observation Generation: RETA adopts HAN to aggregate
spatial contextual information within the graph. To infer a better
graph topology with relation correction and completion, we retrieve session embeddings from intermediate layers to construct
multi-order neighborhood similarity graphs as observations.

B. Graph Construction
To model the rich relations among encrypted sessions, we construct a graph incorporating various network objects as nodes,
and both semantic and behavioral relations as edges.
1) Nodes: We extract four types of nodes to denote the
network objects in TLS communications, including encrypted
session node S, client fingerprint node Fc , server fingerprint
node Fs , certificate node C. The corresponding node features
are as follows:

Encrypted Session Node S denotes an encrypted session,
including both uplink and downlink packets that share the same
5-tuple (source IP, source port, destination IP, destination port,
protocol). The features include the parameters negotiated during
TLS handshake phase for subsequent encrypted communication
and the statistical data of uplink and downlink packets after the
session is established.
Client Fingerprint Node Fc reflects the configuration of the
client application initiating TLS sessions. The features include
supported version, accepted cipher suites, list of extensions, elliptic curves, and elliptic curve formats in ClientHello message,
with the MD5 hash value forming a 32-digit hexadecimal JA3
fingerprint for correlations.
Server Fingerprint Node Fs reflects the security configurations of the accessed server. The features include selected
version, cipher suites, and extensions in ServerHello message,
with the MD5 hash value forming a 32-digit hexadecimal JA3s
fingerprint for correlations.
Certificate Node C denotes the certificate issued by a Certificate Authority (CA) to verify the server’s identity. The features
include the issuer (i.e., non-root issuing CA), the subject (i.e.,
the entity of the certificate owner), the validity period, and the
number of certificates in the Certificate message.

Denial of Service (DDoS) and botnet attacks involve multiple
encrypted sessions collaboratively generated by a collection of
devices. Therefore, analyzing each encrypted session in isolation

the complex relations among encrypted sessions from a global
perspective. The graph can be subdivided into heterogeneous
and homogeneous subgraphs by relations. Based on the TLS
protocol, we propose four metapath-based correlations (C1-C4)
with rich semantics to construct heterogeneous subgraphs. Besides, we introduce DTW and Jaccard distances to measure the

k-NN graph of encrypted sessions based on DTW distance of
bidirectional packet length sequences.
C6: Jaccard distance similarity. Jaccard distance measures
the dissimilarity between sample sets by computing the ratio
of the difference between their union and intersection to the
union size. Given the unordered nature of C&C instructions,
we construct a k-NN graph of encrypted sessions by computing the Jaccard distance of downlink packet length sequences.
Threat activities typically adhere to fixed procedures to establish connections, leading to relatively consistent communication patterns. Inspired by [65], we extend the application
scenario in user action identification to threat identification. To
capture these temporal correlations, we employ DTW distance
to measure the behavioral similarity of bidirectional encrypted
communication patterns. Additionally, since C&C instructions
are often executed in non-strict sequence, we introduce Jaccard
distance to measure the similarity of downlink packet length
sequences, capturing spatial correlations between encrypted
sessions.

C. Interpretability Analysis
To improve the interpretablity of RETA in encrypted malicious traffic detection, we incorporate a detailed analysis of its
hierarchical attention mechanism, including both node-level and
semantic-level attention. This analysis aims to visualize how
RETA prioritizes neighbors and relations among encrypted sessions during the detection process, and to provide insights into
which factors contribute most to decision-making, improving
transparency and trustworthiness in its predictions.
Node-level Attention: RETA captures discriminative local
structures by assigning attention to neighbors under each metapath, encompassing both semantic paths and behavioral similarity
relations. Since each metapath induces a distinct subgraph, the
corresponding neighborhoods differ. As illustrated in Fig. 4(a),
attention scores are computed independently per metapath. Notably, metapath-based neighbors exhibit varied contributions,
reflecting diverse structural semantics, whereas similarity-based
neighbors tend to receive more uniform attention due to their less
discriminative nature. The attention mechanism enables RETA
to distinguish informative neighbors from noisy ones, especially
under complex contexts.
Semantic-level Attention: While node-level attention captures
fine-grained local patterns, semantic-level attention aggregates

VI. DISCUSSION
RETA exhibits superior robustness in encrypted malicious
traffic detection under adversarial network conditions, yet it still
exposes some potential issues to be addressed. In this section,
we provides a critical analysis of RETA’s limitations and present
promising directions for future research.
1) Graph Paradigm: RETA constructs a comprehensive graph
that integrates both heterogeneous and homogeneous subgraphs
to capture semantic metapaths and behavioral similarities among
encrypted sessions. The former are derived from SSL/TLS
protocol-related fields, while the latter are measured by packet
length sequences with various distance metrics. However, as a
general detection system, RETA should not be constrained to
specific protocols or predefined relations. Future research could
explore protocol-independent metapaths and additional distance
metrics to model richer relations among encrypted sessions for
subsequent analysis.
2) Unified Optimization: By employing a unified graph structure learning approach, RETA decomposes the optimization of
the graph into independent sub-optimizations for its heterogeneous and homogeneous subgraphs divided by relations. Specifically, heterogeneous subgraphs are estimated using channel
attention, while homogeneous subgraphs are derived independently through Bayesian inference independently. Future work
should focus on developing a more unified graph optimization framework, incorporating attention-based aggregation and
probability-based estimation, to facilitate the joint optimization
of relation subgraphs.
3) Scalability Concerns: Though RETA achieves strong detection robustness, scalability issues persist when extending to
large graphs including the significant computational overhead
associated with k-NN calculations during homogeneous subgraph construction and observation generation, and the substantial memory requirements imposed by GNNs. We experiment
with a flow aggregation technique that explicitly encodes flow
count information into node embeddings, effectively reducing
graph scale with slight performance impact. Future research
should explore alternative flow aggregation strategies and novel
graph construction paradigms to enhance scalability without
compromising detection efficacy.
4) Attack Simulations: To evaluate RETA’s robustness, we
simulate network fluctuations and adversarial attacks by dropping packets and adding perturbation edges. Future work should
expand these simulations to encompass more realistic attack
scenarios, such as DDoS and botnet attacks. Additionally, topological adversarial attacks that mimic legitimate service interactions require further investigation. A broader range of attack
simulations will facilitate a more comprehensive assessment of
RETA’s robustness and contribute to the development of a more
practical and robust encrypted malicious traffic detection system
to tackle adversarial environments. b
VII. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we propose RETA, a unified framework for
robust encrypted malicious traffic detection in adverse environments. Specifically, we construct a comprehensive graph
integrating both heterogeneous and homogeneous subgraphs to

model the rich semantic metapaths and behavioral similarities
among encrypted sessions. Then we utilize a tailored HAN as
graph encoder and employ channel attention-based aggregation
and Bayesian inference-based estimation to jointly correct noisy
relations. Following an iterative manner, the graph data and detection model achieve mutual optimizations with noisy relation
correction and detection robustness improvement. In evaluations, we modify raw traffic captures to simulate network fluctuations and adversarial attacks. Extensive experiments demonstrate the superior robustness of RETA in adverse environments,
underscore the potential of RETA to advance encrypted traffic
analysis in increasingly adversarial network landscapes. Future
work will explore the extension of RETA to more dynamic and
complex threat environments, further enhancing its efficiency,
adaptability, and resilience.
