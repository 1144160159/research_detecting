Adversarial Sample Based on Structured Fusion
Noise for Botnet Detection in
Industrial Control Systems
Jiazhong Lu , Jimin Peng, Jian Shu, Jiali Yin , Member, IEEE, and Xiaolei Liu

Abstract—The industrial control system’s artificial intelligencebased botnet intrusion detection system has a high detection
performance and efficiency in an environment without interference. However, these systems are not immune to evasion
through adversarial samples. In this study, we introduce a
feature extraction technique tailored for ICS botnet detection.
This approach classifies traffic packets based on network traffic
attributes and ICS-specific identification codes, encompassing the
statuses of ICS devices, enhancing detection precision. Meanwhile, this strategy addresses challenges in ICS data collection
and bolsters experimental efficacy. To build a comprehensive
botnet intrusion dataset within an ICS, we concurrently utilized
existing ICS devices to collect both standard ICS and botnet
traffic. Additionally, we present an innovative adversarial sample
generation method for botnet detection models, integrating both
time-domain and frequency-domain noise. Testing under three
real-world ICS attack scenarios revealed our technique can
markedly degrade the classification performance of eight leading
AI-based detection models, emphasizing its potential for evading
AI-based ICS intrusion detectors.
Index Terms—Adversarial sample, botnet, industrial control
system, fusion noise.

II. R ELATED W ORKS
In this section, we review the existing literature on intrusion
detection in ICS, focusing on detection models, feature extraction and data processing methods, and adversarial sample
attacks.
A. Intrusion Detection Models
Researchers have developed a variety of advanced model
architectures to enhance detection accuracy and efficiency.
In the Internet of Things (IoT) domain, which is closely
related to ICS, Kim et al. [1] proposed a hybrid model that
integrates machine learning and deep learning techniques,
offering the flexibility to classify multiple types of botnet
attacks. To capture the temporal dependencies in botnet traffic,
Imrana et al. [2] introduced a Bidirectional Long Short-Term
Memory (BiLSTM) network, which significantly improved
detection performance through bidirectional data processing.
As model complexity increased, researchers began exploring ensemble and hybrid architectures. Ali et al. [3] designed
the ACLR model, which stacks ANN, CNN, LSTM, and
RNN layers to effectively capture complex attack patterns
and features of botnets. Wazzan et al. [4] proposed a Cross
CNN–LSTM framework for IoT botnet detection, mapping the
MITRE ATT&CK model into the detection process and introducing the kill-chain concept to enable early warning.
In addition, several studies have focused on optimizing
specific models. Chen et al. [5] extracted features using CNNs
and incorporated Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) to enhance the traditional SVM algorithm. Mudassir et al. [6]
developed a multi-layer deep learning model for industrial

IoT botnet classification, achieving an accuracy of up to 99%.
Palla and Tayeb [7] proposed a deep learning–based method
to detect Mirai malware targeting IoT nodes, addressing the
overfitting problem by carefully tuning the number of hidden
layers and neurons. Liao et al. [8] leveraged a combination
of CNN and Transformer architectures to jointly learn the
spatial and temporal characteristics of IoT traffic, thereby
improving the model’s ability to recognize minority attack
classes. Laghrissi et al. [9] constructed an intrusion detection
system (IDS) based on LSTM and proposed three related
LSTM-based variants collectively referred to as PCA +MI.

B. Feature Extraction and Data Processing
In industrial control systems (ICS), effective feature processing is a crucial means of improving intrusion detection
performance, as it enables models to focus on the most informative and discriminative attributes. High-quality features and
balanced datasets form the cornerstone of model performance
[10]. In terms of feature extraction, Alqahtani et al. [11]
employed the Fisher score to identify key features and eliminate irrelevant ones, optimizing classification performance
by minimizing intra-class distance and maximizing inter-class
distance. Abbasi et al. [12] utilized a linear low-cost algorithm
to assign feature weights, effectively mitigating the impact of
redundant or irrelevant attributes.
In ICS-specific contexts, data processing plays a particularly critical role. Jiang and Chen [13] analyzed traffic from
industrial protocols such as Modbus, TCP, and S7, employing
a Denoising Autoencoder (DAE) for feature extraction and
combining SMOTE and Tomek-Link mechanisms to address
the problem of class imbalance. Similarly, Mengara et al. [14]
proposed innovative solutions to cope with class imbalance
and high-dimensional data in intrusion detection. Meidan et al.
[15 developed a network-based anomaly detection approach
for IoT systems by extracting statistical features that capture
behavioral snapshots of benign IoT traffic, demonstrating
potential advantages in binary classification.
Other researchers have focused on dataset-specific processing algorithms. Rohmantyo et al. [16] proposed customized
data processing, training, and testing algorithms for the
N-BaIoT dataset. Gupta and Singh [17] designed a multilayer detection framework incorporating the SHAP method
to explain model predictions, thereby improving system transparency and interpretability. Alkahtani and Aldhyani [18]
developed an IoT botnet detection application using a hybrid
deep learning model trained on the N-BaIoT dataset, which
was generated from nine commercial IoT devices by injecting
BASHLITE and Mirai attacks. Liu et al. [19] introduced
a lightweight IoT intrusion detection approach based on
cor-relation coefficients and variance inflation factors (VIF),
performing flow-level feature selection to enhance efficiency.
Zong et al. [20] proposed an automated fuzzing method for
industrial control protocols (ICPs) based on the Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM), which improved fuzzing
effectiveness, identified protocol vulnerabilities, and reduced
potential economic risks and impacts.

C. Adversarial Attacks
Despite continuous advancements in detection technologies,
the robustness of models has become an increasingly prominent issue, as attackers frequently exploit adversarial samples
to evade detection. When attackers possess knowledge of a
model’s internal parameters and architecture, they can launch
gradient-based or optimization-based attacks [21]. Mohammadian et al. [22] employed the Jacobian Sali-ency Map
Algorithm (JSMA) to determine optimal feature combinations,
enabling attacks with minimal feature perturbations. Haroon
and Ali [23] applied classical gradient-based attacks such as
FGSM and JSMA to several cybersecurity datasets to evaluate
the defensive performance of machine learning algorithms.
In cases where model internals are inaccessible, blackbox attacks are commonly adopted. Liu et al. [24] integrated
genetic algorithms into an IoT detection framework, proposing
a black-box testing system (TLTD) for adversarial evaluation.
Wu et al. [25] introduced a deep reinforcement learning–based
black-box attack, capable of generating adversarial traffic to
deceive detection models effectively. Liu et al. [26] developed
a network traffic obfuscation technique to resist traffic classification, proposing an improved gradient-based attack method
adapted to practical strategies and conducting extensive
black-box experiments across different model combinations.
Similarly, Wang et al. [27] proposed a projection-based adversarial attack framework (ProGen), which significantly reduced
the detection performance of multiple machine learning
models.
Usama et al. [28] leveraged Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) to manipulate specific classes within data-sets
and craft adversarial samples. To address the gradient vanishing problem in traditional DCGANs, Alghazzawi et al.
[29] introduced a hybrid optimization algorithm, where the
generated adversarial samples caused the true positive rate
(TPR) of an SVM-based detection model to plummet from
80% to 0%.
In response to such attacks, researchers have proposed
corresponding defense mechanisms. Ding et al. [30] employed
adversarial training as an active defense strategy to enhance
model robustness by retraining on adversarial samples, and
further incorporated a denoising autoencoder as a passive
defense approach.

D. Data Preprocessing
The category labels of the data we propose to capture
contain benign, SYN, UDP, TCP, a total of four different
categorical values. Because the machine learning model cannot
directly take these values, the strings must first be encoded
before being input into the model for training and testing.
Therefore, we encode the category labels of the model with
one-hot encoding and label encoding.
In this study, the attack traffic consists of TCP and UDP
packets originating from a Bot DoS attack targeting the PLCSchneider. Notably, these packets lack industrial control data,
necessitating the processing of missing values in this segment. On the other hand, the normal traffic packets represent
interactions between industrial control devices utilizing the
Modbus-TCP protocol, and they inherently contain industrial
control data.Given that the industrial control data captured by
the device can only be altered manually through a button
press, and considering that the botnet DoS attack doesn’t
modify the device’s operational value, this specific data is
incorporated into the features extracted from the attack traffic
for our analysis.

To ensure authenticity and maintain operational normality,
a minor fluctuation is introduced to the industrial control data.
This ensures that it remains within acceptable operational
limits. Subsequently, these adjusted data features are appended
to our dataset. Concurrently, we implemented data standardization to normalize the dataset’s features. The primary objective
of this process is to level the playing field among features
by removing any inherent disparities in their value ranges.
A dataset with disparate ranges across its features can severely
compromise the performance of a classification model. By
standardizing the data, not only do we simplify the training
process, but we also enhance the computational efficiency of
the model.
Standardization is an essential preprocessing step, given the
disparate scales of the extracted features. We utilized z-score
standardization, which centers each feature at a mean of 0
and scales it to a standard deviation of 1. This conversion to a
consistent scale is critical for optimizing the training of neural
network architectures and other scale-sensitive algorithms, fostering stable convergence and improving model performance.

Once the detection models have been trained and evaluated,
we generate adversarial samples to assess robustness. In our
methodology, colored noise is treated as a time-series perturbation and is fused to alter the temporal characteristics of flows
(i.e., packet timing and flow duration), whereas Gaussian noise
is applied to the spatial characteristics of payloads (i.e., packet
size and load statistics). We apply the same normalization
and perturbation protocol across sample categories to ensure
consistent interference. By separating temporal and spatial
perturbation domains, the proposed scheme more faithfully
reproduces realistic distortions in both transmission behavior
and payload distribution, thereby improving the fidelity and
representativeness of simulated adversarial attacks.
We leverage normalized noise and, based on varying
perturbation coefficients, embed its absolute value into the
normalized test set. From the 10,000 generated noises, a
random subset, comprising 20%, is integrated into the test
set. Given that the noise perturbation introduced is minimal,
the overall mean and standard deviation of the samples remain
largely unchanged. This subtlety allows an attacker, using the
inverse process of normalization, to craft specific adversarial
samples, further highlighting the necessity for models to be
resilient against such potential intrusions.

The reasons for the different performances of various models when faced with our proposed attack are as follows:
Certain models rely heavily on specific features of the input
data, and if these features are perturbed by adversarial noise,
the model’s classification ability can significantly degrade.
This phenomenon is particularly evident in models that have

not filtered out the effects of noise. Some models overfit the
features of the training data and fail to generalize well to
unseen adversarial examples which often exploit the vulnerabilities of these features.
This higher degree of performance degradation in multiclass
classification tasks is mainly associated with the intrinsic
complexity of their decision boundaries. In multiclass settings,
the separation margins among classes in the feature space are
typically narrower than those in binary classification, making
the decision regions more sensitive to small perturbations.
Consequently, even minor adversarial noise may shift samples
across neighboring class boundaries, resulting in misclassification. Moreover, within ICS traffic data, several attack
categories (such as TCP Garbage and UDP Flood) exhibit
strong temporal and payload correlations with benign traffic.
Such feature overlap increases the likelihood of confusion
under noisy conditions. Additionally, neural networks trained
with a softmax-based cross-entropy objective tend to distribute output probabilities among multiple competing classes,

which further amplifies uncertainty when adversarial perturbations are introduced. These factors collectively explain why
multiclass detection models exhibit greater vulnerability to
adversarial samples compared with their binary counterparts.
V. C ONCLUSION
In the domain of industrial control system (ICS) botnet
detection, this paper first introduces a novel feature extraction
technique. Utilizing this method, we establish a botnet within
the ICS environment and assess its effectiveness across four
neural network models and four machine learning models.
A dedicated ICS botnet detection dataset is also introduced.
Empirical results indicate that all eight models proficiently
identify ICS botnets utilizing the proposed feature extraction
approach.
Additionally, this paper advances a black-box attack strategy, blending time-domain and frequency-domain sampling
noise, to produce adversarial samples. We subsequently examine these samples against the eight models, comparing the
effects of varying noise perturbation coefficients on model
detection performance. Our research underscore the efficacy
of the proposed adversarial sample generation strategy in
reducing classification error rates. New adversarial sample
attack methods can expose potential vulnerabilities in ICS
botnet detection systems, prompting researchers and security
experts to develop new defense mechanisms.
As a roadmap for future endeavors, we propose expanding
the ICS intrusion detection dataset by:
Incorporating a broader spectrum of attacks across multiple
protocols within the ICS, including in-depth analyses of the S7
and Modbus protocols. We also intend to refine the dataset by
integrating man-in-the-middle, backdoor, injection, and other
forms of attacks.
Evaluating and ranking feature significance to optimize
feature selection. We will introduce perturbations selectively
to highly significant features and contrast the implications of
these perturbations across varied features on detection efficacy.
Existing models still make errors when distinguishing
between types of traffic that have significant similarities and
closely related attributes. Future work could focus on a more
in-depth study of these types of traffic.
Generating effective adversarial samples requires substantial computational power and time. However, ICS typically
demand high real-time performance. Attackers may lack sufficient time or computational resources to generate adversarial
examples and promptly inject them into the real-time data
streams of control systems. The attack overhead is not the
focus of this study. In future work, we will explore the impact
of the overhead associated with generating adversarial samples
on the effectiveness of attacks.
To mitigate the real-time computational burden associated
with fusion-noise generation, the noise synthesis process can
be performed offline and stored as a reusable noise library. In
this approach, frequency-domain transforms and noise-fusion
procedures are executed a priori for a range of parameter
settings (e.g., spectral exponent α, sampling window length,
and perturbation coefficients ρ). During online evaluation
or injection, precomputed noise segments are retrieved and

applied to normalized flow records with only lightweight operations (e.g., scaling, interpolation, or element-wise addition).
This precomputation strategy substantially reduces runtime
CPU/GPU requirements and enables rapid deployment in
latency-sensitive ICS environments.
Future research should also investigate effective defense
mechanisms to enhance the robustness of ICS botnet detection
models against such hybrid adversarial perturbations. Potential approaches include adversarial training with fusion noise
based samples, feature-space regularization to improve model
generalization, and ensemble-based detection architectures that
integrate temporal and spatial dependencies. Moreover, adaptive noise filtering and model uncertainty estimation could be
explored to dynamically identify adversarial traffic in real-time
ICS environments. These strategies can form a foundation for
building resilient and self-adaptive detection frameworks capable of maintaining high reliability under adversarial conditions.
