Abstract—Machine learning (ML) is promising in accurately
detecting malicious flows in encrypted network traffic; however,
it is challenging to collect a training dataset that contains
a sufficient amount of encrypted malicious data with correct
labels. When ML models are trained with low-quality training
data, they suffer degraded performance. In this paper, we aim
to address a real-world low-quality training dataset problem,
namely, detecting encrypted malicious traffic generated by continuously evolving malware. We develop RAPIER+ that fully

II. BACKGROUND
A. Data Augmentation
Data augmentation techniques can effectively increase the
size of the training data set without explicitly collecting new
data. The most common data-augmentation strategy is oversampling. In particular, the new data can be randomly sampled
or synthesized based on random combinations of existing
data [8], [33], [34], [38]. As a result, the ML model may
overfit the training set due to the limited diversity of the data.
Recently, the Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) [30] has
been widely applied for data augmentation in various areas,
including malicious encrypted traffic detection [9]. Powered by
an adversarial framework, GAN may learn the distribution of
existing data and then create a variety of new training data that
conform to the distribution. However, these methods are not
resilient to label noises. The false labels will confuse the data
distribution of different categories, leading to more label noises
in new samples. Thus, the existing data augmentation methods
cannot handle the low-quality issue of encrypted training data.
B. Robust Machine Learning Models
Robust machine learning models aim to mitigate the impacts
of incorrectly labeled training data. Several models [28], [35],
[55], [62], [87], [91], [102] apply the label transition matrix,
recording the probability of a category being mislabeled
into another category. However, the label transition matrix
is usually unknown in prior. Recent studies [4], [32], [56],
[75], [40], [41], [46], [51], [57], [97], [99] address the issue
by automatically identifying mislabeled samples based on
their properties, e.g., higher loss value in the training stage.
However, these properties may not hold when the number
of training samples decreases, but may significantly constrain
these methods.

A. Overview
We design our system based on a key observation that
the distribution of normal and malicious data is different.
First, the normal traffic is relatively representative. [37], [59],
[79]. Second, since malware are various in real networks, the
malicious traffic is relatively manifold.
Thereby, given a noisy training set, we can infer the true
labels of training samples located in the densest and sparsest
parts of its data distribution and use them as a basis for correcting other labels. Moreover, the distribution of new malicious
data may be closer to normal data over time since attackers
are likely to imitate normal behaviors to avoid detection. Thus,
we can infer the distribution of new malicious data and then
synthesize new training data to improve the performance of
ML-based detectors on new, unseen testing data.
RAPIER+ consists of three main components, including a
feature extraction module, a label noise correction module,

and a data augmentation module, as shown in Figure 1. The
feature extraction module utilizes a Transformer model to
convert encrypted traffic into feature vectors that capture the
comprehensive behavior of the traffic, while mitigating the
adverse effects of label noise in the low-quality training set and
traffic distortion caused by dynamic network conditions. Next,
the label noise correction module applies an auto-regressive
generative model to accurately estimate the distribution of
limited yet high-dimensional training data and relabel the
training samples’ labels based on their distribution characteristics. Then, with the label-corrected training set, the data
augmentation module infers the possible distribution regions
of new malicious data and applies an improved GAN model
to synthesize new malicious training data located in these
target regions. Meanwhile, to prevent the newly generated
malicious data from increasing false alarms, this module
synthesizes new normal training data that can maintain the
decision boundary of the model. Finally, our system utilizes a
DL-based detector built upon Co-teaching [32] and a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) that is trained on the synthesized
and the label-corrected original training data. Here, we apply
Co-teaching to eliminate the impacts of the small amount
of remaining label noise in the training set. In the testing
stage, the encrypted traffic is inspected by the built ML-based
detector directly after being converted into feature vectors.
B. Feature Extraction
The feature extraction module converts raw encrypted traffic
into feature vectors that represent the traffic’s behaviors,
which facilitates label correction and data augmentation afterward. Existing automatic traffic feature extraction methods
[1], [16], [68], [72], [74] utilize supervised models to profile
the behaviors of network traffic with various versions of
encryption protocols. However, they are ill-suited because
the non-negligible label noises in the low-quality training set
will result in inaccurate feature selection. Even worse, the
order of packets in encrypted traffic may change unpredictably
due to dynamic network conditions [5], [13], [66], [92],
such as varying hosts or different transmission paths. As a
result, LSTM [36] and GRU models [12], which are widely
used in existing traffic feature extraction methods, struggle
to accurately profile the behavior of dynamically distorted
encrypted traffic because of their high sensitivity to input data
order. On the other hand, although less sensitive to input order,
CNN models [44] can only capture partial behaviors between

To accurately capture the comprehensive behavior of
encrypted network traffic while mitigating the effects of label
noise and traffic distortion, our feature extraction module
adopts a Transformer [82] model. The rationale behind this
design is two-fold. First, the Transformer model can be trained
in an unsupervised manner, which inherently helps reduce the
negative impact of label noise in the training set. Second,
the Transformer can comprehensively and accurately capture
the global and order-independent correlations between each
pair of packets within encrypted traffic. Leveraging these finegrained correlations as behavioral patterns of encrypted traffic
allows for more effective differentiation between malicious
and benign traffic.
In particular, our Transformer-based feature extraction module comprises an encoder, which extracts feature vectors from
encrypted traffic to represent their behaviors, and a decoder,
which reconstructs the original traffic using the feature vectors.

D. Data Augmentation
The data augmentation module aims to synthesize new
training data that can improve the detection performance on
unseen testing data according to the label-corrected original
training set Dtrain , which is non-trivial due to the following
reasons: (i) We should ensure the diversity of the new training
data. Otherwise, the machine learning model may still overfit
the new training data, limiting its generalizability. (ii) The
distribution of the testing data is likely to be inconsistent with
the training data because malware is always evolving. As a
result, the traditional data oversampling methods [8], [33],
[34] and the popular GAN-based data synthesis approaches
[9], [21], [30] are not applicable, since they only generate
the data that follows the distribution of existing data. To
address these issues, we predict possible distribution regions
of new malicious data and sample from these target regions
to synthesize new diverse training data.
Our data augmentation module augments training data by
synthesizing both normal and malicious data according to
data distributions. We argue that new malicious data may be
located in two specific regions following the data distribution.
First, considering that sophisticated attackers tend to imitate
normal behaviors to evade detection, new malicious data may
become more similar to normal data over time. Therefore, in
distribution, some new malicious data may be located in the
boundary region outside the normal data and yet very close
to it (denoted as MB ). Second, since new attack methods
are emerging, e.g. Zero-day attacks, new malicious data may
be different from both normal and original malicious data,
i.e. located in the region outside all original training data
(denoted as MO ). To avoid data imbalance, we choose the
boundary region inside the original normal data to guide the
generation of normal data (denoted as NB ), which is essential
for maintaining the decision boundary of the detection model.

the new data synthesized by the generator networks will be
regarded as the new training data. Besides, to avoid the model
collapse problem [7] and further increase the diversity of
synthesized data, our data augmentation module trains several
independent GAN models and combines all their synthesized
data as the new training data. Then, we can fully train the
detection model based on the new training data and Dtrain
to perform accurate detection. For comparison, the previous
version of our work, RAPIER [67], does not employ an
adversarial training-based discriminator design. As a result,
it struggled to generate new samples that precisely fall within
the target regions.

Training and Detecting Overhead. Typically, deep learning models involve large training time requirements, yet it is
not a critical concern for our system. Since the low-quality
training set for our system is usually of limited size, the
deep learning models used by our system can be trained
fast. For instance, when the number of training samples is
500, the feature extraction module, the label noise correction

augmentation, can be trained in parallel to boost efficiency.

system with the recent efficiency improvement techniques,
like over-specification [53] and dropout training [77]. After
training, our system supports low-latency and high-throughput
inference, with a latency of 2ms and a throughput of over
42, 000 network flows per second, making it suitable for realtime deployment.

Evading Detection. Sophisticated attackers may try to
bypass our system. One common strategy is generating adversarial examples by adding well-crafted perturbations in the
encrypted network traffic of malware. Our system has builtin designs to handle adversarial examples. In particular, unlike
traditional deep learning models designed for end-to-end tasks,
our system uses deep generative models and GAN models
to improve the quality of training data before training the
subsequent ML-based detector. This creates an extra barrier for
the evasion attacks to construct adversarial examples for these
models. Another evasion strategy is to mimic benign traffic
behavior. We claim that mimicry traffic are still constrained
by specific functionality (e.g. command-and-control traffic),
which induces subtle deviations from benign features. Our
approach can potentially capture these deviations due to the
Transformer’s ability to comprehensively model traffic features. If the original model fails to capture such features, we
can further apply sequence masking or contrastive learning
under perturbations, encouraging the model to learn a richer
set of features that better cover potential mimicry behaviors.

In the extreme case where malicious traffic perfectly aligns
with the benign distribution, detection becomes inherently
difficult. While rare, such attacks can be partially mitigated
by incorporating host-level contextual signals (e.g. IP access
sequences), which provide additional information beyond traffic features alone.

We develop an encrypted malicious traffic system
RAPIER+ that fully utilizes the different distributions of
normal and malicious traffic data in the feature space to
correct sample labels and augment new data for model training. RAPIER+ is a malware traffic detection system that
simultaneously overcomes the challenges of training data
insufficiency and label noises. We implement RAPIER+ and
