Xinyu Xu , Yingxu Lai , Member, IEEE, Xiao Zhang , and Xinrui Dong

Abstract—As security threats to industrial control systems become more prevalent, it is imperative to deploy
effective intrusion-detection systems. However, the existing methods are insufficient for addressing contemporary
attacks. Rule-based methods are heavily dependent on
manual settings, and the covertness of attacks poses challenges to rule effectiveness. Machine and deep learning
methods exhibit low interpretability owing to their complex
designs, and the semantic gap between the model and the
actual operational interpretation limits their applicability.
To mitigate these shortcomings, we propose an abnormal
logical representation learning (ALRL) intrusion detection
method for industrial control systems. ALRL contains a
specific lightweight neural network and employs knowledge distillation to achieve high classification ability. More
importantly, it can generate effective and concise intrusion detection rules directly from the learned knowledge
of the model. The hierarchical model structure and residual connections ensure high interpretability of the rules.
Experiments conducted on two publicly available industrial
control datasets demonstrate that ALRL can classify attacks with an excellent performance. In addition, the logical
rules generated by ALRL can effectively detect all types of
attacks and exhibit good interpretability.
Index Terms—Control logic, industrial control system
(ICS), intrusion detection, model interpretability, rule generation.

B. Limitations of Prior Methods
Currently, most ICSs employ rule-based methods for IDSs,
which are recognized for their reliability and human-readable
representation of attack behaviors [3], [4], [5]. The advantage of rule-based methods is that ICSs can execute actions
consistently and deterministically because rules define the
system’s behavior and decision logic. Unfortunately, existing
rule-based methods fail to satisfy the requirements for detecting critical attacks in ICSs, primarily for two key reasons. First, the creation of detection rules necessitates the involvement of domain experts throughout the process, making
it time-consuming and labor-intensive to replicate the procedure for each distinct ICS. Second, attackers often employ diverse strategies to conceal their activities, making it
challenging for rules solely rooted in expert knowledge to comprehensively encompass the essential elements of these attacks.
Therefore, rule-based methods can have high false-positive
rates and suffer from low detection rates in realistic attack
scenarios, which can lead to significant damage to physical
processes.

performance [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]. However, deploying
these methods directly in an ICS is challenging. Owing to the
extensive volume of network traffic, misclassification generated
by ML and DL methods requires security analysts to spend a
substantial amount of time and effort manually reviewing false
system alarms, resulting in high error costs. This drawback is
rooted in the sophisticated design of the models intended to
capture complex feature interactions, which suffer from low
interpretability and transparency. A semantic gap exists between
the model and the actual operational interpretation of administrators, making it challenging to translate detection results into

C. Proposed Approach and Key Contributions
In this article, we propose an abnormal logical representation learning (ALRL) intrusion detection method for ICSs.
Our method addresses two limitations of existing mainstream
IDSs, including the limited quality of rule-based methods and
the high training cost and low interpretability of DL methods. Specifically, we designed a lightweight neural network
to capture the logical relationships between industrial devices
and employed knowledge distillation to transfer knowledge
from other models, enhancing the performance of attack classification. To ensure high interpretability, we represented the
learned knowledge in the form of abnormal control logic
rules based on logical neural-symbolic representation. For an
intuitive understanding of the rules, we partitioned the model
structure into distinct learning layers, showcasing the relationships among devices within and across physical stages. Through
residual connections, we enable the model to adjust the complexity of rules based on different attack scenarios. The key
contributions of this study are summarized as follows.
1) We propose ALRL, an intrusion detection method capable
of accurately detecting attacks and generating deterministic and interpretable rules. These rules contain clear
and precise attack information, bridging the gap between
effective but opaque models and transparent but lowquality rules. Our method can help alleviate the workload
of industrial maintainers in analyzing attack patterns and
enhance the security of ICSs.
2) We designed a specific neural network to achieve precise identification of various attack behaviors in limited
computational scenarios. A significant contribution of
our work is the ability to directly generate effective and
concise intrusion detection rules tailored for ICSs by
utilizing model knowledge learned from the classification process. The hierarchical structure design based on
physical stages and residual connections empowers the
rules with explanatory capabilities.
3) We conducted extensive experiments on two publicly
available industrial control datasets. The results demonstrate the effectiveness of ALRL in attack classification. Compared to other rule-based methods, ALRL
successfully reduces the average length of extracted
abnormal control logic rules by approximately 44.05%
and 16.42% on the two datasets while maintaining similar attack detection performance. These rules
exhibit outstanding attack discrimination and good
interpretability.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Rule-Based IDSs
Currently, rule-based IDSs are practical and prevalent within
ICSs owing to their deployment flexibility and human-readable
nature. These rules enable administrators to monitor the realtime status of the system conveniently. Adepu et al. [4] introduced a distributed method in form of rules for attack detection, which relies on expert knowledge of process dynamics
and the entanglement of states among physical components.
However, the intricate operational logic of contemporary ICSs
increases the risk of overlooking the crucial details of abnormal behavior when relying solely on expert knowledge.
Das et al. [5] conducted logical data analysis and designed a
specific Boolean function for supervising attack classification.
This approach learns the detection rules from historical sensor
data and demonstrates excellent localization capabilities. Nevertheless, it overlooks the relationship between the actuators and
sensors, thereby affecting its detection performance, especially
in highly concealed attack scenarios. Ahmim et al. [6] proposed
a hierarchical IDS based on decision trees and rule-based concepts, which exhibited high true-negative and detection rates.
Although decision trees are highly interpretable models capable
of directly extracting decision rules, balancing the precision
and complexity of tree structures is challenging. The pursuit of
high accuracy can result in overly complex models that generate
excessive rule conditions.
B. ML- and DL-Based IDSs
Many existing approaches have incorporated ML techniques
with intrusion detection and have exhibited outstanding detection performance. Zhang et al. [7] leveraged methods such as
k-nearest neighbors and random forests to propose a multilayer
IDS tailored for ICSs. This approach considers multiple data
sources and effectively differentiates between normal and attack
samples. Le et al. [8] proposed extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) model to improve the detection of imbalanced distribution attack types in the industrial internet. Fang et al. [9] proposed
an ICS feature selection method based on genetic algorithms,
which involves feature ranking to eliminate redundant features
and utilizes tree-growing clustering to enhance optimization
speed.
DL methods are becoming increasingly prevalent, leading
to improved detection accuracy. This is attributed to the complex model structures designed to capture feature relationships.
Kravchikd et al. [10] introduced a one-dimensional convolutional neural network with autoencoders to detect anomalies
in IDSs. This approach considers both the time and frequency
domains of the data and demonstrates the effectiveness of principal component analysis for data preprocessing. Huong et al. [11]
proposed a lightweight and robust distributed anomaly detection
architecture, with the hybrid design of autoencoder, transformer,
and fourier mixing sublayer.
However, DL methods often struggle to provide concrete
evidence for model behavioral decisions, introducing uncertainty into predictive outcomes. To explain the decisions made

IV. EXPERIMENTS AND EVALUATIONS
This section initially clarifies the datasets and experimental
setup and then presents the comprehensive experimental results,
aiming at answering the following questions.
Q1. Does the knowledge distillation of ALRL contribute
positively to detection performance?
Q2. Can the rule-based interpretable ALRL model achieve
detection accuracy comparable to that of well-performing intrusion detection models?
Q3. How are the decisions made by ALRL explained?
Q4. What are the characteristics of the detection rules learned
by ALRL?
A. Datasets Description
We took two public industrial control datasets to conduct our
experiment.
1) Secure Water Treatment (SWaT) [21]: This is a publicly
available dataset from a water treatment test bed, which is a
downscaled industrial replica of a water treatment plant. It has
six stages and can produce five gallons of filtered water per
minute. We selected 35 practical impact attacks to evaluate the

physical processes that are advantageous for evaluating IDSs.
2) Water Distribution Testbed (WADI) [22]: This is a publicly
available dataset from a water resource distribution simulation
system and is an extension of the SWaT dataset. There are three
stages related to water distribution. The attacks encompassed
13 categories of cyberattacks, and real-time data were collected
from 69 sensors and 54 actuators.
In both datasets, we used four attack scenarios to test ALRL’s
performance.
1) Single-stage single-point (SSSP): An SSSP attack focuses
on exactly one point in the ICS.
2) Single-stage multipoint (SSMP): An SSMP attack focuses on two or more attack points in an ICS but only
on one industrial process stage.
3) Multistage single-point (MSSP): An MSSP attack is similar to an SSMP attack, except that the SSMP attack is
performed in multiple stages.
4) Multistage multipoint (MSMP): An MSMP attack is an
SSMP attack that is performed in two or more stages of
the ICS.
B. Experimental Methodology and Settings
1) Data Preprocessing: The SWaT dataset contains
4 499 919 raw data samples, while the WADI dataset contains
172 801 raw data samples. Abnormal samples are labeled
with attack types based on publicly available attack manuals.
Considering the nature of the target data sequences, we retained
all attack samples and selected a set of samples occurring 1000 s
before each attack type as normal samples to address imbalances
in sample quantities. After downsampling, the SWaT dataset
contains 47 044 samples, including 26 877 normal samples.
The WADI dataset contains 20 947 samples, including 11 087
normal samples.
2) Baselines for Attack Classification: To demonstrate the
validity of our method, we compared it with seven existing wellperformed methods. Rule-based methods: The classification and
regression tree (CART) [23] is known for its interpretability,
making it suitable for understanding decision-making processes.
However, the generated decision trees, particularly those with
numerous continuous features, can become overly complex.
Therefore, we employed cost-complexity pruning with pruning
values of 0.003, 0.001, and 0.0, assessing the impact of different pruning thresholds on interpretability and performance.
Higher values indicated simpler trees. A rule-based representation learner (RRL) [17] can automatically learn interpretable
nonfuzzy rules for data representation and classification, which
represents a state-of-the-art rule-based classification model.
ML methods: XGBoost [24] is a powerful gradient boosting
tree algorithm that integrates multiple weak classifiers into a
strong classifier. A piecewise linear neural network (PLNN) [25]
represents an MLP in a mathematically equivalent linear form
that adopts piecewise linear activation functions, such as rectified linear unit (ReLU), which can offer explanatory insights into

input samples. These complex ML methods generally achieve
better detection performance than interpretable rule-based methods but compromise interpretability to a great extent.
DL methods: The self-attention and intersample attention transformer (SAINT) [26] proposed a novel enhanced
embedding method based on a deep transformer network that
demonstrated strong performance in processing data samples
with a mixture of continuous, categorical, and ordinal values.
1-D convolutional neural networks (1D-CNN) [10] are effective
cyberattack detection mechanisms in ICS. They achieve optimal
performance while maintaining generality, simplicity, and a
small footprint. Logic understanding IDS (LU-IDS) [13] is a
recently proposed intrusion detection method. A specific DL
module for the ICS was designed based on a residual network.
Owing to its capability of capturing rich feature representations
from data during training, it can serve as a powerful performance
benchmark. Therefore, we utilized this particular residual network as a teacher model to transfer its learned representation to
our model, which can potentially enhance the learning process
and improve performance.
3) Parameter Settings: Within the logical-learning module,
we set L = 2. For the single-stage logic learning layer, we
configure the number of logic units based on the number of
sensors and actuators at each distinct physical stage of the
dataset. In the SWaT dataset, in which the number of devices
designed for each physical stage was approximately equal, we
set the number of disjunctive and conjunctive logic units to 24.
In the WADI dataset, as the secondary grid stage encompasses
multiple water tanks, requiring a larger set of physical devices
for monitoring than the other two stages, we set the number
of logic units to {24, 72, 24} to accommodate the differences.
For the multistage logic learning layers, considering the model
complexity and computational efficiency, we uniformly set the
number of conjunction and disjunction units to 64. We utilized
Adam for the training process with a mini-batch size of 32, which
was selected for its efficient handling of sparse gradients [27].
We adopted a learning rate decay strategy in which the initial
learning rate started at 0.01 and linearly decreased to 0.001. It
provides a smoother transition in the optimization landscape,
with fast convergence in the early stage and an optimal solution
in the late stage. The training was conducted over 50 epochs.
We used the scikit-learn default parameters for CART and
XGBoost. Ten estimators were used for the ensemble models.
For RLL and PLNN, we set the optimizer selection, learning
rate, decay rate, batch size, model depth, and width to be the
same as those for ALRL. For the 1D-CNN, we constructed a
model comprising five convolutional and three linear layers.
These convolutional layers conduct one-dimensional convolutions with kernel sizes decreasing from five to one and a stride
of one. To perform attack classification, we replaced the final
fully connected layer based on the number of attack categories.
For SAINT, we set the number of stages to 1, the number of
attention heads in each attention layer to 4, the embedding size
to 32, and the dropout rate to 0.8. In the data preprocessing of the
1D-CNN and SAINT, the features were normalized to the (0,1)
scale. For the LU-IDS, we encoded the features using IEEE754
and input them into a residual network composed of four residual

G. Discussion on the Challenges of Security Monitoring
Although the concise and effective rules generated by ALRL
strengthen the security of ICSs, some challenges remain in their
deployment in practical security monitoring.
1) Online learning: ALRL’s training methodology involves
the offline training of static data. However, real-world
systems operate in dynamic environments where new
threats continuously emerge.
2) Rule updates: In response to emerging threats, the timely
incorporation of new rules or modifications to existing
rules is necessary. However, the updating process may
disrupt the performance of the existing rules.
Therefore, in future work, we will consider adopting incremental learning during the training process to endow the
model with the ability of dynamic learning, which will augment
the knowledge of the existing model using new data samples.
Moreover, integrating security analyst feedback into the rule
update loop can ensure the real-time efficacy of the rule set.
V. CONCLUSION
This article presents a novel intrusion detection method for
ICSs called ALRL, which can accurately identify multiple
types of attacks and generate the corresponding effective and
human-readable detection rules. This is achieved by designing
specific neural networks and knowledge distillation for attack
classification, coupled with the extraction of anomalous control logic rules using logical neural-symbolic representation.
The hierarchical logical learning model structure and residual connections resulted in the formation of simpler rules.
Extensive experiments on two public datasets demonstrate that,
compared to rule-learning methods, ALRL can automatically
learn complex relationships between industrial control data
features, exhibit outstanding classification performance, and be

capable of generating effective and concise rules. Compared to
DL methods, ALRL exhibits high interpretability owing to its
structural transparency and low complexity, while maintaining
comparable performance. In summary, ALRL emerges as a
promising solution for enhancing the security of ICSs.
