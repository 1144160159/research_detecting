HoneyLLMd: A Large Language Model-Powered
Adaptive Honeypot System
Wenjun Fan , Member, IEEE, Fei Du , Zhihui Wang , Qiyu Cao , Lekun Liu , Kejing Chang ,
and Sang-Yoon Chang

Abstract—A honeypot is a cybersecurity mechanism designed
to lure attackers by emulating vulnerabilities and serving as a
decoy, intentionally crafted to detect and analyze malicious activities. However, achieving a balance between the ability to engage
attackers extensively and the need to minimize exposure of system
resources remains an enduring challenge. High-interaction honeypots (HIHs) provide in-depth system-level observation through full
operating systems, yet they inherently increase the risk of being
compromised due to the extensive exposure of functionalities. In
contrast, low- and medium-interaction honeypots (LIHs/MIHs)
emulate only minimal or disguised services, which cannot spawn
reverse shells or execute real command responses, but significantly
reduce the risk of system intrusion. To overcome this limitation, we
present a large language model (LLM)-powered adaptive honeypot
system capable of supporting both bind and reverse shell interactions with authentic shell-like responses generated by the LLM
rather than a real OS. This design enables convincing, continuous
“request–response” interactions in which attackers unintentionally
reveal behavioral and intent data. Furthermore, a hierarchical
probabilistic automaton (HPA) is employed to model attack transitions and dynamically adapt honeypot strategies. Experimental
validation on real-world attacks demonstrates that the proposed
system achieves superior engagement and data capture while maintaining low security risk.
Index Terms—Deceptive defense, honeypot, large language
model, hierarchical probabilistic automaton, authentic response.

A. Honeypot Interaction Level
The interaction capacity of a honeypot is generally defined
as the extent to which the information system resources are
exposed to network adversary. Therefore, honeypot systems can
be classified into several types as follows.
1) Low-Interaction Honeypots (LIH): An LIH usually exposes less access to the attackers, emulating basic protocols
and network services of an OS. Virtual environments are often
employed in the LIH to simulate the functionality. For example,
Honeyd [10] is a seminal virtual honeypot framework that
simulates entire network topologies at the network layer. By
emulating the TCP/IP stack of various OSs through configurable
‘personalities’, it can effectively deceive remote fingerprinting
tools like Nmap. The main objective of LIHs is to detect and
collect malicious activities, such as unauthorized probes or login
actions [11]. The development of LIH has expanded with the
scope of cyber attacks, such as HoneydV6 [12] that simulates
entire IPv6 networks on a single host, KFSensor [13] for aid
LIH in fingerprinting attack, and HoneyPy [14] for minimizing
Slowloris attack. Beyond these examples, LIHs are primarily
valued for their resource efficiency and ability to be deployed
at scale, serving as an early-warning sensor network. However,
their inherent limitation lies in the shallow level of emulation,
which can be detected by sophisticated attackers.
2) Medium-Interaction Honeypots (MIH): MIHs offer a
higher degree of service emulation than their low-interaction
counterparts. They are capable of limited interaction with attackers, going beyond simple traffic identification, all while operating without a real underlying OS. Representative examples
such as Amun [15] and Dionaea [16], which emulate vulnerable
services by pre-defining the corresponding service reply logic
to capture attack payloads. Cowrie [17] under a shell mode implements a UNIX-like shell environment, providing a deceptive
shell to log adversary commands and keystrokes. While MIHs
offer deeper interaction than LIHs-enabling behavioral analysis
of attack sequences, they remain constrained in capturing full
intrusion chains, such as post-exploitation activities including
rootkit installation.
3) High-Interaction Honeypots (HIH): HIHs are characterized by their provision of a realistic, compromisable environment for adversaries. This is most commonly achieved by deploying real OS and applications, allowing attackers to perform
full intrusion chains. Alternatively, HIHs can be implemented
through high-fidelity emulation (e.g., using instrumented fullsystem virtual machines (VMs)). The honeypot fidelity comes
with increased risk, as attackers gain actual system access [2]. In
other words, if the honeypot would like to learn more from the
adversary, it needs to provide more resources for the malicious
interaction, while that also brings more threats and security
concerns. For example, Argos [18] used QUME to emulate
the OS, and exposed complete system resources to attackers;
however, it faced challenges in security maintenance, dynamic
reconfiguration, and scalable deployment. Cowrie in proxy

complicate configurations, and high maintenance cost [26].

B. Honeypot Shell
Network adversaries commonly establish remote control over
the compromised hosts by spawning interactive command shells,
which serve as C&C channels. These shells are broadly classified
into two types: bind and reverse shells.
In a bind shell (Fig. 1(a)), the compromised host listens on a
specified network port, awaiting an incoming connection from
the attacker. This method succeeds only if the target environment

permits inbound connections to the chosen port. In contrast, a reverse shell (Fig. 1(b)) involves the compromised host initiating
an outbound connection to a listener controlled by the adversary.
Compared to bind shell, this approach offers several operational
advantages for adversaries [29], [30]:
1) Outbound traffic is typically subject to fewer firewall
restrictions than inbound connections;
2) Reverse shells circumvent the need to determine the target’s public IP address, thus avoiding NAT traversal complications;
3) Outbound callbacks can maintain stealth over extended
periods, evading detection more effectively than persistent
listening services.
These advantages have established reverse shells as the dominant C&C pattern in modern exploitation frameworks.
However, conventional honeypots-particularly LIHs and
MIHs-are inherently limited in their ability to handle reverse shells. For instance, the LIH Honeyd [10] and the MIH
Cowrie [17] support only bind shell establishment. Although
HIHs that host full OS can, in theory, support reverse shells,
their operational complexity and security risks limit widespread
deployment. This is particularly concerning given the prevalence
of reverse shells in mainstream penetration frameworks. For instance, Metasploit [31] includes a majority of payloads dedicated
to establishing reverse shells post-exploitation, with numerous modules supporting raw TCP and HTTPS-based callbacks.
Moreover, attackers increasingly employ covert channels, such
as DNS tunneling [32], WebSocket-based callbacks [33], and
ICMP tunneling [34] to evade conventional network controls.
The widespread adoption of reverse shells for C&C, coupled
with their limited support in conventional honeypots, motivates
the development of honeypot systems capable of safely spawning and monitoring reverse-shell connections to enhance threat
intelligence collection.
C. Honeypot Intelligence
1) Statistic Approach: To counter increasingly sophisticated
anti-honeypot techniques, traditional honeypots have become
insufficient to effectively deceive attackers. In this context,
statistic-based honeypots employ mathematical abstraction to
capture the stochastic nature of attacker behaviors. These models
aim to quantify uncertainties, optimize defensive responses, and
support decision-making under incomplete information.
Game theory is particularly relevant to the modeling and
analysis of strategic operations of information [35], including
deception tactics that are highly applicable to honeypots. In [36],
the authors proposed a game-theoretic honeypot framework
integrated with an HPA. This framework models the interactions
between HIHs and attackers, as well as the command transition
probability. The Monte Carlo simulations were tested to find
the Nash equilibria, with the goal of maximizing the honeypot’s
utility. However, the efficacy of such model heavily relies on
the assumption of rational adversaries and predefined behavioral patterns, limiting their effectiveness against non-rational,
automated, or novel threats. An innovative pseudo honeypot
game (PHG) [37] has been proposed, which combines Bayesian

networks to infer the attacker’s intentions, enhances deceptiveness and prevents DDos attacks. Moreover, to address the
limitation of traditional game theory on dynamic behavior modeling, stochastic games (SG) was adopted. In [38], the honeypot
strategy was optimized in terms of the uncertainty of network and
opponents based on one-sided partially observable stochastic
game (OS-POSG). SG was also employed for honeypot defense
in Uncrewed Aerial Vehicle (UAV) systems [39]. In addition,
Markov Decision Process (MDP) and its variants offer another
principled methodology. In paper [40], MDP was utilized to
model the interactions between botmasters and honeypots, and
Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) was
extended to help honeypot predict uncertain honeypot states
as determined by botmasters. Meanwhile, Tapaswi et al. [41]
deployed a Markov chain-based honeypot model on LIHs and
HIHs to identify systems most likely to be attacked, dynamically
changing honeypot IP addresses to avoid detection and collect
more attack data.
Nevertheless, statistical models often struggle with the complexity and adaptability of real-world threats. A significant
limitation remains their inability to dynamically enhance the
fidelity of the honeypot’s interactions.
2) Machine Learning-Based Approach: In response to the
shortcomings of statistical methods, honeypots have begun incorporating learning-based methods.
ML methods have been widely used to assist honeypot systems in malious traffic detection and post-attack data analysis [42]. In [43], Natural Language Processing (NLP) for
detecting social engineering (SE) attacks was developed with

HMM and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) with honeypot captured data. Fraunholz et al. [26] took advantage of
ML to identify unique types of attack sessions and characteristics based on issued commands and provided credentials.
HuntGPT [47] introduced Explainable AI (XAI) to ML techniques for reliable and actionable intrusion detection. Moreover, ML for anti-detection is realized in [48], which developed an learning-evaluated honeypot using Reinforcement
Learning (RL), increasing the number of instructions in the
“secure shell” sequence presented by the honeypot, thereby

intelligent honeypot capable of learning from captured attacks to

III. DESIGN OF HONEYLLMD
This section outlines the threat model underlying our approach, as well as the design of the adaptive HoneyLLMd.
It includes an overview of the system architecture, a detailed
description of each functional module, the honeypot modeling
approach, and the evaluation metrics used.
A. Threat Model
The proposed HoneyLLMd is designed to enhance traditional
low-/medium-interaction honeypots by integrating LLM to generate imitative shell responses. Our work focuses on engaging
and mesmerizing attack activities through shell interaction based
on intelligence strategies. Thus, in addition to the basic data
capture capabilities for port scanning, probing, traffic flooding, etc., our HoneyLLMd is capable of maneuvering to resist
handcrafted/script-generated shell commands. It is assumed that
the adversary is able to exploit the vulnerabilities set up by our
honeypot and can create bind/reverse shell as if our honeypot
can recognize the exploit payload and spawn the corresponding
shell session with the attacker.
Also, we stress on the attackers seeking targets of opportunities (like internet-based opportunists), however, they are
not limited to the attacker aiming for targets of choice, e.g.,
target intruders in an enterprise network. Essentially, it depends
on how the HoneyLLMd would be used and where it will
be deployed. We assume that it would be more likely to be
deployed in the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ), while there is no
constraint to deploy it inside networks. Furthermore, the adversary may employ honeypot detection techniques based on sidechannel signals such as latency, IP addresses, and traffic patterns,
whereas our deception defense approach primarily focuses on
generating authentic shell responses rather than manipulating
other side-channel signals. Thus, enhancing deception quality
by tweaking side-channel signals is outside the scope of this
threat model.

B. System Overview
Fig. 2 illustrates the design of the proposed LLM-powered

components-Request Handler (RH), Shell Engine (SE), Logger,

capabilities.
The RH, as its name suggests, is responsible for handling
malicious connection requests, particularly managing concurrent requests, processing payload operations, and invoking the
appropriate shell emulation processes. Within RH, the Port Listener monitors traditional bind shell ports as well as other ports
that may receive malicious payloads, acting as a preliminary
filter to facilitate reverse shell functionality. Additionally, RH
forwards and displays the commands and responses exchanged
between the attacker and the shell.
The SE primarily provides deceptive responses to attackers
using LLM engine, including fake shells for traditional bind
shell requests and reverse shells for reverse connection requests
identified from malicious payloads.
The Logger records and stores content generated from all
modules of HoneyLLMd, covering essential details such as
incoming commands, decoding procedure, block decisions, and
LLM responses for system analysis and optimization.
The CE consists of two modules. The first part is the HPA,
which is constructed by preprocessing the raw incoming commands into states, and the transition matrices which deliver
the frequency and probability transitions between states. They
are continuously updated when detecting new transitions. The
another part is Block Judgement, which determines whether
incoming content should be blocked according to the transition
matrix.
The LE is an external module that the virtual honeypot relies
on. Multiple LLMs can be integrated into the LE to meet the
performance requirements necessary for effective deception and
defense.

capability and the risk of compromise in honeypots. Thus,
modeling the trade-off between the risk of compromise (security
level) and data capture capability (ability to attract malicious
activities) in a honeypot is an interesting and arduous challenge.
The honeypot design objective is to maximize attack data capture
capability while minimizing the security risk.

being fully compromised, whereby p(x) must be constrained to
an acceptable risk threshold. Therefore, the honeypot objective

Block mechanism and corresponding LLM responses: After
modeling the attacker’s path and training the HPA on historical
attack chains, we compute the attacker’s payoff (Rap ). This value
represents the current probabilistic intent of the attacker to reach
the target malicious state (e.g., payload execution), based on
the observed HPA state transitions. The honeypot implements a
security policy governed by a Block Threshold B ∈ [0, 1]. The
decision is made dynamically: when the calculated attacker payoff (Rap ) exceeds this threshold (Rap > B), the system adaptively
enforces a block, forcing the attacker to diverge from their most
optimal path. Given the complementary relationship between
the honeypot and the attacker’s payoff (Rap + Rhp = 1), we aim
to ensure that a block threshold is found such that the payoff of
the honeypot is at least greater than 0.5.

denied” or “timeout”, so that the attacker perceives the
command as unsuccessful. In contrast, if the input is allowed,
HoneyLLMd generates valid simulation responses, making the
attacker believe that the command has been successfully executed. By adaptively adjusting this blocking policy, the HoneyLLMd is able to enforce fine-grained control while maintaining a coherent and adaptive strategy for engaging the attacker,
ultimately enabling richer observation and capture of attacker
activities.

D. Deception Metrics Labeling Procedure
To implement the deception evaluation metrics, we developed
an automated labeling pipeline using Python and LLM. The
procedure uses the real system output (Rora ) as the oracle to
categorize the LLM response (Rllm ).
The first layer of the pipeline determines the “successful
attack” status by scanning for execution failure indicators. We
utilize RE to identify a predefined and continuously updated set
of failure keywords, such as error, EOF, not found, and
no/not such file. If Rllm does not contain these failure
indicators, it is initially categorized as either SALC or SANLC.
Otherwise, it is categorized as either FALC or FANLC. Also,
the LLM would be used to divide the response into logical
compliance and logical non-compliance. If the response adheres
to one of the following four conditions, it would be classified as
logical non-compliance:
r Syntactic Incorrectness: If Rllm is not in the shell format
of the currently specified system, but rather a response in
other format (e.g., nature language).
r State Inconsistency: If Rllm reflects a system state that
contradicts prior interactions. For instance, if an attacker
successfully deletes a directory, but Rllm still lists it in
subsequent commands.
r Environment Nonalignment: If Rllm references tools or
paths inconsistent with the target OS (e.g., a yum error on
an Ubuntu target).
r Permission Non-compliance: If Rllm indicates an opposite
permission result with Rora .

G. Discussion
The presented discussion summarizes several limitations of
our current work and outlines directions for future research.
First, we currently model honeypot and attacker payoffs as
perfectly complementary (their sum equals one), which may
not reflect real interactions. In practice, their outcomes are not
necessarily constant-sum, as both sides can incur costs or gain
benefits independently, influenced by factors such as operational
overhead, detection risk, and the value of collected information.

Second, our evaluation of LLM-driven deception is limited to
four models for economic reasons. This constraint may bias our
conclusions about temptation and engagement. To strengthen
external validity, future work should broaden the model set,
and report cost-performance tradeoffs (latency, token cost, and
deception metrics). Such an expanded evaluation would also
enable more robust statements about which model capabilities
(e.g., contextual understanding, code synthesis, hallucination
tendencies) most strongly drive honeypot effectiveness.
Finally, although the LLM-based shell decoder consistently
achieves richer, more semantically coherent interpretations of
attacker input than a rule-based RE approach, its runtime cost is
substantially higher. We deliberately retained the LLM decoder
in this study because its expressive power better supports mapping free-form attacker commands into the hierarchical states
required by our HPA framework. However, the latency and
compute overheads are nontrivial and may impede real-time
deployment. A pragmatic path forward is to explore hybrid
pipelines (e.g., a fast RE front end that delegates ambiguous
or complex cases to an LLM), or lightweight LLMs tuned
specifically for shell parsing to reduce latency while preserving
semantic accuracy.
VI. CONCLUSION
This paper presented a novel approach to enhancing cybersecurity measures through the implementation of an LLMpowered adaptive honeypot. By integrating advanced LLM technology, the proposed system successfully emulates authentic
shell interactions without exposing the vulnerabilities of a real
OS, thereby reducing the inherent security risks associated with
HIHs. Moreover, the innovative use of HPA modeling attack
process for optimizing the deception defense strategy not only
deceives attackers into revealing valuable information through
detailed “request-response” interactions but also maintains a low
risk profile. The experimental results from our proof-of-concept
system have demonstrated the effectiveness of this approach,
showcasing its ability to capture a wide range of real-world
attacks while minimizing security risks. This balance represents
a significant advancement in the field of cybersecurity, offering a
solution to the ongoing challenge of engaging attackers without
compromising the security of information systems.
Future research could focus on refining the LLM’s understanding and response to more sophisticated cyber-attack techniques, potentially through the integration of specialized cybersecurity datasets during the training phase. Furthermore,
expanding the range of emulated environments beyond shell
interactions to include web applications, databases, and other
critical systems could broaden the scope of attacks that can
be detected and analyzed. In addition, to balance the trade-off
between shell decoding accuracy and efficiency, exploring the
application of a distillation technique to open-source LLMs
would be valuable.
